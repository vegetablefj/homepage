    Appendix B. Semiorthogonal decompositions and mutations

    This appendix fixes the categorical conventions used to define \(\Ku(X)\text{.}\) It explains why the projection to this component involves mutations, why its Serre functor differs from the Serre functor of \(D^b(X)\text{,}\) and how the cubic equation produces the shift by two. The basic mutation formalism is reviewed in [Kuz10, Section 2].

    Section B.1 Complexes, shifts, and Serre duality

    For a smooth projective variety \(Y\text{,}\) the category \(D^b(Y)=D^b(\operatorname{Coh}Y)\) has bounded complexes of coherent sheaves as objects, with quasi-isomorphisms inverted. A sheaf is viewed as a complex concentrated in degree zero. Our convention is \(\operatorname{Ext}^i(E,F)=\operatorname{Hom}(E,F[i])\text{.}\) Thus the complex \(F[1]\) has terms \(F[1]^j=F^{j+1}\text{.}\)
    A map \(E\to F\) determines a distinguished triangle
    \begin{equation*} E\longrightarrow F\longrightarrow\operatorname{Cone}(E\to F)\longrightarrow E[1]. \end{equation*}
    Applying \(\operatorname{Hom}(G,-)\) or \(\operatorname{Hom}(-,G)\) gives a long exact sequence. For a short exact sequence of sheaves, its associated triangle expresses the quotient as the cone of the injection. A cone need not itself be a sheaf.
    1
    The derived Hom complex \(\operatorname{RHom}(E,F)\) has cohomology \(\operatorname{Ext}^i(E,F)\text{.}\) These spaces are finite-dimensional, and the Euler pairing is
    \begin{equation*} \chi(E,F)=\sum_i(-1)^i\dim\operatorname{Ext}^i(E,F). \end{equation*}
    It is additive in triangles and therefore descends to the Grothendieck group. On a K3 category the Mukai pairing is \(-\chi\text{.}\)
    A Serre functor \(S_{\mathcal D}\) is an autoequivalence with functorial isomorphisms
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Hom}(A,B)^*\simeq\operatorname{Hom}(B,S_{\mathcal D}A). \end{equation*}
    For a smooth projective \(n\)-fold, Serre duality gives \(S_{D^b(Y)}=(-\otimes K_Y)[n]\text{.}\) Hence \(S_{D^b(S)}=[2]\) for a K3 surface, while \(S_{D^b(X)}=(-\otimes\cO_X(-3))[4]\) for a cubic fourfold.
    These formulas concern the indicated categories. The canonical class of the fourfold determines the Serre functor on its whole derived category. The Serre functor on an admissible subcategory also involves projection and can be different.

    Section B.2 Exceptional objects and orthogonality

    An object \(E\) is exceptional if \(\operatorname{Hom}(E,E)=\C\) and \(\operatorname{Hom}(E,E[i])=0\) for \(i\ne0\text{.}\) An exceptional collection \(E_0,\ldots,E_r\) additionally satisfies \(\operatorname{Hom}(E_j,E_i[k])=0\) for every \(j>i\) and every integer \(k\text{.}\) The order is part of the data.
    A semiorthogonal decomposition \(\mathcal D=\langle\mathcal A_1,\ldots,\mathcal A_m\rangle\) consists of full triangulated subcategories which generate \(\mathcal D\) and satisfy \(\operatorname{Hom}(\mathcal A_j,\mathcal A_i[k])=0\) for \(j>i\text{.}\) Generation means that every object can be obtained by successive triangles with terms in these subcategories. A subcategory is admissible if its inclusion has both a left and a right adjoint.2
    For a subcategory \(\mathcal B\subset\mathcal D\text{,}\) the right orthogonal \(\mathcal B^\perp\) consists of objects \(F\) such that \(\operatorname{Hom}(B,F[k])=0\) for every \(B\in\mathcal B\) and every \(k\text{.}\) The left orthogonal \({}^\perp\mathcal B\) reverses the arguments of Hom. With this convention,
    \begin{equation*} \Ku(X)=\langle\cO_X,\cO_X(1),\cO_X(2)\rangle^\perp \end{equation*}
    occurs on the left in the decomposition of \(D^b(X)\text{.}\)
    The required exceptional collection follows from elementary cohomology. For \(t=-1,-2\text{,}\) the hypersurface sequence
    \begin{equation*} 0\longrightarrow\cO_{\Pp^5}(t-3)\longrightarrow\cO_{\Pp^5}(t)\longrightarrow\cO_X(t)\longrightarrow0 \end{equation*}
    gives \(H^i(X,\cO_X(t))=0\) for all \(i\text{.}\) At \(t=0\text{,}\) it gives \(H^0(X,\cO_X)=\C\) and no higher cohomology. Since \(\operatorname{Ext}^k(\cO_X(j),\cO_X(i))=H^k(X,\cO_X(i-j))\text{,}\) these are exactly the exceptional and semiorthogonality conditions.

    Section B.3 Removing exceptional components

    For an exceptional object \(E\text{,}\) its left mutation on an object \(F\) is specified by the evaluation triangle
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{RHom}(E,F)\otimes E\longrightarrow F\longrightarrow L_E(F). \end{equation*}
    Applying \(\operatorname{RHom}(E,-)\) shows that the first arrow becomes an isomorphism, so \(L_E(F)\in E^\perp\text{.}\) Applying Hom into an object of \(E^\perp\) also shows that \(L_E\) is the left adjoint projection to that orthogonal.
    For the cubic exceptional collection, the projection to \(\Ku(X)\) is
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{pr}=L_{\cO_X}\circ L_{\cO_X(1)}\circ L_{\cO_X(2)}. \end{equation*}
    The rightmost mutation is applied first. After eliminating \(\cO_X(2)\text{,}\) eliminating \(\cO_X(1)\) preserves the first vanishing because \(\operatorname{Hom}(\cO_X(2),\cO_X(1)[k])=0\text{.}\) The same reasoning applies to the final mutation through \(\cO_X\text{.}\) Thus the output lies in all three right orthogonals. For \(A\in\Ku(X)\text{,}\) the evaluation triangles give \(\operatorname{Hom}(\operatorname{pr}F,A)\simeq\operatorname{Hom}(F,A)\text{.}\) Hence \(\operatorname{pr}\) is the left adjoint to the inclusion.
    For example, if \(\ell\subset X\) is a line, then \(H^0(X,I_\ell(1))\) has dimension four. The ideal sheaf \(I_\ell(1)\) therefore does not lie in \(\Ku(X)\text{:}\) its Hom from \(\cO_X\) is nonzero. Applying \(\operatorname{pr}\) changes the object by triangles. Constructions of stable objects associated with lines use such modifications, rather than assuming that every ideal sheaf already belongs to the component.3
    For an object \(F\in\Ku(X)\text{,}\) tensoring by \(\cO_X(1)\) preserves orthogonality to \(\cO_X(1)\) and \(\cO_X(2)\text{,}\) but may lose orthogonality to \(\cO_X\text{.}\) The rotation functor repairs exactly this loss:
    \begin{equation*} R(F)=L_{\cO_X}(F(1)). \end{equation*}
    The vanishing conditions show that its output belongs to \(\Ku(X)\text{.}\) Its autoequivalence property follows from mutation theory, or from its interpretation as grading shift in the hypersurface category.

    Section B.4 The cubic equation and the shift by two

    Proposition B.1.

    Let \(i:\mathcal A\hookrightarrow\mathcal D\) be an admissible inclusion into a category with Serre functor, and let \(\operatorname{pr}=i^*\) be its left adjoint. Then
    \begin{equation*} S_{\mathcal A}^{-1}=\operatorname{pr}\circ S_{\mathcal D}^{-1}\circ i. \end{equation*}

    Proof.
    For \(A,B\in\mathcal A\text{,}\) adjunction and Serre duality give
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal A}(\operatorname{pr}S_{\mathcal D}^{-1}iB,A)\simeq\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal D}(S_{\mathcal D}^{-1}iB,iA)\simeq\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal A}(A,B)^*. \end{equation*}
    This is the defining property of \(S_{\mathcal A}^{-1}B\text{.}\) The functorial identifications determine the stated functor.
    Tensoring commutes with mutations after twisting the exceptional object: \((-\otimes\cO_X(1))L_E\simeq L_{E(1)}(-\otimes\cO_X(1))\text{.}\) Applying this identity three times gives
    \begin{equation*} R^3=L_{\cO_X}L_{\cO_X(1)}L_{\cO_X(2)}(-\otimes\cO_X(3))=\operatorname{pr}(-\otimes\cO_X(3)) \end{equation*}
    on \(\Ku(X)\text{.}\) Since \(S_{D^b(X)}^{-1}=(-\otimes\cO_X(3))[-4]\text{,}\) the proposition gives
    \begin{equation*} S_{\Ku(X)}^{-1}=R^3[-4]. \end{equation*}
    The remaining relation is \(R^3\simeq[2]\text{.}\) Its origin is the degree-three equation. In the graded matrix-factorization description of a hypersurface, an object is a pair of graded free modules with maps whose two composites are multiplication by the defining polynomial. A double homological shift returns to the same two modules with grading advanced by the polynomial degree. For a cubic, the internal grading shift \((1)\) therefore satisfies \((3)=[2]\text{.}\) Orlov identifies the matrix-factorization category with the hypersurface’s residual category [Orl09, Theorem 3.11]. Under the identification for the cubic component, the internal degree-one shift corresponds to \(R\text{;}\) the shift calculation is explained in [Huy17, Section 3.5].
    Substitution gives \(S_{\Ku(X)}^{-1}=[-2]\text{,}\) hence
    \begin{equation*} S_{\Ku(X)}=[2]. \end{equation*}
    The mutation and adjunction computations explain how the four-dimensional ambient Serre functor becomes two-dimensional on the component. The identification with matrix factorizations is the substantive hypersurface theorem used in the last step.

    Section B.5 Families and Brauer twisting

    A Brauer class \(\alpha\in\operatorname{Br}(S)\) can be represented by an Azumaya algebra, locally a matrix algebra in the étale topology. Its modules define twisted coherent sheaves, up to the usual Morita equivalence. One can also describe twisting by local sheaves whose transition isomorphisms satisfy the cocycle condition only up to a multiplicative two-cocycle representing \(\alpha\text{.}\) When \(\alpha=0\text{,}\) they are ordinary sheaves.
    The same phenomenon occurs when local universal families on a moduli space fail to glue to an ordinary global family. Their scalar discrepancies define a Brauer class. A twisted universal family can still induce a Fourier–Mukai functor. Numerical conditions providing a class with Mukai pairing one are sufficient to trivialize this obstruction in the K3 moduli constructions used in Chapter 7.5
    For a kernel \(\mathcal E\) on a product \(S\times X\text{,}\) its Fourier–Mukai transform has the form
    \begin{equation*} \Phi_{\mathcal E}(F)=Rp_{X*}(Lp_S^*F\mathbin{\otimes}^{L}\mathcal E). \end{equation*}
    In the twisted case, the kernel carries the opposite twist on the \(S\) factor, so the tensor product descends. Full faithfulness and essential surjectivity require proof; the existence of a family of objects alone does not imply an equivalence.

The derived categories used here have enhancements, and mutation functors are constructed in that setting. The displayed triangles specify their action without requiring a functorial choice of cones in every triangulated category.

    2

    Semiorthogonality specifies one direction of vanishing. Maps in the other direction can carry substantial information. A semiorthogonal decomposition is therefore not usually a direct product of categories.

    3

    The three mutations do not generally commute. Their order here is dictated by the exceptional collection. A projection functor is also different from tensoring by a line bundle, which can move an object out of the orthogonal.

    4

    The relation \(R^3=[2]\) concerns rotation on the component. On \(D^b(X)\text{,}\) tensoring three times by \(\cO_X(1)\) is tensoring by \(\cO_X(3)\text{.}\) This is not a cohomological shift.

    5

    A nonzero Brauer class on a particular K3 surface does not prove that its twisted derived category has no untwisted realization on another K3 surface. The untwisted criterion asks for the existence of some such surface, and is expressed by the whole algebraic Mukai lattice.
