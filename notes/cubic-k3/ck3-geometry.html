    Chapter 1 The geometry and cohomology of a cubic fourfold

    Section 1.1 The hypersurface and its polarization

    Let \(V\) be a six-dimensional complex vector space. A cubic fourfold is a smooth hypersurface \(X=V(f)\subset\mathbb P(V)\) defined by a homogeneous cubic polynomial. Write \(h=c_1(\mathcal O_X(1))\text{.}\) The word fourfold refers to the complex dimension of \(X\text{,}\) so its underlying real manifold has dimension eight. Integration takes a class in \(H^8(X,\mathbb Z)\) to an integer. In particular, \(\int_X h^4=3\text{,}\) since four general hyperplanes cut the cubic in three points.1
    The canonical bundle follows from the conormal sequence. For a smooth hypersurface of degree three in \(\mathbb P^5\text{,}\) taking determinants gives
    \begin{gather*} K_X=(K_{\mathbb P^5}\otimes\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^5}(3))|_X=\mathcal O_X(-3). \end{gather*}
    Thus \(X\) is Fano of index three. The Lefschetz hyperplane theorem gives \(\operatorname{Pic}(X)=\mathbb Z\mathcal O_X(1)\text{,}\) so the index is exactly three. The cubic has an ample anticanonical bundle, whereas a K3 surface has trivial canonical bundle. The relation with K3 surfaces must therefore concern another geometric object or a part of cohomology.
    The same hyperplane theorem gives the integral cohomology below the middle degree. It agrees with that of projective space: \(H^0(X,\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z\text{,}\) \(H^2(X,\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z h\text{,}\) and \(H^1(X,\mathbb Z)=H^3(X,\mathbb Z)=0\text{.}\) Poincaré duality gives the corresponding groups above the middle degree. All cohomology is torsion-free. The odd groups vanish, and the only even group whose rank is not one is \(H^4(X,\mathbb Z)\text{.}\)
    The hyperplane powers need not generate the groups in which they lie. If \(\ell\subset X\) is a line, its cohomology class generates \(H^6(X,\mathbb Z)\text{,}\) because \(\int_X h[\ell]=1\text{.}\) Consequently \(h^3=3[\ell]\text{.}\) In top degree, one similarly has \(h^4=3[\mathrm{pt}]\text{.}\) These relations express divisibility in integral cohomology. They do not mean that a formal division by three is available for every algebraic cycle class. The middle-degree class \(h^2\text{,}\) by comparison, is primitive as an integral vector: if it were divisible by an integer \(k>1\text{,}\) its square three would be divisible by \(k^2\text{.}\)2

    Section 1.2 The Jacobian ring and the Hodge numbers

    The Hodge numbers can be calculated from the defining equation. Let \(S=\mathbb C[x_0,\ldots,x_5]\) with its usual grading, and define the Jacobian ring
    \begin{gather*} R_f=S/(\partial f/\partial x_0,\ldots,\partial f/\partial x_5). \end{gather*}
    Smoothness says that the six partial derivatives have no common zero in projective space. Their affine common zero is therefore the origin. Since the polynomial ring is Cohen–Macaulay, these six quadrics form a regular sequence. The Hilbert series of the quotient is
    \begin{gather*} \sum_{j\geq0}\dim(R_f)_j\,t^j=\frac{(1-t^2)^6}{(1-t)^6}=(1+t)^6. \end{gather*}
    Every coefficient can now be read from the binomial expansion.
    Griffiths’ residue theorem relates these graded pieces to primitive cohomology [Gri69, Section 10]:
    \begin{gather*} H^{4-p,p}(X)_{\mathrm{prim}}\simeq(R_f)_{3(p+1)-6}. \end{gather*}
    One way to see the degree is to contract \(dx_0\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_5\) with the Euler vector field \(\sum_i x_i\partial/\partial x_i\text{.}\) The resulting homogeneous five-form \(\Omega\) has degree six. A rational form \(P\Omega/f^{p+1}\) descends to projective space when \(\deg P+6=3(p+1)\text{.}\) Taking its residue gives a middle cohomology class. Reducing pole order identifies derivatives of \(f\) with relations in the Jacobian ideal. The theorem asserts that this construction gives the stated Hodge pieces, rather than merely a collection of classes in them.
    3
    The indices for \(p=0,1,2,3,4\) are \(-3,0,3,6,9\text{.}\) The negative piece is zero, and the quotient has top degree six. The middle coefficients of \((1+t)^6\) therefore give
    \begin{gather*} (h^{4,0}_{\mathrm{prim}},h^{3,1}_{\mathrm{prim}},h^{2,2}_{\mathrm{prim}},h^{1,3}_{\mathrm{prim}},h^{0,4}_{\mathrm{prim}})=(0,1,20,1,0). \end{gather*}
    Restoring the ambient class \(h^2\) adds one dimension of type \((2,2)\text{.}\) Thus the middle Hodge numbers of \(X\) are \((0,1,21,1,0)\text{,}\) and its fourth Betti number is twenty-three. The one-dimensional \(H^{3,1}\) is the period line that will recur in the K3 comparisons.

    Example 1.1.

    For the Fermat cubic \(f=x_0^3+\cdots+x_5^3\text{,}\) the quotient is
    \begin{gather*} R_f=\mathbb C[x_0,\ldots,x_5]/(x_0^2,\ldots,x_5^2). \end{gather*}
    A basis consists of square-free monomials. There is one constant monomial, twenty degree-three monomials, and one degree-six monomial \(x_0x_1\cdots x_5\text{.}\) This gives the primitive Hodge numbers directly. The calculation computes dimensions for every smooth cubic because the regular-sequence Hilbert series is independent of the coefficients. It does not assert that different cubics have isomorphic Hodge structures.
    The distinction between dimensions and structures is already visible here. A period line is a one-dimensional subspace of a fixed complex vector space after a local topological identification of the family. It can move while all dimensions remain constant. The Jacobian description makes that movement computable by differentiating the rational forms with respect to the coefficients of \(f\text{.}\)

    Section 1.3 Primitive and transcendental cohomology

    Write \(L_X=H^4(X,\mathbb Z)\) and equip it with the cup-product pairing \((a,b)_X=\int_X ab\text{.}\) The primitive lattice is
    \begin{gather*} L_X^0=(h^2)^\perp=\{a\in L_X:(a,h^2)_X=0\}. \end{gather*}
    Over \(\mathbb Q\) there is an orthogonal decomposition \(L_{X,\mathbb Q}=\mathbb Q h^2\perp L_{X,\mathbb Q}^0\text{.}\) The projection of a class \(a\) to the primitive part is \(a-\frac{(a,h^2)_X}{3}h^2\text{.}\) The denominator explains why this rational decomposition need not be an integral direct sum.
    Poincaré duality makes \(L_X\) unimodular. Its signature is \((21,2)\text{:}\) the real plane underlying \(H^{3,1}\oplus H^{1,3}\) is negative, and the real \((2,2)\) part is positive. Since \((h^2,h^2)_X=3\text{,}\) the full lattice is odd. Hassett determines the lattice together with its primitive part in [Has00, Proposition 2.1.2]. In particular, \(L_X^0\) is even, has rank twenty-two and discriminant three, and has signature \((20,2)\text{.}\) The sublattice \(\mathbb Z h^2\perp L_X^0\) has index three in \(L_X\text{.}\)
    The integral Hodge lattice is \(A_X=L_X\cap H^{2,2}(X)\text{.}\) Its orthogonal complement \(T_X=A_X^\perp\subset L_X\) is the transcendental lattice. The word transcendental refers to this orthogonal complement, not to the complement of a chosen basis of classes represented by surfaces. Since \(h^2\in A_X\text{,}\) one has \(T_X\subset L_X^0\text{.}\) If \(r=\operatorname{rank}A_X\text{,}\) then \(\operatorname{rank}T_X=23-r\text{.}\) A very general cubic has \(A_X=\mathbb Z h^2\text{,}\) so its primitive and transcendental lattices coincide. On a special cubic, the primitive lattice still has rank twenty-two, while the transcendental rank is smaller.4

    Example 1.2.

    Suppose that \(X\) contains a plane \(P\text{.}\) Then \(([P],h^2)_X=1\text{,}\) so \([P]\) does not lie in \(L_X^0\text{,}\) although it is primitive as an integral vector. Its primitive rational projection is \([P]-h^2/3\text{.}\) Multiplying by three gives the integral Hodge class \(3[P]-h^2\in L_X^0\text{.}\) This class is nonzero: otherwise \(h^2\) would be divisible by three. Thus a plane produces an algebraic class in primitive cohomology and strictly reduces the transcendental rank.
    The self-intersection can also be calculated. Writing \(u=c_1(\mathcal O_P(1))\text{,}\) the normal sequence of \(P\subset X\subset\mathbb P^5\) gives \(c(N_{P/X})=(1+u)^3/(1+3u)\text{.}\) Its degree-two term is \(3u^2\text{,}\) so \(([P],[P])_X=3\text{.}\) The two classes \(h^2,[P]\) therefore span a lattice with Gram matrix \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)\text{,}\) of discriminant eight. The integral primitive class \(3[P]-h^2\) has square twenty-four. This example separates three quantities: the degree of the surface, its self-intersection, and the discriminant of the lattice it supplies. None is determined by the word primitive alone.
    A Tate twist \(V(1)\) changes a Hodge bidegree \((p,q)\) to \((p-1,q-1)\text{.}\) The weight-four Hodge structure \(L_X^0(1)\) consequently has weight two and Hodge numbers \((1,20,1)\text{.}\) In comparisons with weight-two polarized structures, we use the negative of the cubic cup form. This gives signature \((2,20)\) on \(L_X^0(1)\text{.}\) The two operations, shifting Hodge indices and changing the displayed bilinear form, are stated separately so that the sign can be checked in each comparison.5

    Section 1.4 Deformations and rationality

    A cubic polynomial in six variables has \(\binom83=56\) coefficients. Scalar multiples define the same hypersurface, so smooth cubic equations form a nonempty Zariski open subset of \(\mathbb P^{55}\text{.}\) Projective coordinate changes form \(\operatorname{PGL}(6)\text{,}\) of dimension thirty-five. Their quotient has dimension twenty. The Jacobian ring explains this subtraction intrinsically: the infinitesimal change of equation produced by a linear change of variables belongs to the degree-three Jacobian ideal.
    The six quadratic partial derivatives have no syzygy with linear coefficients, because the first syzygies of a regular sequence of quadrics are generated by the Koszul relations with quadratic coefficients. Their products with the six coordinates therefore span a thirty-six-dimensional subspace of \(S_3\text{.}\) Euler’s identity \(3f=\sum_i x_i(\partial f/\partial x_i)\) includes the rescaling direction in that subspace. The quotient \((R_f)_3\) has dimension \(56-36=20\) and is the tangent space of deformations modulo coordinate change. The normal and Euler sequences identify it with \(H^1(X,\mathcal T_X)\text{,}\) where \(\mathcal T_X\) is the tangent sheaf. Finite automorphisms can produce quotient singularities in the coarse moduli space, but they do not change its dimension.
    The residue construction also explains why twenty moduli can be detected by the period line. Its infinitesimal variation is the multiplication map \((R_f)_3\otimes(R_f)_0\to(R_f)_3\text{.}\) The factor \((R_f)_0=\mathbb C\) represents \(H^{3,1}(X)\text{,}\) and the target represents \(H^{2,2}(X)_{\mathrm{prim}}\text{.}\) Multiplication by the constant generator identifies the deformation space with the possible first derivatives of that line. This is the infinitesimal period calculation. The global Torelli theorem in Chapter 4 determines a cubic from its integral polarized Hodge structure.
    The parameter space of smooth cubic equations is connected. Any two smooth cubic fourfolds can therefore be joined in a smooth projective family, possibly after restricting to a connected sequence of parameter curves. This explains why their Betti and Hodge numbers agree. It does not make their period lines or their rational Hodge lattices equal. The locations at which extra rational classes acquire type \((2,2)\) will define the special loci considered later.
    A fourfold is rational if it is birational to \(\mathbb P^4\text{.}\) It is stably rational if its product with some projective space is rational. It is unirational if a projective space admits a dominant rational map to it. Rationality implies stable rationality, which implies unirationality. Smooth complex cubic fourfolds are unirational; a parametrization can have degree greater than one, so this fact does not give a birational map. These notes use K3 structures to compare cohomology, varieties of lines, and categories. Each connection has to be distinguished from a rationality conclusion.6

    1. Degrees and dimensions.

    A divisor class has cohomological degree two. A surface in a fourfold has complex codimension two and cohomological degree four. The expression \(h^4\) is a top-degree class, while \(H^4(X,\mathbb Z)\) is the middle cohomology group.

    2. An integral distinction.

    The statement that cohomology outside the middle degree is inherited from projective space is safest over \(\mathbb Q\text{.}\) Over \(\mathbb Z\text{,}\) the restriction of \(h^3\) is three times a generator. This distinction will matter when discriminants of lattices are compared.

    3. A theorem behind the count.

    The Hilbert series is a short commutative-algebra calculation. The identification with Hodge cohomology uses the residue theorem. Counting polynomial monomials alone would not prove a statement about the Hodge decomposition.

    4. Two orthogonal complements.

    Primitive cohomology is orthogonal to the single class \(h^2\text{.}\) Transcendental cohomology is orthogonal to every rational Hodge class of type \((2,2)\text{.}\) The twenty-dimensional complex space \(H^{2,2}_{\mathrm{prim}}\) need not contain a nonzero rational vector.
    Primitivity of an integral vector has a different meaning: the vector has no nontrivial integer factor. Such a vector need not belong to primitive cohomology.

    5. K3 type.

    A weight-two Hodge structure of K3 type has a one-dimensional \((2,0)\) part. This description does not assert that it is the second cohomology of a projective K3 surface. Such a surface has an ample integral \((1,1)\) class; the transcendental part of a very general cubic has no such class.

    6. General and very general.

    A general point belongs to a specified nonempty Zariski open subset. A very general point avoids a specified countable union of proper closed algebraic subsets. Conditions on rational Hodge classes naturally involve the latter quantifier, since there are countably many rational classes to consider.
