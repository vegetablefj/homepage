    Chapter 6 The Kuznetsov component

    A smooth cubic fourfold has a category with the Serre duality and Hochschild homology of a K3 surface. This category exists for every cubic fourfold. An equivalence with the derived category of an actual K3 surface requires an additional condition. We construct the category, explain its two-dimensional Calabi–Yau property, and describe the equivalences arising from a plane and from a Pfaffian presentation.

    Section 6.1 Three exceptional line bundles

    Let \(X\subset\Pp^5\) be a smooth cubic fourfold, and write \(D^b(X)\) for the bounded derived category of coherent sheaves on \(X\text{.}\) For objects \(E,F\text{,}\) the group \(\operatorname{Ext}^k(E,F)\) is \(\operatorname{Hom}(E,F[k])\text{.}\) An object is exceptional when its endomorphisms are the scalars and its other self-Ext groups vanish. A sequence of exceptional objects \((E_1,\ldots,E_r)\) is an exceptional collection if \(\operatorname{Ext}^*(E_j,E_i)=0\) for \(j>i\text{.}\)
    The hypersurface sequence
    \begin{equation*} 0\longrightarrow\cO_{\Pp^5}(t-3)\longrightarrow\cO_{\Pp^5}(t)\longrightarrow\cO_X(t)\longrightarrow0 \end{equation*}
    gives \(H^*(X,\cO_X(-1))=H^*(X,\cO_X(-2))=0\text{.}\) It also gives \(H^0(X,\cO_X)=\C\) and \(H^k(X,\cO_X)=0\) for \(k>0\text{.}\) Consequently \(\cO_X\text{,}\) \(\cO_X(1)\text{,}\) and \(\cO_X(2)\) are exceptional, and the required groups between them vanish in the indicated direction.
    1
    Let \(\mathcal E=\langle\cO_X,\cO_X(1),\cO_X(2)\rangle\) be the triangulated subcategory generated by these line bundles. The Kuznetsov component is its right orthogonal
    \begin{equation*} \Ku(X)=\mathcal E^\perp=\{F\in D^b(X):\operatorname{Hom}(\cO_X(i),F[k])=0\text{ for }i=0,1,2\text{ and all }k\in\Z\}. \end{equation*}
    The subcategory generated by an exceptional collection is admissible: its inclusion has left and right adjoints. Its orthogonal therefore gives the semiorthogonal decomposition
    \begin{equation*} D^b(X)=\langle\Ku(X),\cO_X,\cO_X(1),\cO_X(2)\rangle. \end{equation*}
    In this ordering, semiorthogonality means that morphisms from a later component to an earlier component vanish. Every object can be obtained by successive extension triangles from objects in the four displayed components. It does not mean that an object is their direct sum. The exceptional objects describe three copies of \(D^b(\operatorname{Spec}\C)\text{,}\) while \(\Ku(X)\) contains the rest of the category.2
    For an exceptional object \(E\text{,}\) left mutation is defined by the evaluation triangle
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{RHom}(E,F)\otimes E\longrightarrow F\longrightarrow L_E(F). \end{equation*}
    Mutation removes the part detected by morphisms from \(E\text{.}\) Successive mutations give the projection
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{pr}=L_{\cO_X}\circ L_{\cO_X(1)}\circ L_{\cO_X(2)}:D^b(X)\longrightarrow\Ku(X). \end{equation*}
    Composition is read from right to left. This projection is the left adjoint of the inclusion \(i:\Ku(X)\hookrightarrow D^b(X)\text{.}\) The adjunction and the triangles are explained in Appendix B.
    A line gives a concrete object in this orthogonal. Its ideal is generated by the four independent linear equations of the line. Define a coherent sheaf \(F_\ell\) by
    \begin{equation*} 0\longrightarrow F_\ell\longrightarrow\cO_X^{\oplus4}\longrightarrow I_{\ell/X}(1)\longrightarrow0. \end{equation*}
    The map on global sections is an isomorphism, so \(H^*(X,F_\ell)=0\text{.}\) Twisting by \((-1)\text{,}\) the sequence for \(I_{\ell/X}\) and the isomorphism \(H^0(\cO_X)\to H^0(\cO_\ell)\) give the same vanishing for \(F_\ell(-1)\text{.}\) Twisting once more uses the vanishings for \(\cO_X(-1)\) and \(\cO_\ell(-1)\text{,}\) giving \(H^*(X,F_\ell(-2))=0\text{.}\) Thus \(F_\ell\in\Ku(X)\text{.}\) This is an orthogonality check on a sheaf of rank three, not just a formal property of an unnamed subcategory.
    The mutation calculation agrees with the sequence. There is no morphism from \(\cO_X(2)\) to \(\cO_\ell(1)\) in any degree. Mutation through \(\cO_X(1)\) gives \(I_{\ell/X}(1)[1]\text{,}\) and mutation through \(\cO_X\) gives \(F_\ell[2]\text{.}\) Hence \(\operatorname{pr}(\cO_\ell(1))\simeq F_\ell[2]\text{.}\) The shifts matter for the objects, while the even shift leaves the K-class equal to \([F_\ell]\text{.}\)

    Section 6.2 Serre duality and rotation

    A Serre functor on a category with finite-dimensional morphism spaces is an autoequivalence \(S\) satisfying functorial isomorphisms \(\operatorname{Hom}(E,F)\simeq\operatorname{Hom}(F,S(E))^\vee\text{.}\) On a smooth projective fourfold it is tensor product with the canonical bundle followed by \([4]\text{.}\) Adjunction gives \(\omega_X=\cO_X(-3)\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} S_{D^b(X)}(E)=E(-3)[4]. \end{equation*}
    The Serre functor on an admissible component involves projection. Using the left adjoint \(\operatorname{pr}\text{,}\) the formula for the inverse Serre functor is
    \begin{equation*} S_{\Ku(X)}^{-1}=\operatorname{pr}\circ S_{D^b(X)}^{-1}\circ i. \end{equation*}
    It follows that \(S_{\Ku(X)}^{-1}(E)=\operatorname{pr}(E(3))[-4]\text{.}\) This is an adjunction identity, not an ordinary restriction of \(E\mapsto E(-3)[4]\text{.}\)
    3
    For \(E\in\Ku(X)\text{,}\) the rotation functor is defined by
    \begin{equation*} R(E)=L_{\cO_X}(E(1)). \end{equation*}
    It takes values in \(\Ku(X)\text{,}\) and successive mutation triangles give
    \begin{equation*} R^3(E)=\operatorname{pr}(E(3)). \end{equation*}
    The orthogonality check and the calculation with three mutations appear in Section B.4.
    The hypersurface theorem of Orlov identifies this rotation with the degree-one shift in the category of graded matrix factorizations of the cubic equation. In that category the degree-three shift is the homological shift \([2]\text{.}\) Thus \(R\) is an autoequivalence and \(R^3\simeq[2]\text{.}\) The mutation and adjunction identities yield
    \begin{equation*} S_{\Ku(X)}^{-1}\simeq R^3[-4]\simeq[-2], \end{equation*}
    and hence \(S_{\Ku(X)}\simeq[2]\text{.}\) See Section B.4 for the calculation, and [Huy17, Section 3.5] for the graded matrix factorization description.
    For objects in the component, Serre duality now reads
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Ext}^k(E,F)\simeq\operatorname{Ext}^{2-k}(F,E)^\vee. \end{equation*}
    This property is called two-dimensional Calabi–Yau duality. Kuznetsov’s general theorem on residual categories of hypersurfaces gives the same conclusion [KuzCY, Corollary 4.1]. A K3 surface satisfies this duality because its canonical bundle is trivial. An abelian surface does too, so the Serre functor alone does not characterize a K3 derived category.

    Section 6.3 Hochschild homology

    Hochschild homology gives a second comparison with a K3 surface. For a smooth projective complex variety, the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg isomorphism gives
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{HH}_k(D^b(X))\simeq\bigoplus_{p-q=k}H^q(X,\Omega_X^p). \end{equation*}
    The diagonal Hodge numbers of a cubic fourfold are \(1,1,21,1,1\text{.}\) Its only off-diagonal Hodge numbers are \(h^{3,1}=h^{1,3}=1\text{.}\) Therefore \(\operatorname{HH}_0(D^b(X))\) has dimension twenty-five, and \(\operatorname{HH}_{2}\) and \(\operatorname{HH}_{-2}\) each have dimension one.
    Hochschild homology is additive for a semiorthogonal decomposition [KuzHH]. Each exceptional object contributes one copy of \(\C\) in degree zero. Removing the three exceptional components gives
    \begin{equation*} \dim\operatorname{HH}_k(\Ku(X))=\begin{cases}22,&k=0,\\1,&k=2\text{ or }-2,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases} \end{equation*}
    These are the Hochschild homology dimensions of a K3 surface.
    4
    The term K3 category refers here to the smooth proper component with this Calabi–Yau duality and K3 Hochschild homology. It permits categories that are not equivalent to the derived category of any surface. The distinction will be visible in the algebraic Mukai lattice: for a cubic outside all special divisors, that lattice is positive definite of rank two and has no isotropic vector.

    Section 6.4 A plane and its Clifford algebra

    Suppose that \(X\) contains a plane \(P\text{.}\) Projection from \(P\text{,}\) resolved by blowing up the plane, gives a quadric surface fibration \(q:\widetilde X\to\Pp^2\text{.}\) Assume its discriminant sextic is smooth. The double cover \(S\to\Pp^2\) branched along that sextic is a smooth K3 surface. Over a smooth quadric there are two rulings by lines. Choosing a ruling accounts for the double cover, while choosing a line in that ruling gives a \(\Pp^1\) over the corresponding point of \(S\text{.}\)
    The relative family of these lines is a Brauer–Severi fibration over \(S\text{.}\) Locally in the étale topology it is the projectivization of a rank-two bundle. Globally there can be an obstruction to choosing that bundle, represented by \(\alpha\in\operatorname{Br}(S)[2]\text{.}\) The even Clifford algebra \(\mathcal B_0\) of the quadratic form describes the same obstruction. Its rank is eight over \(\Pp^2\text{;}\) after passage to the discriminant cover it is described by an Azumaya algebra of rank four.
    The derived category of the quadric fibration has a semiorthogonal decomposition containing \(D^b(\Pp^2,\mathcal B_0)\) and two copies of \(D^b(\Pp^2)\text{.}\) The blowup formula gives another decomposition containing \(D^b(X)\) and one copy of \(D^b(P)\text{.}\) Kuznetsov compares these decompositions by mutations and proves
    \begin{equation*} \Ku(X)\simeq D^b(\Pp^2,\mathcal B_0)\simeq D^b(S,\alpha). \end{equation*}
    Here the middle category is the category of coherent right Clifford modules, and the last is the category of twisted coherent sheaves [Kuz10, Lemma 4.2 and Theorem 4.3]. The comparison of components is a theorem about the mutation functors, not a consequence of counting the exceptional objects.
    5
    If \(\alpha=0\text{,}\) Morita equivalence gives \(D^b(S,\alpha)\simeq D^b(S)\text{.}\) When \(\alpha\ne0\text{,}\) the category is still a twisted K3 category. The twist records the obstruction in this particular construction. Whether the same category has an untwisted realization on some other K3 surface is a separate question, answered by the existence of a hyperbolic plane in its algebraic Mukai lattice.

    Section 6.5 A Pfaffian presentation

    Let \(V\) be a six-dimensional vector space and \(L\subset\bigwedge^2V^*\) a six-dimensional subspace. The Pfaffian hypersurface parametrizes skew forms of rank at most four. Assume that its intersection with \(\Pp(L)\) is a smooth cubic fourfold \(X\text{.}\) The orthogonal section
    \begin{equation*} S=\operatorname{Gr}(2,V)\cap\Pp(L^\perp) \end{equation*}
    is a smooth degree-fourteen K3 surface. The Grassmannian has dimension eight and canonical bundle \(\cO(-6)\text{.}\) The six hyperplanes defining this section give trivial canonical bundle by adjunction, and the hyperplane theorem gives \(H^1(S,\cO_S)=0\text{.}\)
    Homological projective duality compares complementary linear sections of the Grassmannian and the Pfaffian. In this dimension the comparison identifies the residual category on the cubic side with the entire derived category of the ordinary K3 surface \(S\text{.}\) It constructs a Fourier–Mukai equivalence
    \begin{equation*} \Ku(X)\simeq D^b(S) \end{equation*}
    with an ordinary kernel on \(S\times X\) [Kuz10, Theorem 3.1]. This is an untwisted equivalence. The K3 surface arises as an actual Grassmannian section in the homological projective duality theorem.
    6
    The examples have the same Serre functor and Hochschild homology, but different possible geometric realizations. A very general cubic containing a plane has a twisted realization and no untwisted realization. A smooth Pfaffian cubic has an untwisted one. A cubic outside the special divisors has neither. Distinguishing these cases requires the integral lattice and its Hodge structure, to which we turn in Chapter 7.

    1. Shift and twist.

    The shift \([k]\) moves the degrees of a complex. The twist \((t)\) means tensor product with \(\cO_X(t)\text{.}\) These are different operations, including in the formula for the Serre functor.

    2. The direction of orthogonality.

    The condition is \(\operatorname{Hom}(\cO_X(i),F[k])=0\text{.}\) Reversing the two arguments defines the other orthogonal and changes the projection functor.

    3. Which adjoint occurs?

    If \(i^!\) is the right adjoint of the inclusion, then \(S_{\Ku(X)}=i^!S_{D^b(X)}i\text{.}\) The left adjoint occurs in the formula for the inverse. Interchanging them would give an incorrect Serre calculation.

    4. A necessary comparison.

    The calculation is an equality of graded dimensions. Constructing an equivalence requires a functor and a proof that it is fully faithful and essentially surjective. Matching these dimensions does not provide such a functor.

    5. The smooth discriminant hypothesis.

    A plane in a smooth cubic can give a singular discriminant sextic. The description by a smooth double-plane K3 and an Azumaya algebra uses the hypothesis stated here. The general lattice criterion in Chapter 7 applies to every smooth cubic.

    6. Two sources of K3 surfaces.

    For a plane, the family of lines in a chosen ruling can have a Brauer obstruction. For the smooth Pfaffian section, the equivalence comes from the derived category of the Grassmannian section itself. The two constructions explain why a twist occurs in one description and an ordinary category in the other.
