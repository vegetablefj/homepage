    Chapter 6 Kuznetsov component

    The Kuznetsov component is the part of the derived category left after removing three exceptional line bundles. We construct its projection and an object associated with a line, then compute its Serre functor and Hochschild homology. Two geometric constructions produce equivalences with K3 derived categories: a plane gives a twisted category, allowing a trivial twist, while a Pfaffian presentation gives an untwisted one. The mutation calculations are proved here and in the appendix. The hypersurface, quadric-fibration, and homological projective duality theorems used to obtain equivalences are stated with their hypotheses and invoked from the literature.

    Section 6.1 Three exceptional line bundles

    All categories and exact functors are complex-linear, and composition is read from right to left. For a smooth projective complex variety \(Y\text{,}\) write \(D^b(Y)=D^b(\operatorname{Coh}Y)\text{,}\) with \(F[1]^j=F^{j+1}\) and \(\operatorname{Ext}^k(E,F)=\operatorname{Hom}(E,F[k])\text{.}\) Fix a smooth complex cubic fourfold \(X\subset\Pp^5\text{.}\)
    The hypersurface sequence
    \begin{equation*} 0\longrightarrow\cO_{\Pp^5}(t-3)\longrightarrow\cO_{\Pp^5}(t)\longrightarrow\cO_X(t)\longrightarrow0 \end{equation*}
    gives \(H^*(X,\cO_X(-1))=H^*(X,\cO_X(-2))=0\text{.}\) It also gives \(H^0(X,\cO_X)=\C\) and \(H^k(X,\cO_X)=0\) for \(k>0\text{.}\) Consequently \(\cO_X\text{,}\) \(\cO_X(1)\text{,}\) and \(\cO_X(2)\) are exceptional, and the required groups between them vanish in the indicated direction.
    1
    Let \(\mathcal E=\langle\cO_X,\cO_X(1),\cO_X(2)\rangle\) be the triangulated subcategory generated by these line bundles. The Kuznetsov component is its right orthogonal
    \begin{equation*} \Ku(X)=\mathcal E^\perp=\{F\in D^b(X):\operatorname{Hom}(\cO_X(i),F[k])=0\text{ for }i=0,1,2\text{ and all }k\in\Z\}. \end{equation*}
    This exceptional collection is admissible and gives the semiorthogonal decomposition
    \begin{equation*} D^b(X)=\langle\Ku(X),\cO_X,\cO_X(1),\cO_X(2)\rangle. \end{equation*}
    We use the admissibility and decomposition theorem in [Kuz10, Section 2].
    With our convention, \(\Ku(X)\) occurs on the left: morphisms from any of the three line bundles to an object of \(\Ku(X)\) vanish in every degree. The exceptional components are three copies of \(D^b(\operatorname{Spec}\C)\text{.}\) The residual component is defined by this particular embedding into \(D^b(X)\text{.}\)
    For each of these three line bundles \(E\text{,}\) the left mutation used in the projection is given by the evaluation triangle
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{RHom}(E,F)\otimes E\longrightarrow F\longrightarrow L_E(F). \end{equation*}
    Mutation removes the part detected by morphisms from \(E\text{.}\) Successive mutations give the projection
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{pr}=L_{\cO_X}\circ L_{\cO_X(1)}\circ L_{\cO_X(2)}:D^b(X)\longrightarrow\Ku(X). \end{equation*}
    This projection is the left adjoint of the inclusion \(i:\Ku(X)\hookrightarrow D^b(X)\text{.}\) The adjunction and the triangles are explained in Appendix A.
    A line gives an object in this orthogonal. Its ideal is generated by the four independent linear equations of the line. Define a coherent sheaf \(F_\ell\) by
    \begin{equation*} 0\longrightarrow F_\ell\longrightarrow\cO_X^{\oplus4}\longrightarrow I_{\ell/X}(1)\longrightarrow0. \end{equation*}
    The map on global sections is an isomorphism, so \(H^*(X,F_\ell)=0\text{.}\) Twisting by \((-1)\text{,}\) the sequence for \(I_{\ell/X}\) and the isomorphism \(H^0(\cO_X)\to H^0(\cO_\ell)\) give the same vanishing for \(F_\ell(-1)\text{.}\) Twisting once more uses the vanishings for \(\cO_X(-1)\) and \(\cO_\ell(-1)\text{,}\) giving \(H^*(X,F_\ell(-2))=0\text{.}\) Thus \(F_\ell\in\Ku(X)\text{.}\) The construction gives a coherent sheaf of rank three in the component.
    The mutation calculation agrees with the sequence. There is no morphism from \(\cO_X(2)\) to \(\cO_\ell(1)\) in any degree. Mutation through \(\cO_X(1)\) gives \(I_{\ell/X}(1)[1]\text{,}\) and mutation through \(\cO_X\) gives \(F_\ell[2]\text{.}\) Hence \(\operatorname{pr}(\cO_\ell(1))\simeq F_\ell[2]\text{.}\) The shifts matter for the objects, while the even shift leaves the K-class equal to \([F_\ell]\text{.}\)

    Section 6.2 Serre duality and rotation

    Adjunction gives \(\omega_X=\cO_X(-3)\text{.}\) Serre duality on the cubic therefore gives
    \begin{equation*} S_{D^b(X)}(E)=E(-3)[4]. \end{equation*}
    The Serre functor on an admissible component involves projection. Using the left adjoint \(\operatorname{pr}\text{,}\) the formula for the inverse Serre functor is
    \begin{equation*} S_{\Ku(X)}^{-1}=\operatorname{pr}\circ S_{D^b(X)}^{-1}\circ i. \end{equation*}
    It follows that \(S_{\Ku(X)}^{-1}(E)=\operatorname{pr}(E(3))[-4]\text{.}\) This is an adjunction identity, not an ordinary restriction of \(E\mapsto E(-3)[4]\text{.}\)
    2
    For \(E\in\Ku(X)\text{,}\) the rotation functor is defined by
    \begin{equation*} R(E)=L_{\cO_X}(E(1)). \end{equation*}
    It takes values in \(\Ku(X)\text{,}\) and successive mutation triangles give
    \begin{equation*} R^3(E)=\operatorname{pr}(E(3)). \end{equation*}
    The orthogonality check and the calculation with three mutations appear in Section A.2.
    The hypersurface theorem needed here has the following specialization. If \(f\in\C[x_0,\ldots,x_5]\) is homogeneous of degree three and \(X=\{f=0\}\subset\Pp^5\) is smooth, Orlov’s graded matrix-factorization category is equivalent to \(\Ku(X)\text{.}\) Under the equivalence the internal degree-one shift corresponds to \(R\text{,}\) and the internal degree-three shift is \([2]\text{.}\) We use this equivalence and its compatibility with grading as a theorem, with its construction in [Orl09, Theorem 3.11] and the cubic shift description in [Huy17, Section 3.5]. Thus \(R\) is an autoequivalence and \(R^3\simeq[2]\text{.}\) The mutation and adjunction identities now give
    \begin{equation*} S_{\Ku(X)}^{-1}\simeq R^3[-4]\simeq[-2], \end{equation*}
    hence \(S_{\Ku(X)}\simeq[2]\text{.}\) The complete calculation with mutations appears in Section A.2.
    For objects in the component, Serre duality now reads
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Ext}^k(E,F)\simeq\operatorname{Ext}^{2-k}(F,E)^\vee. \end{equation*}
    This property is called two-dimensional Calabi–Yau duality. Kuznetsov’s general theorem on residual categories of hypersurfaces gives the same conclusion [Kuz19, Corollary 4.1]. A K3 surface satisfies this duality because its canonical bundle is trivial. An abelian surface does too, so the Serre functor alone does not characterize a K3 derived category.

    Section 6.3 Hochschild homology

    The calculation uses two comparison theorems. For a smooth projective complex variety \(Y\text{,}\) Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg gives
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{HH}_k(D^b(Y))\simeq\bigoplus_{p-q=k}H^q(Y,\Omega_Y^p), \end{equation*}
    and an admissible semiorthogonal decomposition gives a direct sum on Hochschild homology [Kuz09, Theorems 8.3 and 7.3]. We use homological grading; the homology grading in that paper has the opposite sign. A cubic has diagonal Hodge numbers \(1,1,21,1,1\) and only two off-diagonal entries, \(h^{3,1}=h^{1,3}=1\text{.}\) Hence its whole category has Hochschild dimension twenty-five in degree zero and one in degrees two and minus two.
    Each exceptional object contributes one copy of \(\C\) in degree zero. Removing the three exceptional components gives
    \begin{equation*} \dim\operatorname{HH}_k(\Ku(X))=\begin{cases}22,&k=0,\\1,&k=2\text{ or }-2,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases} \end{equation*}
    These are the Hochschild homology dimensions of a K3 surface.
    3
    The expression K3 category records the Serre functor \([2]\) and the K3 Hochschild homology just computed. Equivalences \(\Ku(X)\simeq D^b(S,\alpha)\) with a smooth projective K3 surface are governed by Theorem 7.3.

    Section 6.4 A plane and its Clifford algebra

    Suppose that the smooth cubic \(X\) contains a plane \(P\text{.}\) Projection from \(P\text{,}\) resolved by blowing it up, gives a quadric surface fibration \(q:\widetilde X\to\Pp^2\text{.}\) Assume that its discriminant sextic \(C\) is smooth. The discriminant double cover \(\pi:S\to\Pp^2\text{,}\) branched over \(C\text{,}\) is a smooth K3 surface. A smooth quadric has two rulings by lines. Choosing a ruling accounts for the double cover, and choosing a line in it gives a projective line over the corresponding point of \(S\text{.}\)
    The family of these lines is a Brauer–Severi fibration over \(S\text{.}\) Its obstruction to being the projectivization of a rank-two vector bundle gives a class \(\alpha\in\operatorname{Br}(S)[2]\text{.}\) The fibration \(q\) also determines the sheaf \(\mathcal B_0\) of even Clifford algebras on \(\Pp^2\text{.}\) It has rank eight as an \(\cO_{\Pp^2}\)-module. The corresponding Azumaya algebra \(\mathcal A\) on \(S\) has rank four and satisfies \(\pi_*\mathcal A\simeq\mathcal B_0\text{.}\) In particular this relation is a direct-image statement.
    We use coherent right modules and choose the sign of \(\alpha\) so that \(D^b(S,\mathcal A)\simeq D^b(S,\alpha)\text{.}\) Replacing \(\mathcal A\) by its opposite changes this sign.

    Theorem 6.1.

    Under the smooth discriminant hypothesis just stated, the algebras and functors associated with the quadric fibration give equivalences
    \begin{equation*} \Ku(X)\simeq D^b(\Pp^2,\mathcal B_0)\simeq D^b(S,\mathcal A)\simeq D^b(S,\alpha). \end{equation*}
    The middle two categories are bounded derived categories of coherent right modules. The direct-image relation and the equivalences are [Kuz10, Lemma 4.2 and Theorem 4.3].
    We use this result as the quadric-fibration theorem specialized to the given plane. Its proof compares two decompositions of \(D^b(\widetilde X)\text{.}\) The quadric-fibration decomposition has one Clifford component and two copies of \(D^b(\Pp^2)\text{;}\) the blowup decomposition has \(D^b(X)\) and one copy of \(D^b(P)\text{.}\) Kuznetsov’s sequence of mutations identifies the indicated components. Huybrechts gives another account using the universal lines over the Brauer–Severi fibration [Huy23, Chapter 7, Section 3.1].4
    If \(\alpha=0\text{,}\) the construction gives \(\Ku(X)\simeq D^b(S)\text{.}\) There are \(X\in\mathcal C_8\cap\mathcal C_{14}\) for which the plane construction has \(\alpha\ne0\text{,}\) yet \(\Ku(X)\simeq D^b(S')\) for a degree-fourteen K3 surface \(S'\) [ABBVA14, Corollary B].

    Section 6.5 A Pfaffian presentation

    Let \(V\) be a six-dimensional vector space and \(L\subset\bigwedge^2V^*\) a six-dimensional subspace. The Pfaffian hypersurface parametrizes skew forms of rank at most four. Assume that its intersection with \(\Pp(L)\) is a smooth cubic fourfold \(X\text{.}\) The orthogonal section
    \begin{equation*} S=\operatorname{Gr}(2,V)\cap\Pp(L^\perp) \end{equation*}
    is a smooth degree-fourteen K3 surface. The Grassmannian has dimension eight and canonical bundle \(\cO(-6)\text{.}\) The six hyperplanes defining this section give trivial canonical bundle by adjunction, and the hyperplane theorem gives \(H^1(S,\cO_S)=0\text{.}\)
    We use the following specialization of homological projective duality: for every smooth Pfaffian cubic \(X\) with complementary section \(S\) as above, there is a Fourier–Mukai equivalence
    \begin{equation*} \Ku(X)\simeq D^b(S). \end{equation*}
    This is [Kuz10, Theorem 3.1]. The general theorem constructs the equivalence from complementary linear sections of the Grassmannian and the Pfaffian. In this dimension the entire category of the ordinary K3 section occurs, so the kernel is untwisted on \(S\times X\text{.}\) For a comparison with the geometric construction from a family of quartic scrolls, see [Huy23, Chapter 7, Section 3.2].

    2. Which adjoint occurs?

    If \(i^!\) is the right adjoint of the inclusion, then \(S_{\Ku(X)}=i^!S_{D^b(X)}i\text{.}\) The left adjoint occurs in the formula for the inverse. Interchanging them would give an incorrect Serre calculation.

    5. Two sources of K3 surfaces.

    For a plane, the family of lines in a chosen ruling can have a Brauer obstruction. For the smooth Pfaffian section, the equivalence comes from the derived category of the Grassmannian section itself. The two constructions explain why a twist occurs in one description and an ordinary category in the other.
