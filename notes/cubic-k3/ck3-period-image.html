    Chapter 5 The image of the period map

    Torelli determines a cubic fourfold from its period. The image theorem answers a different question: which polarized integral Hodge structures are periods of smooth cubic fourfolds? Two arithmetic arrangements of hyperplanes are excluded. Their defining vectors have squares two and six, but the second condition includes a divisibility requirement. We compute the corresponding saturated labels, explain their geometric limits, and describe the compactification argument that proves there are no further exclusions.

    Section 5.1 Two arrangements in the period domain

    Use the lattice and component fixed in Chapter 4. Thus \(L\) is the odd unimodular middle cohomology lattice of signature \((21,2)\text{,}\) and \(c\) is the characteristic vector corresponding to \(h^2\text{,}\) with \(c^2=3\text{.}\) The abstract primitive lattice \(L_0=c^\perp\) is even, has signature \((20,2)\text{,}\) and has discriminant group of order three. The chosen component \(\mathcal D^+\) of the period domain consists of isotropic lines \(\mathbb C\omega\subset L_0\otimes\mathbb C\) satisfying \((\omega,\overline\omega)\lt0\text{.}\) The group \(\Gamma\) is the restriction to \(L_0\) of the isometries of \(L\) fixing \(c\) and preserving that component.
    For a primitive vector \(\delta\in L_0\text{,}\) its divisibility is the positive generator of the ideal of its integral pairings:
    \begin{gather*} \operatorname{div}_{L_0}(\delta)\mathbb Z=\{(\delta,x):x\in L_0\}. \end{gather*}
    A root will mean a vector of square two. A long root will mean a vector of square six and divisibility three. These conventions use the cubic cup product. They are the conventions used by Laza; after reversing the pairing, the corresponding squares would be minus two and minus six.
    For a positive vector \(\delta\text{,}\) let \(H_\delta\) be the hyperplane section of the period domain defined by \((\omega,\delta)=0\text{.}\) Define
    \begin{gather*} \mathcal H_\Delta=\bigcup_{\delta^2=2}H_\delta. \end{gather*}
    The second arrangement is
    \begin{gather*} \mathcal H_\infty=\bigcup_{\substack{\delta^2=6\\\operatorname{div}_{L_0}(\delta)=3}}H_\delta. \end{gather*}
    Both are locally finite, \(\Gamma\)-invariant arithmetic arrangements. Each has one orbit of hyperplanes, so its image in the quotient is an irreducible divisor [Laz10, Definition 2.13 and Lemma 2.14].

    Theorem 5.1.

    The period map induces an isomorphism of quasi-projective varieties
    \begin{gather*} \mathcal C\simeq\bigl(\mathcal D^+\setminus(\mathcal H_\Delta\cup\mathcal H_\infty)\bigr)/\Gamma. \end{gather*}
    Equivalently, a period in \(\mathcal D^+\) is realized by a smooth cubic fourfold exactly when its integral primitive Hodge classes contain neither a root nor a long root.
    For an integral primitive vector, orthogonality to the period line is equivalent to being of type \((2,2)\text{:}\) its pairing with the conjugate line vanishes as well. The theorem therefore imposes conditions on the integral Hodge lattice, beyond the Hodge numbers and the signature. The inequalities defining the domain alone do not impose these exclusions.
    Each hyperplane in the two arrangements is nonempty and has complex dimension nineteen. To see this, a period can be regarded as an oriented negative definite real two-plane, spanned by the real and imaginary parts of \(\omega\text{.}\) The orthogonal complement of a positive vector has signature \((19,2)\text{,}\) so it still contains such planes. The root becomes an extra positive Hodge direction. Its exclusion is a geometric restriction on cubic periods; positivity of the Hodge form would allow it. Both discriminants consequently define divisors in the larger arithmetic period space even though neither gives a smooth Hassett divisor.

    Section 5.2 Why the discriminants are six and two

    The discriminant of the saturated lattice generated with \(c\) is not the square of its primitive orthogonal vector. For a root of square two, the orthogonal sum \(\mathbb Zc\oplus\mathbb Z\delta\) has Gram matrix
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}. \end{align*}
    Its determinant is six. If its saturation had index \(r\text{,}\) the saturated determinant would be \(6/r^2\text{,}\) an integer. No integer \(r\gt1\) has square dividing six. The lattice is already saturated, and the hyperplane has label discriminant six.
    A long root gives an orthogonal sum of determinant eighteen, but that sum is not saturated. Since \(L\) is unimodular and \(c\) is primitive, the inclusion \(\mathbb Zc\oplus L_0\subset L\) has index three. Its gluing identifies the order-three discriminant groups of its summands. The class of \(\delta/3\) generates the discriminant group of \(L_0\text{.}\) After changing the sign of \(\delta\text{,}\) the gluing gives an integral vector
    \begin{gather*} u=\frac{c+\delta}{3}\in L. \end{gather*}
    Its square is one and \((c,u)=1\text{.}\) The lattice generated by \(c,u\) has matrix
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}. \end{align*}
    Its determinant is two, so it is saturated by the same index argument. It contains the original sum with index three. This is the discriminant-two hyperplane [Laz10, Proposition 2.15 and Lemma 2.17].
    The converse calculation is equally useful. In a saturated label with the last matrix, the vector \(3u-c\) is orthogonal to \(c\) and has square six. Its pairings with \(L_0\) are divisible by three. Since the discriminant group of \(L_0\) has order three, its divisibility is exactly three. A discriminant-six label in the first normal form contains a vector of square two. Thus the two descriptions by roots and by saturated labels agree.
    The discriminant group explains the possible divisibilities without a choice of coordinates. If a primitive vector has divisibility \(m\text{,}\) then \(\delta/m\) belongs to the dual lattice and its class has order \(m\) in \(L_0^*/L_0\text{.}\) Primitiveness is needed for this order assertion. It follows that \(m\) divides three. Since divisibility also divides the square of the vector, a vector of square two has divisibility one. A vector of square six allows both possibilities, and the example below distinguishes them. Replacing the integral lattice by its rational quadratic space would lose precisely this distinction.
    The divisibility requirement cannot be removed. Choose a hyperbolic summand \(U\subset L_0\) with isotropic basis \(e,f\) and \((e,f)=1\text{.}\) Set \(\delta=e+3f\text{.}\) Its square is six, but \((\delta,f)=1\text{,}\) so its divisibility is one. The lattice \(\mathbb Zc\oplus\mathbb Z\delta\) has determinant eighteen. Any proper saturation would have index three and would require \(\delta/3\text{,}\) up to sign, to pair integrally with \(L_0\text{.}\) Its pairing with \(f\) is \(1/3\text{,}\) excluding that possibility. This vector defines a discriminant-eighteen hyperplane, whose general period is allowed by the image theorem. The twisted K3 example for discriminant eighteen in Chapter 7 is compatible with this calculation.2

    Section 5.3 Nodes, vanishing cycles, and the ADE extension

    An ordinary double point explains the first exclusion. Its local smoothing has equation \(z_0^2+\cdots+z_4^2=t\text{.}\) The vanishing cycle is a real four-sphere, whose cohomology class \(\delta\) has square two. Picard–Lefschetz monodromy acts on middle cohomology by
    \begin{gather*} T(x)=x-(x,\delta)\delta. \end{gather*}
    This reflection fixes \(\delta^\perp\text{,}\) sends \(\delta\) to \(-\delta\text{,}\) and has order two. After the base change \(t=s^2\text{,}\) the monodromy is trivial. The limiting period is orthogonal to the vanishing cycle, so it belongs to \(\mathcal H_\Delta\text{.}\)
    The K3 geometry can be seen by projecting a general cubic with one node \(p=[1:0:\cdots:0]\text{.}\) Its equation is
    \begin{gather*} x_0q_2(x_1,\ldots,x_5)+q_3(x_1,\ldots,x_5)=0. \end{gather*}
    Projection from \(p\) is birational to \(\mathbb P^4\text{.}\) The directions of lines contained in the cubic through \(p\) form the complete intersection \(S=\{q_2=q_3=0\}\subset\mathbb P^4\text{.}\) For a general one-nodal cubic, this is a smooth degree-six K3 surface: adjunction gives trivial canonical bundle, and the complete-intersection sequence gives \(H^1(S,\mathcal O_S)=0\text{.}\) Its primitive cohomology supplies the nonalgebraic part of the limiting cubic Hodge structure [Has00, Section 4.2 and Proposition 4.2.1].
    The same surface explains a birational model for the singular Fano variety of lines. Two general points of \(S\) give two lines through the node. Their span is a plane whose cubic intersection contains those lines and a residual third line. Conversely, a general line away from the node spans a plane with the node; the residual plane conic has a double point at the node and splits into two lines over \(\mathbb C\text{.}\) These constructions are inverse on open subsets, giving a birational correspondence with \(S^{[2]}\text{.}\) This concerns the Fano variety of the singular cubic, whose resolution is discussed in [Has00, Lemma 6.3.1].
    At an isolated ADE singularity, the vanishing cycles form a positive definite root lattice in this cup convention. The local monodromy group is finite and generated by the associated reflections. Killing finite monodromy permits the period map to extend. Write \(\mathcal C^{\mathrm{ADE}}\) for the moduli space of cubics with at worst isolated simple singularities, including smooth cubics. These cubics are GIT stable [Laz09, Theorem 1.1]. The extended map is an open embedding with image
    \begin{gather*} \mathcal C^{\mathrm{ADE}}\simeq(\mathcal D^+\setminus\mathcal H_\infty)/\Gamma. \end{gather*}
    The extension and its image are proved in [Laz10, Proposition 3.2 and Theorem 1.1] and [Loo09, Theorem 4.1].
    Allowing ADE singularities fills the arrangement \(\mathcal H_\Delta\) outside \(\mathcal H_\infty\text{.}\) A general point of that divisor corresponds to a one-nodal cubic. Intersections of root hyperplanes can describe several nodes or other ADE singularities. The extension includes all simple singularities, not only nodes.3

    Section 5.4 The Veronese degeneration and a degree-two K3 surface

    The second arrangement has a different geometric limit. Regard \(\mathbb P^5\) as the projective space of symmetric three-by-three matrices. The determinant equation defines a cubic fourfold \(X_0\text{.}\) Its singular locus is the rank-one locus, the Veronese surface \(V=\nu_2(\mathbb P^2)\text{.}\) Every matrix of rank at most two lies on a secant or tangent line to this surface, so \(X_0\) is its secant variety. This cubic has a two-dimensional singular locus and is outside the ADE extension.
    Consider a pencil \(\det(M)+tG(M)=0\) for which the general fiber is smooth and \(C=V\cap\{G=0\}\) is smooth. The restriction of \(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^5}(1)\) to \(V\simeq\mathbb P^2\) is \(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^2}(2)\text{.}\) Thus \(G|_V\) is a plane sextic. Its double cover \(S\to\mathbb P^2\) is a K3 surface with a polarization of square two. Semistable reduction after a quadratic base change introduces a quadric surface bundle over \(V\text{,}\) with discriminant \(C\text{.}\) Its two rulings explain the same double cover.
    For these pencils, the limiting Hodge structure is pure and contains a label \(K_2\) of discriminant two. Hassett identifies its orthogonal complement over \(\mathbb C\) by an isomorphism of Hodge structures
    \begin{gather*} K_2^\perp\otimes\mathbb C\simeq H^2(S,\mathbb C)_{\mathrm{prim}}(-1) \end{gather*}
    [Has00, Theorem 4.4.1]. The limiting period therefore lies on \(\mathcal H_\infty/\Gamma\text{.}\) The integral gluing calculation above explains why the orthogonal primitive vector has square six and divisibility three.
    The central determinantal cubic does not determine one limiting period. Varying \(G|_V\) varies the double-plane K3 surface. Blowing up its point in the GIT moduli space records these smoothing directions; the resulting exceptional divisor maps birationally to the discriminant-two divisor. This gives a description at general points, where the sextic is smooth. This general-point argument does not treat degenerate sextics or the unigonal degree-two K3 locus; the full proof treats them separately [Laz10, Sections 4.1–4.2].4

    Section 5.5 Why surjectivity requires control of degenerations

    Local Torelli and global injectivity make the smooth period map an open embedding. They do not imply that every period avoiding the two arrangements occurs. An open image could omit further points. The missing argument is properness over the proposed image after ADE cubics are added: a family whose periods converge inside \((\mathcal D^+\setminus\mathcal H_\infty)/\Gamma\) must admit an ADE limit.
    GIT supplies a projective compactification. Semistable replacement, after finite base change and a change of projective coordinates, gives a central semistable cubic with closed orbit representing the limiting moduli point. The period argument must determine whether this cubic has simple singularities. Laza analyzes the singularities of closed orbits and compares their degenerations with their limiting mixed Hodge structures. In his classification, Type I is the ADE case. For Types II and III, the monodromy has a nontrivial unipotent part, which leads to the boundary of the Baily–Borel compactification. Type IV includes the Veronese secant degeneration and a curve of further indeterminacies. An interior limiting period forces the monodromy to be finite. Laza’s Type IV theorem then places that period on \(\mathcal H_\infty/\Gamma\text{.}\)
    These alternatives exclude every non-ADE central limit when the limiting period stays in the proposed open target. The mixed Hodge arguments, the treatment of the indeterminacy locus, and the properness conclusion occur in [Laz10, Theorems 3.1 and 4.11, and Section 5.1]. Properness makes the extended image closed in that target. It is open and nonempty, and the target is connected, so it is the whole target. Removing the discriminant of singular ADE cubics gives the smooth image theorem.
    Connectedness here is compatible with deleting the arrangement. The domain component is a connected complex manifold; paths can be perturbed to avoid a locally finite collection of complex hyperplanes, each of real codimension two. Passing to the arithmetic quotient preserves connectedness. Properness over this open space is therefore enough for the open-and-closed argument. One does not need every singular cubic to define an interior period. The classification instead determines which limits reach that open space and excludes all others from the properness test.
    Looijenga proves the result through arithmetic arrangements and automorphic forms, including an independent proof of injectivity. The GIT compactification is the arrangement compactification associated with \(\mathcal H_\infty\) [Loo09, Theorem 4.1]. Laza obtains the same compactification comparison [Laz10, Theorem 1.2]. This arrangement compactification is a birational modification of the Baily–Borel model. Suitable blow-ups, or a common resolution, separate the smoothing directions and retain their degree-two K3 data. The GIT and Baily–Borel models are obtained by different contractions. In particular, directions contracted to the Veronese point on the GIT side can have different interior periods on the arithmetic side.5

    Section 5.6 Hassett divisors and the scope of the exclusions

    The label calculations place Hassett’s nonemptiness criterion in the period domain. A positive saturated rank-two lattice containing \(c\) has discriminant congruent to zero or two modulo six. Its additional class defines a period hyperplane. Lattice embeddings and the arithmetic group determine the hyperplanes of a given discriminant up to one orbit. For discriminants two and six, these are exactly the two excluded arrangements. Their inverse images in the moduli space of smooth cubics are empty.
    For \(d\gt6\) with \(d\equiv0,2\pmod6\text{,}\) the rank-two label admits the required primitive embedding, and its hyperplane is not one of the excluded hyperplanes. A general point avoids their intersections. The image theorem realizes that point by a smooth cubic, and the orbit statement gives the irreducible divisor \(\mathcal C_d\text{.}\) This explains how the period description recovers the existence and irreducibility statement of Theorem 3.1. Hassett proved that statement earlier by deformation arguments [Has00, Theorems 3.2.3 and 4.3.1]; the later image theorem determines the whole complement.
    For a proposed algebraic lattice of larger rank, checking one allowed discriminant is insufficient. Every integral primitive class in that lattice must avoid the two root conditions. A larger lattice containing an allowed label can still contain a discriminant-two or discriminant-six sublabel, preventing realization by a smooth cubic. The image theorem tests the whole lattice of Hodge classes.6
