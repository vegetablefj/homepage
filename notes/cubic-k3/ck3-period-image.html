    Chapter 4 Image of the period map

    Torelli determines a cubic fourfold from its period. The image theorem asks which periods in the cubic domain occur. Two arithmetic arrangements are excluded: one is defined by vectors of square two, the other by vectors of square six and divisibility three. We compute their saturated rank-two sublattices and describe geometric limits on them. The final section outlines the degeneration argument, identifying the classification and compactification theorems that it uses.

    Section 4.1 Two arrangements in the period domain

    Use the lattice and component fixed in Chapter 3. Thus \(L\) is the odd unimodular middle cohomology lattice of signature \((21,2)\text{,}\) and \(c\) is the characteristic vector corresponding to \(h^2\text{,}\) with \(c^2=3\text{.}\) The abstract primitive lattice \(L_0=c^\perp\) is even, has signature \((20,2)\text{,}\) and has discriminant group of order three. The chosen component \(\mathcal D^+\) of the period domain consists of isotropic lines \(\mathbb C\omega\subset L_0\otimes\mathbb C\) satisfying \((\omega,\overline\omega)\lt0\text{.}\) The group \(\Gamma\) is the restriction to \(L_0\) of the isometries of \(L\) fixing \(c\) and preserving that component.
    In the cubic cup-product convention, a root is a vector \(\delta\in L_0\) with \(\delta^2=2\text{.}\) A long root has \(\delta^2=6\) and \(\operatorname{div}_{L_0}(\delta)=3\text{.}\) These are Laza’s conventions [Laz10, Definition 2.16]. Both kinds of vector are primitive. After reversing the pairing, the squares would instead be minus two and minus six.
    For a positive vector \(\delta\text{,}\) let \(H_\delta\) be the hyperplane section of the period domain defined by \((\omega,\delta)=0\text{.}\) Define
    \begin{gather*} \mathcal H_\Delta=\bigcup_{\delta^2=2}H_\delta. \end{gather*}
    The second arrangement is
    \begin{gather*} \mathcal H_\infty=\bigcup_{\substack{\delta^2=6\\\operatorname{div}_{L_0}(\delta)=3}}H_\delta. \end{gather*}
    Both are locally finite, \(\Gamma\)-invariant arithmetic arrangements. Each has one orbit of hyperplanes, so its image in the quotient is an irreducible divisor [Laz10, Definition 2.13 and Lemma 2.14].

    The smooth image theorem is due independently to Laza and Looijenga, extending the exclusions studied by Voisin and Hassett [Laz10, Theorem 1.1] and [Loo09, Theorem 4.1].

    Theorem 4.1.

    The period map induces an isomorphism of quasi-projective varieties
    \begin{gather*} \mathcal C\simeq\bigl(\mathcal D^+\setminus(\mathcal H_\Delta\cup\mathcal H_\infty)\bigr)/\Gamma. \end{gather*}
    Equivalently, a period in \(\mathcal D^+\) is realized by a smooth cubic fourfold exactly when its integral primitive Hodge classes contain neither a root nor a long root.
    For an integral primitive vector, orthogonality to the period line is equivalent to being of type \((2,2)\text{:}\) its pairing with the conjugate line vanishes as well. The theorem therefore imposes conditions on the integral Hodge lattice, beyond the Hodge numbers and the signature. The inequalities defining the domain alone do not impose these exclusions.
    The criterion tests every primitive Hodge class, including when the integral Hodge lattice has rank greater than two. Containing one positive saturated rank-two sublattice of an allowed discriminant is insufficient: further classes may include a root or a long root. This whole-lattice restriction is an immediate consequence of Theorem 4.1.
    Each hyperplane in the two arrangements is nonempty and has complex dimension nineteen. To see this, a period can be regarded as an oriented negative definite real two-plane, spanned by the real and imaginary parts of \(\omega\text{.}\) The orthogonal complement of a positive vector has signature \((19,2)\text{,}\) so it still contains such planes. The root becomes an extra positive Hodge direction. Its exclusion is a geometric restriction on cubic periods; positivity of the Hodge form would allow it. Both arrangements consequently give divisors in the larger arithmetic period space, although no smooth cubic has a period on them.

    Section 4.2 Why the discriminants are six and two

    Fix the cubic pair \((L,c)\) and a vector \(\delta\in L_0\text{.}\) We examine the saturation \(M_\delta=(\mathbb Qc+\mathbb Q\delta)\cap L\text{.}\) For a root of square two, the orthogonal sum \(\mathbb Zc\oplus\mathbb Z\delta\) has Gram matrix
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}. \end{align*}
    Its determinant is six. If its saturation had index \(r\text{,}\) then \(\det M_\delta=6/r^2\) would be an integer. Thus \(r=1\text{,}\) and \(M_\delta\) has discriminant six.
    A long root gives an orthogonal sum of determinant eighteen. For this cubic pair, its saturation has index three: after choosing the sign of \(\delta\text{,}\) the vector
    \begin{gather*} u=(c+\delta)/3 \end{gather*}
    belongs to \(L\) [Laz10, Lemma 2.17]. Its square is one and \((c,u)=1\text{.}\) In the basis \(c,u\text{,}\) the saturated lattice has matrix
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}. \end{align*}
    Its determinant is two, and no further integral saturation is possible. Thus the long-root hyperplane corresponds to a saturated rank-two sublattice of discriminant two.
    Conversely, a saturated rank-two sublattice containing \(c\) with the last matrix contains \(\delta=3u-c\text{,}\) of square six and orthogonal to \(c\text{.}\) Its pairings with \(L_0\) are divisible by three. It is primitive; since \(A_{L_0}\simeq\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\text{,}\) its divisibility is exactly three. The first matrix contains a vector of square two. These are the two rank-two configurations in the image theorem [Laz10, Proposition 2.15 and Lemma 2.17].
    The divisibility requirement cannot be removed. Choose a hyperbolic summand \(U\subset L_0\) with isotropic basis \(e,f\) and \((e,f)=1\text{.}\) Let \(\delta=e+3f\text{.}\) Its square is six, but \((\delta,f)=1\text{,}\) so its divisibility is one. The rank-two orthogonal sum with \(c\) has determinant eighteen. A proper saturation would have index three and would require a vector \((ac+b\delta)/3\) with at least one coefficient nonzero modulo three. Pairing with \(f\) forces \(3\mid b\text{;}\) integrality of its square then forces \(3\mid a\text{.}\) Hence it is already saturated. A very general period on this hyperplane is allowed by the image theorem: its only primitive integral Hodge direction is generated by \(\delta\text{,}\) which is neither a root nor a long root.2

    Section 4.3 Nodes, vanishing cycles, and the ADE extension

    An ordinary double point explains the first exclusion. Its local smoothing has equation \(z_0^2+\cdots+z_4^2=t\text{.}\) The vanishing cycle is a real four-sphere, whose cohomology class \(\delta\) has square two. Picard–Lefschetz monodromy acts on middle cohomology by
    \begin{gather*} T(x)=x-(x,\delta)\delta. \end{gather*}
    This reflection fixes \(\delta^\perp\text{,}\) sends \(\delta\) to \(-\delta\text{,}\) and has order two. After the base change \(t=s^2\text{,}\) the monodromy is trivial. The limiting period is orthogonal to the vanishing cycle, so it belongs to \(\mathcal H_\Delta\text{.}\)
    The K3 geometry can be seen by projecting a general cubic with one node \(p=[1:0:\cdots:0]\text{.}\) Its equation is
    \begin{gather*} x_0q_2(x_1,\ldots,x_5)+q_3(x_1,\ldots,x_5)=0. \end{gather*}
    Projection from \(p\) is birational to \(\mathbb P^4\text{.}\) The directions of lines contained in the cubic through \(p\) form the complete intersection \(S=\{q_2=q_3=0\}\subset\mathbb P^4\text{.}\) For a general one-nodal cubic, this is a smooth degree-six K3 surface: adjunction gives trivial canonical bundle, and the complete-intersection sequence gives \(H^1(S,\mathcal O_S)=0\text{.}\) Its primitive cohomology supplies the nonalgebraic part of the limiting cubic Hodge structure [Has00, Section 4.2 and Proposition 4.2.1].
    The same surface explains a birational model for the singular Fano variety of lines. Two general points of \(S\) give two lines through the node. Their span is a plane whose cubic intersection contains those lines and a residual third line. Conversely, a general line away from the node spans a plane with the node; the residual plane conic has a double point at the node and splits into two lines over \(\mathbb C\text{.}\) These constructions are inverse on open subsets, giving a birational correspondence with \(S^{[2]}\text{.}\) This concerns the Fano variety of the singular cubic, whose resolution is discussed in [Has00, Lemma 6.3.1].
    We now use an extension theorem. Let \(\mathcal C^{\mathrm{ADE}}\) be the coarse moduli space of complex cubic fourfolds with at worst isolated simple singularities, including smooth cubics. Such cubics are GIT stable [Laz09, Theorem 1.1]. Their local vanishing-cycle monodromy is a finite Weyl group. After local finite base change, the smooth-fibre periods extend; descending to the arithmetic quotient defines a period map on \(\mathcal C^{\mathrm{ADE}}\) [Laz10, Proposition 3.2]. The stronger conclusion, which includes injectivity and surjectivity, is
    \begin{gather*} \mathcal C^{\mathrm{ADE}}\simeq(\mathcal D^+\setminus\mathcal H_\infty)/\Gamma. \end{gather*}
    This is an external theorem of Laza and Looijenga [Laz10, Theorem 1.1], [Loo09, Theorem 4.1]. The final section outlines the global degeneration argument needed for this conclusion. Huybrechts places this extension in the period-map comparison in [Huy23, Chapter 6, Sections 6.4–6.5].
    Allowing ADE singularities fills the arrangement \(\mathcal H_\Delta\) outside \(\mathcal H_\infty\text{.}\) A general point of that divisor corresponds to a one-nodal cubic. Intersections of root hyperplanes can describe several nodes or other ADE singularities. The extension includes all simple singularities, not only nodes.3

    Section 4.4 The Veronese degeneration and a degree-two K3 surface

    The second arrangement has a different geometric limit. Regard \(\mathbb P^5\) as the projective space of symmetric three-by-three matrices. The determinant equation defines a cubic fourfold \(X_0\text{.}\) Its singular locus is the rank-one locus, the Veronese surface \(V=\nu_2(\mathbb P^2)\text{.}\) Every matrix of rank at most two lies on a secant or tangent line to this surface, so \(X_0\) is its secant variety. This cubic has a two-dimensional singular locus and is outside the ADE extension.
    Consider a pencil \(\det(M)+tG(M)=0\) for which the general fiber is smooth and \(C=V\cap\{G=0\}\) is smooth. The restriction of \(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^5}(1)\) to \(V\simeq\mathbb P^2\) is \(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^2}(2)\text{.}\) Thus \(G|_V\) is a plane sextic. Its double cover \(S\to\mathbb P^2\) is a K3 surface with a polarization of square two. Semistable reduction after a quadratic base change introduces a quadric surface bundle over \(V\text{,}\) with discriminant \(C\text{.}\) Its two rulings explain the same double cover.
    For such a pencil, after the finite base change in semistable reduction, Hassett proves that the limiting Hodge structure is pure and contains a saturated rank-two sublattice \(K_2\subset L\) containing \(c\) and of discriminant two. Its orthogonal complement satisfies an isomorphism of complex Hodge structures
    \begin{gather*} K_2^\perp\otimes\mathbb C\simeq H^2(S,\mathbb C)_{\mathrm{prim}}(-1). \end{gather*}
    These are the conclusions of [Has00, Theorem 4.4.1]; the semistable model and the limiting-Hodge calculation are external inputs here. The rank-two computation above then places the limiting period on \(\mathcal H_\infty/\Gamma\text{.}\) The complex Hodge comparison alone would not establish its integral divisibility-three condition.
    The central determinantal cubic does not determine one limiting period. Varying \(G|_V\) varies the double-plane K3 surface. Blowing up its point in the GIT moduli space records these smoothing directions; the resulting exceptional divisor maps birationally to the discriminant-two divisor. This gives a description at general points, where the sextic is smooth. This general-point argument does not treat degenerate sextics or the unigonal degree-two K3 locus; the full proof treats them separately [Laz10, Sections 4.1–4.2].4

    Section 4.5 Why surjectivity requires control of degenerations

    Let \(Y=(\mathcal D^+\setminus\mathcal H_\infty)/\Gamma\text{.}\) By [Laz10, Proposition 3.2], the period map extends to a regular morphism \(\mathcal C^{\mathrm{ADE}}\to\mathcal D^+/\Gamma\text{.}\) We restrict this morphism over \(Y\text{.}\) Laza proves surjectivity there by properness. The argument below outlines the use of the projective GIT compactification, semistable replacement, and the comparison of limiting cubics with their monodromy and periods [Laz10, Theorems 3.1 and 4.11, and Section 5.1].
    The arithmetic quotient \(\mathcal D^+/\Gamma\) is irreducible, and \(Y\) is a nonempty Zariski open subset [Laz10, Section 2.2]. Local Torelli gives a nonempty analytic open subset in the image of the smooth cubic locus. A proper Zariski-closed subset of \(Y\) cannot contain such an analytic open subset, so the smooth period image is Zariski dense in \(Y\text{.}\) This is the dominance underlying the birational period map with which Laza begins [Laz10, Section 5.1].
    Take the closure of the graph of the smooth period map in the product of the projective GIT compactification with \(Y\text{.}\) Its projection to \(Y\) is proper. Curve selection reduces the study of its boundary points to one-parameter families of smooth cubics over a punctured disk whose periods tend to points of \(Y\text{.}\) After finite base change and a change of coordinates, semistable replacement gives a central cubic whose closed orbit represents the GIT limit. An interior limiting period implies finite monodromy, by the Hodge-theoretic extension result used in [Laz10, Section 5.1, pp. 701–702].
    Laza’s classification then leaves only an ADE limit. Type I consists of cubics with simple singularities. For Types II and III, the monodromy has a nontrivial unipotent part and the limiting period lies on the Baily–Borel boundary [Laz10, Theorem 3.1]. The Type IV closed orbits form an indeterminacy curve containing the Veronese secant point [Laz10, Theorem 2.6]. For a Type IV limit with finite monodromy, the limiting period belongs to \(\mathcal H_\infty/\Gamma\) [Laz10, Theorem 4.11]. Both alternatives are excluded by the chosen point of \(Y\text{.}\) Every such graph limit therefore has an ADE cubic model. Regularity of the extension on the ADE locus and separatedness of the period space identify the graph closure over that locus with the graph of the extended map. This proves properness over \(Y\text{,}\) as in [Laz10, Section 5.1].
    The image of the extended map over \(Y\) is closed by properness and contains the Zariski dense image of the smooth locus. It is therefore all of \(Y\text{.}\) Proposition 3.2 places the periods of singular ADE cubics in \(\mathcal H_\Delta/\Gamma\text{,}\) while smooth cubics avoid that divisor. Thus every point of \(Y\setminus(\mathcal H_\Delta/\Gamma)\) comes from a smooth cubic, proving the smooth image assertion.
    The compactification theorem makes the boundary comparison precise. The GIT compactification is isomorphic to the Looijenga arrangement compactification associated with \(\mathcal H_\infty\text{,}\) by [Laz10, Theorem 1.2] and [Loo09, Theorem 4.1]. We use the arrangement construction through this compactification theorem. It modifies the Baily–Borel model along the arrangement before contracting in a different direction. On the GIT side the Veronese cubic is one point, whereas its smoothing directions can carry different degree-two K3 periods. The modification is what allows these descriptions to fit the same compactification theorem. For the comparison with cubic moduli, see also [Huy23, Chapter 6, Sections 6.5 and 6.7].5
