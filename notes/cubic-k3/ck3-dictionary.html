    Chapter 7 Mukai lattice and derived equivalences

    The integral Mukai lattice relates the Kuznetsov component to the middle cohomology of the cubic. We first construct its Hodge structure and the distinguished \(A_2\text{.}\) We then state the stability and moduli theorems used to construct K3 surfaces, explain the obstruction to an untwisted universal family, and compare the resulting existence conditions with Hassett’s discriminants. The final sections describe the Fano variety and the LLSvS eightfold as moduli spaces and prove a categorical Torelli criterion using rotation.

    Section 7.1 Topological K-theory and the Hodge structure

    Use the Euler pairing on complex topological K-theory \(K^0_{\mathrm{top}}(X)\text{,}\) defined by Riemann–Roch. Set
    \begin{equation*} \widetilde H(\Ku(X),\Z)=\{\kappa\in K^0_{\mathrm{top}}(X):\chi(\cO_X(i),\kappa)=0\text{ for }i=0,1,2\}. \end{equation*}
    The pairing in these notes is \((\kappa,\eta)=-\chi(\kappa,\eta)\text{.}\) On the component it is symmetric. For K3 Mukai vectors it is \((r,c,s)\cdot(r',c',s')=c\cdot c'-rs'-r's\text{.}\)
    The cohomology of \(X\) has no torsion and no odd part, and its total even rank is twenty-seven. The spectral sequence for topological K-theory therefore gives a free group of that rank. The three exceptional classes split off three summands, leaving rank twenty-four. Addington and Thomas identify the resulting integral lattice, with our sign, as
    \begin{equation*} \widetilde H(\Ku(X),\Z)\simeq U^{\oplus4}\oplus E_8(-1)^{\oplus2}. \end{equation*}
    In particular it is even unimodular of signature \((4,20)\text{.}\) We use their construction of this lattice and its cohomological comparisons [AT14, Sections 2.2–2.4]. Their pairing is the Euler pairing, so their lattice forms have the opposite sign.
    1
    The Mukai vector gives the complexified lattice a weight-two Hodge structure: the inverse images of \(H^{3,1}(X)\) and \(H^{1,3}(X)\) are its \((2,0)\) and \((0,2)\) lines, and their orthogonal complement has type \((1,1)\text{.}\) Set
    \begin{equation*} N_X=\widetilde H(\Ku(X),\Z)\cap\widetilde H^{1,1}(\Ku(X)). \end{equation*}
    This is the image of algebraic K-theory and is isomorphic to \(K_{\mathrm{num}}(\Ku(X))\) with pairing \(-\chi\text{.}\) The integral equality, not merely its rational version, is [AT14, Proposition 2.4]; it is also proved through moduli spaces in [BLMNPS21, Theorem 29.2]. We write \(\mathbf v:K_0(\Ku(X))\to N_X\) for the numerical Mukai-vector map.
    For an untwisted projective K3 surface \(S\text{,}\) the degree-zero and degree-four classes span an algebraic copy of \(U\) in its Mukai lattice. For a twisted pair \((S,\alpha)\text{,}\) choose a rational B-field lift \(B\) of \(\alpha\text{.}\) One description keeps \(\widetilde H(S,\Z)\) as the underlying integral lattice and replaces its period line by \(e^BH^{2,0}(S)\text{.}\) The point class stays algebraic and isotropic. The choice of \(B\) is an auxiliary description of the twisted Mukai Hodge structure; it is not additional data in the pair \((S,\alpha)\) [Huy17, Sections 2.1 and 3.2].

    Section 7.2 The distinguished \(A_2\) and cubic cohomology

    For a line \(\ell\subset X\text{,}\) let \(\lambda_1=[\operatorname{pr}(\cO_\ell(1))]\) and \(\lambda_2=[\operatorname{pr}(\cO_\ell(2))]\text{.}\) Their topological K-classes are independent of the line. The mutation triangle gives \([L_E(F)]=[F]-\chi(E,F)[E]\text{,}\) so the classes are
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_1=[\cO_\ell(1)]-[\cO_X(1)]+4[\cO_X] \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_2=[\cO_\ell(2)]-[\cO_X(2)]+4[\cO_X(1)]-6[\cO_X]. \end{equation*}
    Their independence follows from the equality of the cohomological classes of lines and the torsion-free topological K-group.
    The two classes generate a primitive positive definite \(A_2\subset N_X\text{,}\) with Gram matrix
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Their determinant three excludes a proper integral overlattice. The Mukai vector restricts to an integral Hodge isometry
    \begin{equation*} A_2^\perp\simeq H^4(X,\Z)_{\mathrm{prim}}(1). \end{equation*}
    On the right the pairing is the negative of the cubic cup product. This gives signature \((2,20)\) on both sides [AT14, Proposition 2.3].
    2
    With \(A_X=H^4(X,\Z)\cap H^{2,2}(X)\text{,}\) one has \(\operatorname{rk}N_X=\operatorname{rk}A_X+1\text{.}\) The comparison can be made on quotients: the second Chern class induces an isomorphism of abelian groups
    \begin{equation*} \overline c_2:\widetilde H(\Ku(X),\Z)/A_2\xrightarrow{\sim}H^4(X,\Z)/\Z h^2. \end{equation*}
    It maps algebraic classes onto Hodge classes [AT14, Proposition 2.4]. Although \(c_2\) is not additive before taking the quotient, its cross term is a multiple of \(h^2\text{,}\) which makes this map additive.
    A saturated rank-two label \(K\subset A_X\) containing \(h^2\) corresponds through this quotient map to a saturated rank-three sublattice \(M\subset N_X\) containing \(A_2\text{.}\) The corresponding orthogonals are isometric with the stated sign, and \(|\operatorname{disc}M|=\operatorname{disc}K\text{.}\) These assertions include saturation and nondegeneracy, as in [AT14, Propositions 2.3–2.5]. For a very general point of \(\mathcal C_d\text{,}\) these are the full algebraic lattices. Their ranks increase at intersections of divisors.
    For a cubic outside all Hassett divisors, \(N_X=A_2\text{.}\) It has no nonzero isotropic vector. The realization theorem below will therefore exclude every twisted projective K3 realization of that category.

    Section 7.3 Stability conditions and the moduli theorem

    Fix a nonzero \(v\in N_X\text{,}\) a numerical Bridgeland stability condition \(\sigma=(Z,\mathcal P)\) on \(\Ku(X)\) with \(Z:N_X\to\C\text{,}\) and a real lift \(\phi\) with \(Z(v)\in\R_{>0}e^{i\pi\phi}\text{.}\) The notation \(M_\sigma(v,\phi)\) denotes the coarse moduli space of stable objects \(E\in\mathcal P(\phi)\) with \(\mathbf v(E)=v\text{,}\) when the moduli theorem supplies it. We suppress \(\phi\) in \(M_\sigma(v)\) after fixing this choice. For primitive \(v\text{,}\) the condition that \(\sigma\) is \(v\)-generic means that all semistable objects of this class and phase are stable. Such stability conditions exist in the component used in the theorem.3

    Theorem 7.1.

    Let \(X\) be a smooth complex cubic fourfold. There is a distinguished nonempty connected component \(\operatorname{Stab}^{\dagger}(\Ku(X))\) of numerical stability conditions. Let \(v\in N_X\) be nonzero and primitive with \(v^2\geq0\text{,}\) let \(\sigma\) be \(v\)-generic in that component, and fix a compatible phase lift. Then \(M_\sigma(v)\) is nonempty, smooth, projective, and irreducible holomorphic symplectic, of dimension \(v^2+2\text{.}\) It is deformation equivalent to a Hilbert scheme of points on a K3 surface. Its second cohomology has an integral Hodge isometry with \(v^\perp\) if \(v^2>0\text{,}\) and with \(v^\perp/\Z v\) if \(v^2=0\text{.}\) The orthogonal complement here is taken in \(\widetilde H(\Ku(X),\Z)\text{.}\)
    The existence theorem is [BLMS23, Theorem 1.2]. The component and moduli statements, including the Hodge isometries, are [BLMNPS21, Theorems 29.1–29.2]. We invoke this combined result with exactly the primitive-vector and genericity hypotheses above. Its proof constructs stability in families and deforms the moduli problem to a twisted K3 surface.
    For a stable object \(E\text{,}\) simplicity gives \(\operatorname{Hom}(E,E)=\C\text{.}\) The slicing gives vanishing of its negative self-Ext groups, and Serre duality gives \(\operatorname{Ext}^2(E,E)=\C\) and vanishing above degree two. The identity \(\chi(E,E)=-v^2\) therefore yields
    \begin{equation*} \dim\operatorname{Ext}^1(E,E)=v^2+2. \end{equation*}
    This is the tangent dimension of the constructed moduli space. Its alternating form is Yoneda composition followed by trace,
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Ext}^1(E,E)\otimes\operatorname{Ext}^1(E,E)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Ext}^2(E,E)\longrightarrow\C. \end{equation*}
    Serre duality makes the form nondegenerate; the moduli theorem also supplies smoothness and closedness.
    4

    Section 7.4 Isotropic moduli and derived equivalences

    When \(v\) in the moduli theorem is isotropic, the moduli space is a projective K3 surface \(S\text{.}\) The universal objects on the moduli stack have scalar automorphisms. Their descent to \(S\times X\) may therefore give a twisted universal kernel. For the right-module convention used here, a kernel from \(D^b(S,\alpha)\) to \(\Ku(X)\) has source twist \(\alpha^{-1}\text{.}\)

    Proposition 7.2.

    For \(X,v,\sigma,\phi\) as in Theorem 7.1 with \(v^2=0\text{,}\) the universal construction gives a Brauer class \(\alpha\in\operatorname{Br}(S)\) and an equivalence \(D^b(S,\alpha)\simeq\Ku(X)\text{.}\) For this construction \(\alpha\) is trivial if and only if there is \(w\in N_X\) with \((v,w)=1\text{.}\) This is the cubic specialization of [BLMNPS21, Proposition 33.1 and Lemma 33.2].
    The orthogonality calculation explains one part of the equivalence argument. Distinct stable objects \(E,F\) of this class in the fixed category \(\mathcal P(\phi)\) have \(\operatorname{Hom}(E,F)=\operatorname{Hom}(F,E)=0\text{.}\) Their negative Ext groups vanish by the slicing. Duality eliminates degrees at least two, and \(\chi(E,F)=-(v,v)=0\) eliminates degree one. Thus they are mutually orthogonal, as are point sheaves at different points of a surface. The self-Ext dimensions are one, two, and one. The cited universal-kernel theorem supplies the full faithfulness and essential surjectivity that this calculation alone would not establish.

    Theorem 7.3.

    For every smooth complex cubic fourfold \(X\text{,}\) there is an equivalence \(\Ku(X)\simeq D^b(S)\) for some smooth projective K3 surface \(S\) if and only if \(N_X\) contains \(U\text{.}\) There is an equivalence \(\Ku(X)\simeq D^b(S,\alpha)\) for some smooth projective K3 surface and Brauer class if and only if \(N_X\) contains a nonzero isotropic vector.

    Proof.
    For necessity, a K3 equivalence induces an integral Mukai Hodge isometry [Huy17, Proposition 3.3]. A point class gives a primitive algebraic isotropic vector; in the untwisted case the degree-zero and degree-four classes give \(U\text{.}\) Conversely, divide a nonzero isotropic vector by its integral common factor and choose a generic stability condition in the distinguished component. The moduli theorem and the universal-kernel proposition give a twisted realization. If \(N_X\) contains \(U\text{,}\) an isotropic generator pairs to one with the other generator, so the proposition gives a trivial twist. These are [BLMNPS21, Corollary 29.7 and Proposition 33.1].
    Here \(N_X\) is even and nondegenerate: if \(\rho=\operatorname{rk}A_X\text{,}\) the Addington–Thomas comparison gives its signature \((2,\rho-1)\) [AT14, Proposition 2.5]. For a chosen primitive isotropic \(v\in N_X\text{,}\) extension to a copy of \(U\) is equivalent to \(\operatorname{div}_{N_X}(v)=1\text{.}\) Indeed, if \((v,w)=1\text{,}\) evenness makes \(w-(w^2/2)v\) isotropic with the same pairing. More generally, in an even nondegenerate lattice the existence of a nonzero isotropic vector is equivalent to an embedding of \(U(n)\) for some positive \(n\text{,}\) without a primitiveness requirement. To see sufficiency of an isotropic vector, choose \(w\) with \((v,w)=n\ne0\) and write \(w^2=2a\text{;}\) then \(nw-av\) is isotropic and pairs with \(v\) to \(n^2\text{.}\) Applied to the Mukai Hodge structure, these are the two embedding conditions in [Huy17, Lemma 2.6].
    For a specified pair \((S,\alpha)\text{,}\) Huybrechts’s updated formulation says that an equivalence with \(\Ku(X)\) exists exactly when the corresponding integral Mukai Hodge structures are isometric [Huy25, Theorem 5.1 and Remark 5.2]. Earlier deformation arguments proved the categorical converse on dense open subsets of the relevant divisors; the moduli results give the stated conclusion for every smooth \(X\text{.}\)
    On a projective K3 surface, regard the Brauer class as a homomorphism \(\alpha:T_S\to\Q/\Z\) and set \(T(S,\alpha)=\ker\alpha\text{.}\) If its order is \(n\text{,}\) then \([T_S:T(S,\alpha)]=n\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} |\operatorname{disc}T(S,\alpha)|=n^2|\operatorname{disc}T_S|. \end{equation*}
    For a very general cubic in \(\mathcal C_d\) with this realization, the transcendental rank is twenty-one, so \(S\) has Picard rank one. If its primitive polarization has square \(e\text{,}\) the integral comparison gives \(d=n^2e\) [Huy17, Lemma 2.13 and Section 3.2]. The very general plane example has \(e=2\) and a Clifford class of order two, hence \(d=8\text{.}\) Passing to rational Hodge structures loses this index.

    Section 7.5 Discriminants and Eisenstein norms

    The lattice criteria have arithmetic forms. A nonempty Hassett divisor has discriminant satisfying \((*)\text{:}\) the integer \(d\) is greater than six and congruent to zero or two modulo six.
    Among such integers, the untwisted condition \((**)\) requires that \(d\) be divisible by neither four nor nine, and by no odd prime congruent to two modulo three.
    The twisted condition \((**')\) requires that \(v_p(d/2)\) be even for every prime \(p\equiv2\pmod3\text{.}\) The prime two is included in this condition.
    The lattice-to-discriminant theorems used here have the following scope. For a smooth cubic \(X\text{,}\) existence of \(U\subset N_X\) is equivalent to membership in at least one \(\mathcal C_d\) satisfying \((*)\) and \((**)\text{.}\) Existence of a nonzero isotropic vector is equivalent to membership in at least one such divisor satisfying \((*)\) and \((**')\text{.}\) These are integral embedding results, not conclusions from a norm calculation alone [AT14, Theorem 3.1] and [Huy17, Lemma 2.6 and Proposition 2.10].

    The norm calculation.

    The form of \(A_2\) gives
    \begin{equation*} \frac12(a\lambda_1+b\lambda_2)^2=a^2-ab+b^2=N(a+b\zeta), \end{equation*}
    where \(\zeta\) is a primitive cube root of unity. The ring \(\Z[\zeta]\) is Euclidean for this norm: round the two real coordinates in the basis \(1,\zeta\text{.}\) The norm of the error is at most \(3/4<1\text{,}\) which proves Euclidean division and unique factorization.
    For \(p\ne3\text{,}\) the polynomial \(t^2+t+1\) has a root over \(\mathbb F_p\) exactly when \(\mathbb F_p^*\) contains an element of order three. Thus primes congruent to two modulo three are inert and have norm \(p^2\text{,}\) while primes congruent to one split into conjugate primes of norm \(p\text{.}\) In the split case the corresponding prime ideals have index \(p\) and are principal by Euclideanity. The prime three is ramified, with \(N(1-\zeta)=3\text{.}\)

    Proposition 7.4.

    For a positive integer \(m\text{,}\) a representation \(m=a^2-ab+b^2\) with integers \(a,b\) exists if and only if \(v_p(m)\) is even at every prime \(p\equiv2\pmod3\text{.}\) A representation with \(\gcd(a,b)=1\) exists if and only if no such prime divides \(m\) and \(9\nmid m\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    In the norm of an Eisenstein integer, an inert prime has even exponent because conjugation fixes it. A split prime permits any exponent by using one conjugate factor, and powers of \(1-\zeta\) give every exponent at three. These choices prove both necessity and sufficiency for the first assertion.
    For the primitive assertion, an inert prime dividing a norm forces the Eisenstein integer to vanish in \(\mathbb F_{p^2}\text{,}\) so it divides both coefficients. If nine divides the norm, reduction modulo three gives \(b\equiv-a\text{.}\) Substitution \(b=-a+3k\) gives \(a^2-ab+b^2\equiv3a^2\pmod9\text{;}\) hence three divides both coefficients. Conversely, use only split primes and at most one factor of \(1-\zeta\text{.}\) At each split prime choose the full required power of one conjugate prime, without the other. No rational prime then divides both coefficients. This gives the required primitive representation.
    For \(m=d/2\text{,}\) the first norm criterion is exactly \((**')\text{.}\) For even \(d\text{,}\) the primitive criterion excludes four and nine from \(d\text{,}\) together with all odd primes congruent to two modulo three, and is exactly \((**)\text{.}\) The separate condition \((*)\) is still required for a smooth cubic label.
    Now take the rank-three lattice \(M\subset N_X\) corresponding to a label of discriminant \(d\text{,}\) as in Section 7.2. It has signature \((2,1)\) and contains the distinguished \(A_2\text{.}\) Choose an integral generator \(w\) of its orthogonal direction, and let \(r=[M:A_2\oplus\Z w]\text{.}\) The equality \(|\operatorname{disc}M|=d\) gives \(-w^2=r^2d/3\text{.}\) A vector \(a\lambda_1+b\lambda_2+cw\) with rational coefficients is isotropic exactly when
    \begin{equation*} N(a+b\zeta)=a^2-ab+b^2=\frac{r^2d}{6}c^2. \end{equation*}
    A nonzero isotropic vector has \(c\ne0\text{,}\) since \(A_2\) is positive definite. Using \(N(1-\zeta)=3\text{,}\) we obtain
    \begin{equation*} N\left(\frac{(1-\zeta)(a+b\zeta)}{rc}\right)=\frac{d}{2}. \end{equation*}
    Conversely, if \(N(\eta)=d/2\) with \(\eta\in\Q(\zeta)\text{,}\) take \(c=1\) and \(a+b\zeta=r\eta/(1-\zeta)\text{.}\) This gives a nonzero rational isotropic vector.
    For the positive integer \(m=d/2\text{,}\) being a rational norm is equivalent to being an integral norm. Indeed, write \(\eta=u/t\) with \(u\in\Z[\zeta]\) and \(t\in\Z_{>0}\text{.}\) At an inert prime, \(v_p(N(u))\) is even, so \(v_p(N(\eta))=v_p(N(u))-2v_p(t)\) is even. Conversely, the parity condition gives an integral norm by Proposition 7.4. Thus rational isotropy of this rank-three lattice gives exactly \((**')\text{.}\) The integral embedding theorem in the lattice-to-discriminant criteria supplies the passage to saturated labels and to the full algebraic Mukai lattice.
    The conditions give the following values for small discriminants.
    For \(d=8\text{,}\) the exponent of two in \(d/2\) is even, so a twisted realization exists throughout the divisor. Its very general member has no untwisted realization. For \(d=12\) or \(20\text{,}\) an odd exponent at an inert prime excludes the twisted condition. For \(d=18\text{,}\) the ramified prime three is allowed by \((**')\) but its square prevents primitiveness. For \(d=50\text{,}\) the inert prime five occurs to an even exponent, but every norm representation has a common factor. For \(d=14\text{,}\) the primitive representation \(7=3^2-3\cdot1+1^2\) agrees with the untwisted Pfaffian example.5

    Section 7.6 Lines and twisted cubics as moduli spaces

    For a line \(\ell\subset X\text{,}\) write \(I_\ell=I_{\ell/X}\text{.}\) Serre duality and the sequence for \(I_\ell(-2)\) give
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Ext}^2(I_\ell,\cO_X(-1))\simeq H^2(X,I_\ell(-2))^\vee\simeq\C. \end{equation*}
    Choose a nonzero class \(\varepsilon_\ell:I_\ell\to\cO_X(-1)[2]\) and define \(P_\ell=\operatorname{Cone}(\varepsilon_\ell)[-1]\text{.}\) It fits into
    \begin{equation*} \cO_X(-1)[1]\longrightarrow P_\ell\longrightarrow I_\ell\xrightarrow{\varepsilon_\ell}\cO_X(-1)[2]. \end{equation*}
    Nonzero scalar multiples give isomorphic cones. This is the line object used by Li, Pertusi, and Zhao [LPZ23a, Section 4, p. 635].
    The triangle gives \(H^*(X,P_\ell)=H^*(X,P_\ell(-1))=0\text{.}\) After twisting by \((-2)\text{,}\) its only possible cohomology is eliminated by the connecting map
    \begin{equation*} H^2(X,I_\ell(-2))\longrightarrow H^4(X,\cO_X(-3)). \end{equation*}
    Both spaces are one-dimensional, and the map is the Serre-duality pairing with the nonzero class \(\varepsilon_\ell\text{,}\) so it is an isomorphism. Hence \(H^*(X,P_\ell(-2))=0\) as well, proving \(P_\ell\in\Ku(X)\text{.}\) Its ordinary cohomology sheaves are \(\mathcal H^{-1}(P_\ell)=\cO_X(-1)\) and \(\mathcal H^0(P_\ell)=I_\ell\text{.}\)
    Additivity in the defining triangle gives \([P_\ell]=[I_\ell]-[\cO_X(-1)]\text{.}\) Since \(\operatorname{ch}(\cO_\ell)=h^3/3-h^4/6\text{,}\) this yields
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{ch}(P_\ell)=h-\frac{h^2}{2}-\frac{h^3}{6}+\frac{h^4}{8}. \end{equation*}
    Substitution in the class formulas of Section 7.2 gives \(\mathbf v(P_\ell)=\lambda_1+\lambda_2\text{.}\) In contrast, the rank-three sheaf \(F_\ell\) constructed in Section 6.1 has \(\mathbf v(F_\ell)=\lambda_1\text{,}\) because \(\operatorname{pr}(\cO_\ell(1))\simeq F_\ell[2]\text{.}\) These are two different objects attached to the same line.

    Theorem 7.6.

    For every smooth complex cubic fourfold \(X\text{,}\) there is a stability condition \(\sigma\in\operatorname{Stab}^{\dagger}(\Ku(X))\) for which the objects \(P_\ell\) are stable of one phase \(\phi\text{,}\) and
    \begin{equation*} F(X)\simeq M_\sigma(\lambda_1+\lambda_2,\phi). \end{equation*}
    Every stable object in this moduli space is isomorphic to \(P_\ell\) for a line \(\ell\subset X\text{.}\)
    This is [LPZ23a, Theorem 1.1 and Section 4]. It includes cubics containing planes. The vector has square two, so the dimension is four.
    Twisting the defining triangle by \(\cO_X(1)\) and applying \(L_{\cO_X}\) kills its first term and gives
    \begin{equation*} R(P_\ell)\simeq L_{\cO_X}(I_\ell(1))\simeq F_\ell[1]. \end{equation*}
    Hence \(R[-1]\) sends \(P_\ell\) to \(F_\ell\text{.}\) Transporting the stability condition by this equivalence gives a moduli description of \(F(X)\) with Mukai vector \(\lambda_1\text{.}\)
    If \(\Ku(X)\simeq D^b(S,\alpha)\text{,}\) transporting the objects and the stability condition describes \(F(X)\) as a moduli space of stable objects on that twisted K3 surface. Addington proves that \(F(X)\) is birational to a moduli space of stable sheaves on an ordinary K3 surface if and only if \(X\) carries a label satisfying \((*)\) and \((**)\) [Add16, Theorem 1].
    The variety \(F(X)\) is birational to \(S^{[2]}\) for some K3 surface if and only if there is a labelling of discriminant \(d\) satisfying \((*)\) and integers \(a\ne0,n\) with
    \begin{equation*} da^2=2(n^2+n+1). \end{equation*}
    This is [Add16, Theorem 2]. The condition comes from an isotropic class pairing to one with the Mukai vector that defines the fourfold, rather than merely the existence of some copy of \(U\) in \(N_X\text{.}\) An isomorphism to a Hilbert square further requires agreement of ample cones.

    Example 7.7.

    For \(d=14\text{,}\) the equation holds with \(a=1\) and \(n=2\text{.}\) Discriminant \(74=2\cdot37\) satisfies \((**)\text{,}\) but the equation has no integral solution [Add16, p. 2 and Table 1]. For a very general \(X\in\mathcal C_{74}\text{,}\) the lattice \(A_X\) has rank two, so its only label has discriminant seventy-four. Hence \(F(X)\) is birational to a moduli space of stable sheaves on a K3 surface, but is not birational to the Hilbert square of any K3 surface.
    Suppose \(X\) contains no plane, and let \(M_3(X)\) be the closure in \(\operatorname{Hilb}^{3t+1}(X)\) of the locus of smooth rational degree-three curves spanning a projective three-space; its points parametrize generalized twisted cubics. Lehn, Lehn, Sorger, and van Straten obtain a smooth projective irreducible holomorphic symplectic eightfold \(Z(X)\) from \(M_3(X)\) by a \(\mathbb P^2\)-fibration followed by a divisorial contraction [LLSvS17, Theorems A–B].
    Li, Pertusi, and Zhao identify this eightfold with a moduli space in \(\Ku(X)\text{:}\) for an appropriate \(\sigma\in\operatorname{Stab}^{\dagger}(\Ku(X))\) and a compatible phase lift \(\phi\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} Z(X)\simeq M_\sigma(2\lambda_1+\lambda_2,\phi). \end{equation*}
    This is [LPZ23a, Theorem 1.2]. Since \((2\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^2=8-4+2=6\text{,}\) the tangent-space calculation gives \(\dim Z(X)=8\text{.}\)

    Section 7.7 Rotation and categorical Torelli

    The category attached to a cubic carries its particular rotation functor \(R_X\text{.}\) On its Mukai lattice, the induced isometry \(R_X^H\) cyclically permutes \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,-\lambda_1-\lambda_2\) and is the identity on \(A_2^\perp\) [Huy17, Proposition 3.12]. Thus the sum of the nontrivial eigenspaces is \(A_2\otimes\C\text{.}\) The rotation here is the geometric functor constructed in Section 6.2, rather than an unspecified autoequivalence with the same third power.
    For two smooth complex cubic fourfolds, every complex-linear fully faithful exact functor between their Kuznetsov components is of Fourier–Mukai type [LPZ23b, Theorem 1.3]. For components, this means that the functor composed with the source projection and target inclusion has a kernel on the product of the cubics. Such an equivalence induces an integral Hodge isometry of the entire Mukai lattices, restricting to its numerical action on \(N_X\) [Huy17, Proposition 3.4].

    Theorem 7.8.

    Let \(X,X'\) be smooth complex cubic fourfolds. They are isomorphic if and only if there is a complex-linear exact equivalence \(\Phi:\Ku(X)\xrightarrow{\sim}\Ku(X')\) whose induced numerical isometry \(\Phi^N:N_X\xrightarrow{\sim}N_{X'}\) satisfies
    \begin{equation*} \Phi^N\circ R_X^H|_{N_X}=R_{X'}^H|_{N_{X'}}\circ\Phi^N. \end{equation*}

    Proof.
    A projective isomorphism induces an equivalence commuting with rotation, so gives the condition. Conversely, representability supplies an integral Mukai Hodge isometry \(\widetilde\Phi^H\) extending \(\Phi^N\text{.}\) The commutation on \(N_X\) preserves the sum of the two nontrivial rotation eigenspaces. Since \(A_2\) is primitive and contained in \(N_X\text{,}\) it is the intersection of that complex span with the integral Mukai lattice. Therefore \(\widetilde\Phi^H\) sends the distinguished \(A_2\) onto the distinguished \(A_2\) for \(X'\text{.}\) Its restriction to their orthogonals gives an integral Hodge isometry \(H^4(X,\Z)_{\mathrm{prim}}\simeq H^4(X',\Z)_{\mathrm{prim}}\text{.}\) The classical Torelli theorem in Chapter 3 implies \(X\simeq X'\text{.}\) As explained there, an overall sign may be needed to make this primitive isometry extend to the full middle lattice fixing the hyperplane square. The existence of an isomorphism does not assert that the initially given primitive map is itself that pullback.
    For the functorial formulation and its relation to the moduli description of \(F(X)\text{,}\) see [PS23, Theorem 7.7 and Remark 7.14].
    Huybrechts and Rennemo give a reconstruction by the Jacobian ring. Under an equivalence of Fourier–Mukai type together with an isomorphism between the natural kernels of \(\Phi\circ R_X\) and \(R_{X'}\circ\Phi\text{,}\) their graded algebra of morphisms between rotation kernels recovers an isomorphism of Jacobian rings and hence an isomorphism of cubics [HR19, Corollary 3.7, Proposition 3.9, and Corollary 3.10]. The Jacobian ring also governed infinitesimal periods in Chapter 1; here its full graded multiplication determines the cubic equation.

    Section 7.8 Space of stability conditions

    The distinguished component in Theorem 7.1 also has a description by central charges. Use the Mukai pairing to write \(Z(v)=(\Omega,v)\text{,}\) with \(\Omega\in N_X\otimes\C\text{.}\) Let \(\mathcal P_0^+(N_X)\) be the component containing these central charges in the set where \(\operatorname{Re}\Omega,\operatorname{Im}\Omega\) span a positive definite two-plane and \((\Omega,\delta)\ne0\) for every \(\delta\in N_X\) with \(\delta^2=-2\text{.}\) Then the central-charge map is a covering
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Stab}^{\dagger}(\Ku(X))\longrightarrow\mathcal P_0^+(N_X) \end{equation*}
    by [BLMNPS21, Theorem 29.1]. For fixed primitive \(v\) with \(v^2\geq0\text{,}\) the spaces \(M_\sigma(v,\phi)\) are instead the moduli spaces of stable objects from Theorem 7.1, with the stated genericity and phase hypotheses.
    When \(N_X=A_2\text{,}\) Feyzbakhsh and Pertusi prove that any two numerical stability conditions differ by the action of \(\widetilde{\operatorname{GL}}_2^+(\R)\) [FP23, Corollary 3.9]. Thus, for any \(\sigma\in\operatorname{Stab}^{\dagger}(\Ku(X))\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Stab}(\Ku(X))=\operatorname{Stab}^{\dagger}(\Ku(X))=\sigma\cdot\widetilde{\operatorname{GL}}_2^+(\R). \end{equation*}
    Their hypothesis is exactly the absence of additional algebraic Mukai classes, which holds outside the Hassett divisors. Since \(A_2\) is positive definite, the central-charge domain is the space of bases of \(A_2\otimes\R\) with the orientation selected by \(\sigma\text{.}\) The group action changes the central charge and relabels phases while preserving the stable objects. Consequently the line objects \(P_\ell\) give the moduli description of \(F(X)\) for every stability condition in this case, after transporting the phase lift.
    For a projective K3 surface \(S\) of Picard rank one, Bayer and Bridgeland prove that \(\operatorname{Stab}^{\dagger}(D^b(S))\) is contractible and is preserved by every exact autoequivalence [BB17, Theorem 1.3]. Their theorem concerns the union of the autoequivalence translates of this component and proves that this union is the component itself.

    Section 7.9 Autoequivalences and Fourier–Mukai partners

    Write \(\operatorname{Aut}(\Ku(X))\) for the group of complex-linear exact autoequivalences, modulo isomorphism of functors. By [LPZ23b, Theorem 1.3], these are all of Fourier–Mukai type. Their action on the Mukai lattice gives
    \begin{equation*} \rho:\operatorname{Aut}(\Ku(X))\longrightarrow O_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(\widetilde H(\Ku(X),\mathbb Z)). \end{equation*}
    The kernel consists of cohomologically trivial autoequivalences. The symplectic subgroup \(\operatorname{Aut}_s(\Ku(X))\) consists of those acting as the identity on \(\widetilde H^{2,0}(\Ku(X))\) [Huy17, Section 3.4].
    For a fixed stability condition \(\sigma\text{,}\) let \(\operatorname{Aut}(\Ku(X),\sigma)\) be the subgroup satisfying \(\Phi\sigma=\sigma\text{,}\) including equality of the slicings. Its symplectic subgroup is the intersection with \(\operatorname{Aut}_s(\Ku(X))\text{.}\) Commuting with rotation instead means an isomorphism \(\Phi R_X\simeq R_X\Phi\text{.}\) This implies the numerical commutation used in Theorem 7.8. Fixing a stability condition and commuting with rotation impose different conditions.6
    For a very general smooth cubic fourfold, Huybrechts computes
    \begin{gather*} \operatorname{Aut}_s(\Ku(X))=\langle R_X\rangle\simeq\mathbb Z,\\ \operatorname{Aut}(\Ku(X))=\langle R_X[-1]\rangle\simeq\mathbb Z \end{gather*}
    [Huy17, Theorem 1.2 and Corollary 3.13]. Since \(R_X^3\simeq[2]\text{,}\) the even shifts have index three in the symplectic group. This computation uses a very general point of the full moduli space.
    For every smooth cubic, Ouchi recovers its automorphism group using the stability conditions \(\sigma_\alpha\) for \(0<\alpha<1/4\) constructed in [Ouc21, Theorem 3.8]. Pushforward induces
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Aut}(X)\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{Aut}(\Ku(X),\sigma_\alpha). \end{equation*}
    The isomorphism restricts to the symplectic subgroups [Ouc21, Theorem 6.1]. Here an automorphism of \(X\) is symplectic when it acts trivially on \(H^{3,1}(X)\text{.}\) Thus a specified stability condition recovers geometric automorphisms from categorical ones.
    A Fourier–Mukai partner of \(X\) is a smooth cubic fourfold \(Y\) with a complex-linear exact equivalence \(\Ku(X)\simeq\Ku(Y)\text{;}\) partners are counted up to isomorphism of cubic fourfolds. Rotation compatibility is not required. Huybrechts proves that there are finitely many, and that a nonspecial cubic, with \(A_X=\mathbb Z h^2\text{,}\) has only itself as a partner [Huy17, Theorems 1.1 and 1.5(i)]. An equivalence preserves the Mukai Hodge structure, but need not carry the distinguished \(A_2\) onto itself. This explains why forgetting rotation allows nonisomorphic cubics.
    Special cubics can have many partners. Böhning, Graf von Bothmer, and Marquand prove that a very general cubic with a symplectic involution has exactly \(1120\) nontrivial partners, all birational to it [BvBM26, Theorem 1.2 and Section 5.2]. Their examples also show that admitting a symplectic automorphism is not preserved by Fourier–Mukai partnership [BvBM26, Corollary 1.4].
    For a very general \(X\in\mathcal C_{44}\text{,}\) Brooke and Marquand construct its unique nontrivial Fourier–Mukai partner \(Y\text{,}\) prove that \(X\) and \(Y\) are birational, and show that \(F(X)\simeq F(Y)\) [BM26, Theorems 1.1, 1.5, and 1.7]. The birational map is obtained from quintics double along a degree-ten Enriques surface contained in \(X\text{.}\) Thus the isomorphism of the varieties of lines need not preserve their Plücker polarizations.
    More generally, if \(X\) is very general in a nonempty \(\mathcal C_d\) and \(Y\) is a smooth cubic fourfold, birationality of \(F(X)\) and \(F(Y)\) implies \(\Ku(X)\simeq\Ku(Y)\) [BM26, Corollary 6.2]. The reverse implication fails: Brooke, Frei, and Marquand show that a very general \(X\in\mathcal C_{546}\) has a Fourier–Mukai partner \(Y\) whose variety of lines is not birational to \(F(X)\) [BFM24, Theorem 6.1].
