    Chapter 7 The Mukai lattice and K3 realizations

    The Mukai lattice extends the primitive cohomology of a cubic fourfold by a positive definite lattice of rank two. Its algebraic part distinguishes three possibilities for the Kuznetsov component: an untwisted K3 derived category, a twisted K3 derived category, or a K3 category with no surface realization. The distinction is integral. A hyperbolic plane gives the untwisted case, while a nonzero isotropic vector gives the twisted case. Moduli spaces of stable objects explain why these lattice conditions produce surfaces and why their Brauer classes occur.

    Section 7.1 Topological K-theory and the pairing

    For objects of \(D^b(X)\text{,}\) the Euler pairing is
    \begin{equation*} \chi(E,F)=\sum_k(-1)^k\dim\operatorname{Ext}^k(E,F). \end{equation*}
    Riemann–Roch extends it to topological K-theory. Write \(K^0_{\mathrm{top}}(X)\) for the group of complex topological vector bundles, with the usual relations, and set
    \begin{equation*} \widetilde H(\Ku(X),\Z)=\{\kappa\in K^0_{\mathrm{top}}(X):\chi(\cO_X(i),\kappa)=0\text{ for }i=0,1,2\}. \end{equation*}
    We use the Mukai pairing \((\kappa,\eta)=-\chi(\kappa,\eta)\text{.}\) The sign agrees with the pairing \(c\cdot c'-rs'-r's\) on Mukai vectors \((r,c,s)\) of K3 surfaces.
    The even cohomology of \(X\) has total rank twenty-seven and has no torsion, while its odd cohomology vanishes. The spectral sequence for topological K-theory gives a free group of the same rank. The three exceptional classes split off three summands, leaving rank twenty-four. With the pairing just defined, this is an even unimodular lattice
    \begin{equation*} \widetilde H(\Ku(X),\Z)\simeq U^{\oplus4}\oplus E_8(-1)^{\oplus2} \end{equation*}
    of signature \((4,20)\text{.}\) Addington and Thomas construct its integral relation with cubic cohomology [AT14, Sections 2.3–2.4]. Their Euler pairing has the opposite sign to our Mukai pairing.
    1
    The Mukai vector gives this lattice a weight-two Hodge structure. Its \((2,0)\) line is the inverse image of \(H^{3,1}(X)\text{,}\) its \((0,2)\) line is the inverse image of \(H^{1,3}(X)\text{,}\) and the remaining part has type \((1,1)\text{.}\) Define the algebraic Mukai lattice by
    \begin{equation*} N_X=\widetilde H(\Ku(X),\Z)\cap\widetilde H^{1,1}(\Ku(X)). \end{equation*}
    It equals the image of algebraic K-theory, or equivalently the numerical Grothendieck group with pairing \(-\chi\text{.}\) The integral equality follows from the integral Hodge theorem for cubic fourfolds; it is also a conclusion of [BLMNPS21, Theorem 29.2].
    For a K3 surface \(S\text{,}\) the same construction is the ordinary Mukai lattice \(H^0(S,\Z)\oplus H^2(S,\Z)\oplus H^4(S,\Z)\text{.}\) The Mukai vector of a sheaf is \((r,c_1,r+\operatorname{ch}_2)\text{.}\) Its degree-zero and degree-four classes are algebraic and span a copy of \(U\text{.}\) For a twisted K3 surface, a rational B-field lift \(B\) of the Brauer class changes the period line to \(e^BH^{2,0}(S)\text{.}\) One can describe the twisted Hodge structure on the same underlying integral Mukai lattice. The point class remains algebraic and isotropic, but an algebraic copy of \(U\) need not exist.

    Section 7.2 The distinguished \(A_2\) and primitive cohomology

    Let \(\ell\subset X\) be a line. Project its classes to the component and define \(\lambda_1=[\operatorname{pr}(\cO_\ell(1))]\) and \(\lambda_2=[\operatorname{pr}(\cO_\ell(2))]\text{.}\) These are topological K-classes independent of the chosen line. The mutation triangle gives \([L_E(F)]=[F]-\chi(E,F)[E]\text{,}\) so these classes can be computed without constructing the complexes. Explicitly,
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_1=[\cO_\ell(1)]-[\cO_X(1)]+4[\cO_X], \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_2=[\cO_\ell(2)]-[\cO_X(2)]+4[\cO_X(1)]-6[\cO_X]. \end{equation*}
    Their Mukai pairing has matrix
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    They generate a primitive positive definite lattice \(A_2\subset N_X\text{.}\) Its determinant is three, so a proper integral overlattice would have index whose square divides three, which is impossible. The orthogonal complement has an integral Hodge isometry
    \begin{equation*} A_2^\perp\simeq H^4(X,\Z)_{\mathrm{prim}}(1). \end{equation*}
    On the right, the pairing is the negative of the cubic cup pairing. This convention makes its signature \((2,20)\text{,}\) the signature of the complement of \(A_2\) in the Mukai lattice [AT14, Proposition 2.3].
    2
    Let \(A_X=H^4(X,\Z)\cap H^{2,2}(X)\text{.}\) An additional integral Hodge class in primitive cohomology gives an additional algebraic Mukai class, and \(\operatorname{rk}N_X=\operatorname{rk}A_X+1\text{.}\) For a cubic outside the special divisors, \(A_X=\Z h^2\) and \(N_X=A_2\text{.}\) A positive definite lattice has no nonzero isotropic vector. This already prevents that component from being the derived category of any twisted projective K3 surface.
    A saturated rank-two labelling \(K\subset A_X\) containing \(h^2\) corresponds to a saturated rank-three sublattice \(M\subset N_X\) containing \(A_2\text{.}\) Their discriminants have the same absolute value [AT14, Propositions 2.4–2.5]. Thus the labelled discriminant is also visible in the category. At a very general point of \(\mathcal C_d\text{,}\) these are the full algebraic lattices and have ranks two and three. At intersections of divisors, both ranks can be larger.

    Section 7.3 Untwisted and twisted realizations

    Recall that \(U\) has a basis \(e,f\) with \(e^2=f^2=0\) and \((e,f)=1\text{.}\) A nonzero vector of square zero is isotropic. The category has the following characterizations for every smooth complex cubic fourfold \(X\text{.}\)

    Theorem 7.1.

    There exists a smooth projective K3 surface \(S\) with \(\Ku(X)\simeq D^b(S)\) if and only if \(N_X\) contains \(U\text{.}\) There exist a smooth projective K3 surface \(S\) and a Brauer class \(\alpha\) with \(\Ku(X)\simeq D^b(S,\alpha)\) if and only if \(N_X\) contains a nonzero isotropic vector.
    The lattice comparison in the untwisted case is due to Addington and Thomas, and the twisted comparison is due to Huybrechts. The categorical equivalences for all cubics follow from the construction of moduli spaces in [BLMNPS21, Corollary 29.7 and Proposition 33.1]. The moduli construction is explained below. Historically, the converse categorical assertions were proved first on a dense open subset of each relevant Hassett divisor. The current statement has no such restriction; see [Huy25, Theorem 5.1 and Remark 5.2].
    The two lattice conditions differ by divisibility. For a primitive isotropic vector \(v\in N_X\text{,}\) define \(\operatorname{div}_{N_X}(v)\) as the positive generator of \(\{(v,w):w\in N_X\}\subset\Z\text{.}\) If this generator is one, choose \(w\) with \((v,w)=1\text{.}\) Evenness lets us replace it by \(w-(w^2/2)v\text{,}\) which is also isotropic. The resulting pair spans \(U\text{.}\) Conversely, either isotropic generator of a copy of \(U\) has divisibility one.3
    A nonzero isotropic vector is equivalent here to the existence of a sublattice \(U(n)\) for some positive integer \(n\text{.}\) This embedding need not be primitive. Requiring a primitive embedding of \(U(n)\) would change the condition. A copy of \(U\text{,}\) in contrast, is automatically primitive because it is unimodular. The elementary lattice proofs and the role of saturation appear in Appendix A.
    These criteria involve the entire category and some possible surface realization. They do not prescribe a particular K3 surface, polarization, or Brauer class. Even if one realization has a nontrivial twist, another realization can be untwisted when \(N_X\) contains a different isotropic vector of divisibility one.
    The transcendental lattice gives another interpretation of the twist. For a projective K3 surface, a Brauer class can be viewed as a homomorphism \(\alpha:T_S\to\Q/\Z\text{.}\) Its twisted transcendental lattice is \(T(S,\alpha)=\ker\alpha\text{.}\) If \(\alpha\) has order \(n\text{,}\) this kernel has index \(n\) in \(T_S\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} |\operatorname{disc}T(S,\alpha)|=n^2|\operatorname{disc}T_S|. \end{equation*}
    The rational vector space and its Hodge structure do not change when one passes to a finite-index sublattice. The integral discriminant does.
    Suppose that a very general cubic in \(\mathcal C_d\) has a twisted realization on \((S,\alpha)\text{.}\) Its transcendental rank is twenty-one, so \(S\) has Picard rank one. If its primitive ample class has square \(e\text{,}\) the integral comparison gives \(d=n^2e\text{.}\) For a very general cubic containing a plane, the double-plane K3 has degree two and the Clifford class has order two, giving \(d=8\text{.}\) This explains the index-two transcendental comparison in that example. It also explains why a rational Hodge isomorphism, which does not record an index, cannot distinguish all the integral conditions above [Huy17, Lemma 2.13].

    Section 7.4 Discriminants and Eisenstein norms

    The lattice criteria have arithmetic forms. A nonempty Hassett divisor has discriminant satisfying \((*)\text{:}\) the integer \(d\) is greater than six and congruent to zero or two modulo six.
    Among such integers, the untwisted condition \((**)\) requires that \(d\) be divisible by neither four nor nine, and by no odd prime congruent to two modulo three.
    The twisted condition \((**')\) requires that \(v_p(d/2)\) be even for every prime \(p\equiv2\pmod3\text{.}\) The prime two is included in this condition.
    The untwisted criterion says that \(\Ku(X)\simeq D^b(S)\) for some K3 surface if and only if \(X\in\mathcal C_d\) for at least one \(d\) satisfying \((*)\) and \((**)\text{.}\) The twisted criterion says the same with \(D^b(S,\alpha)\) and \((**')\text{.}\) The first equivalence of lattice conditions is [AT14, Theorem 3.1]. The second is [Huy17, Theorem 1.3 and Proposition 2.10]. Applying Theorem 7.1 gives the categorical statements for every \(X\text{.}\)4
    The form of these conditions comes from \(A_2\text{.}\) In its displayed basis,
    \begin{equation*} \frac12(a\lambda_1+b\lambda_2)^2=a^2-ab+b^2. \end{equation*}
    If \(\zeta\) is a primitive cube root of unity, this is the norm of \(a+b\zeta\) in the Eisenstein integers. The integer \(m=d/2\) is represented by this norm precisely when all prime factors congruent to two modulo three have even exponent. It is represented with \(\gcd(a,b)=1\) precisely when none of those primes divides \(m\) and \(9\nmid m\text{.}\) For even \(d\text{,}\) the primitive condition is exactly \((**)\text{.}\)
    One sees the norm equation in a rank-three lattice containing \(A_2\text{.}\) Over \(\Q\text{,}\) its orthogonal third direction has negative square. Writing an isotropic vector as an \(A_2\) component and a multiple of that direction gives a binary norm equal to a positive coefficient times a square. The index of \(A_2\) plus the third direction in its saturation determines that coefficient. This accounts for the two congruence classes of \(d\) and the factors of three. Passing from this observation to the precise existential criterion in terms of labelled sublattices uses the integral embedding theorems of Addington–Thomas and Huybrechts. The factorization of Eisenstein integers proves the arithmetic assertions; it does not by itself construct a categorical equivalence. See Appendix A.
    The following examples compare the conditions on a discriminant. The last two columns concern the number \(d\text{,}\) with the existential qualification for an individual cubic as just stated.
    For \(d=8\text{,}\) the exponent of two in \(d/2\) is even, so a twisted realization exists throughout the divisor. Its very general member has no untwisted realization. For \(d=12\) or \(20\text{,}\) an odd exponent at an inert prime excludes the twisted condition. For \(d=18\text{,}\) the ramified prime three is allowed by \((**')\) but its square prevents primitiveness. For \(d=50\text{,}\) the inert prime five occurs to an even exponent, but every norm representation has a common factor. For \(d=14\text{,}\) the primitive representation \(7=3^2-3\cdot1+1^2\) agrees with the untwisted Pfaffian example.5

    Section 7.5 Stable objects and the surface they parametrize

    A Bridgeland stability condition specifies a central charge and a notion of phase for objects, with Harder–Narasimhan filtrations and a support property. It permits moduli spaces of stable complexes, not only stable sheaves. Bayer, Lahoz, Macrì, and Stellari construct stability conditions on \(\Ku(X)\) for every smooth cubic fourfold [BLMS23, Theorem 1.2]. There is a distinguished connected component \(\operatorname{Stab}^{\dagger}(\Ku(X))\text{.}\) For a primitive algebraic Mukai vector \(v\text{,}\) a \(v\)-generic stability condition avoids walls where an object of class \(v\) could become strictly semistable.
    For \(v^2\geq0\) and \(\sigma\) in that component generic for \(v\text{,}\) the moduli space \(M_\sigma(v)\) is nonempty, smooth, projective, and irreducible holomorphic symplectic, of dimension \(v^2+2\text{.}\) It is deformation equivalent to a Hilbert scheme of points on a K3 surface [BLMNPS21, Theorem 29.2]. The primitiveness and genericity assumptions are needed for this smooth moduli statement.
    The dimension has a direct explanation. A stable object \(E\) is simple, so \(\operatorname{Hom}(E,E)=\C\text{.}\) Calabi–Yau duality gives \(\operatorname{Ext}^2(E,E)=\C\) and vanishing outside degrees zero, one, and two. Since \(\chi(E,E)=-v^2\text{,}\) its tangent space has dimension
    \begin{equation*} \dim\operatorname{Ext}^1(E,E)=v^2+2. \end{equation*}
    The pairing on the tangent space is Yoneda composition followed by trace,
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Ext}^1(E,E)\otimes\operatorname{Ext}^1(E,E)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Ext}^2(E,E)\longrightarrow\C. \end{equation*}
    Serre duality makes it nondegenerate. The moduli theorem includes smoothness and closedness of the resulting holomorphic two-form; these require deformation theory in addition to the dimension calculation.
    6
    When \(v\) is primitive and isotropic, \(M_\sigma(v)\) is a K3 surface \(S\text{.}\) Stable objects have scalar automorphisms, so a universal object exists on the moduli stack. Descending it to \(S\times X\) may require a Brauer twist. The resulting family defines an equivalence \(D^b(S,\alpha)\simeq\Ku(X)\text{.}\) For this construction the Brauer class can be taken to be zero precisely when some \(w\in N_X\) has \((v,w)=1\) [BLMNPS21, Proposition 33.1 and Lemma 33.2]. Thus the distinction between an isotropic vector and a copy of \(U\) is also the distinction between a possibly twisted universal family and an untwisted one.
    The objects parametrized by distinct points have the orthogonality expected of distinct point sheaves. If \(E\) and \(F\) are nonisomorphic stable objects of this isotropic class, stability gives \(\operatorname{Hom}(E,F)=\operatorname{Hom}(F,E)=0\text{.}\) Serre duality gives \(\operatorname{Ext}^2(E,F)=0\text{.}\) The Euler characteristic is zero, so \(\operatorname{Ext}^1(E,F)=0\) as well. At a single point, the self-Ext dimensions are one, two, and one, as for a point on a smooth surface. The universal family and the deformation map relate these Ext groups to those on \(S\text{.}\) This is why a two-dimensional moduli space can reconstruct a surface derived category. Full faithfulness and essential surjectivity are proved as part of the cited result; the equality of dimensions alone would not prove the equivalence.
    The second cohomology of this surface is \(v^\perp/\Z v\text{,}\) with the Hodge structure and pairing induced by the Mukai lattice. For \(v^2>0\text{,}\) the corresponding formula is \(H^2(M_\sigma(v),\Z)\simeq v^\perp\text{.}\) These formulas explain why the rank-twenty-four lattice controls both categorical equivalences and the cohomology of holomorphic symplectic moduli spaces.

    Section 7.6 The Fano variety as a moduli space

    For every smooth cubic fourfold, the Fano variety of lines has the description
    \begin{equation*} F(X)\simeq M_\sigma(\lambda_1+\lambda_2) \end{equation*}
    for the stability condition constructed and used by Li, Pertusi, and Zhao [LPZ20, Theorem 1.1]. Their theorem associates to each line a stable object of the displayed class and proves that all objects in this moduli space arise in that way. It applies without a hypothesis excluding planes. The vector has square two, so the dimension formula gives four.
    This description exists even when \(\Ku(X)\) has no surface realization. If \(\Ku(X)\simeq D^b(S,\alpha)\text{,}\) transporting the objects and the stability condition describes \(F(X)\) as a moduli space of stable objects on that twisted K3 surface. In the untwisted case, Addington proves that the same condition is equivalent to \(F(X)\) being birational to a moduli space of stable sheaves on an ordinary K3 surface [Add16, Theorem 1]. Moduli of complexes for a transported stability condition and moduli of sheaves are related here by the geometry of stability conditions.7
    In precise arithmetic terms, \(F(X)\) is birational to \(S^{[2]}\) for some K3 surface if and only if there is a labelling of discriminant \(d\) satisfying \((*)\) and integers \(a\ne0,n\) with
    \begin{equation*} da^2=2(n^2+n+1). \end{equation*}
    This is [Add16, Theorem 2]. It implies \((**)\) but is stronger. The condition comes from an isotropic class pairing to one with the Mukai vector that defines the fourfold, rather than merely the existence of some copy of \(U\) in \(N_X\text{.}\)
    The integral Hodge isometry for an associated polarized K3 surface concerns the orthogonal of a chosen labelling. The equivalence of categories concerns the whole Mukai Hodge structure. The Fano variety is a moduli space for a specified vector, and its birational and ample cones depend on that vector and the stability condition. These statements have different data. The Mukai lattice relates them while preserving the distinctions between deformation equivalence, birationality, isomorphism, and equivalence of categories.

    1. The integral group.

    The Mukai vector \(v(\kappa)=\operatorname{ch}(\kappa)\sqrt{\operatorname{td}(X)}\) takes values in rational cohomology. The integral Mukai lattice is defined using topological K-theory. It is not the sum of the integral even cohomology groups with an arbitrarily chosen pairing.

    2. The sign of the form.

    With the Euler pairing, the two classes span \(-A_2\) and their orthogonal has the cubic cup form. We use \(-\chi\text{,}\) so the two signs are reversed. The Tate twist shifts the Hodge types from weight four to weight two.

    3. Primitive is not divisibility one.

    A vector can be primitive as an element of the group \(N_X\) while all of its pairings are divisible by an integer greater than one. Untwisted realization requires some isotropic vector of divisibility one. Twisted realization requires some nonzero isotropic vector, whose primitive generator may have higher divisibility.

    4. Some labelling.

    A cubic can lie in more than one Hassett divisor. Failure of \((**)\) for one labelling does not exclude an untwisted realization coming from another labelling. Statements about the very general member of one divisor can use its rank-three algebraic Mukai lattice.

    5. Checking the prime two.

    Testing only odd primes would incorrectly include \(d=12\) and \(d=20\) in the twisted list. The relevant integer is \(d/2\text{,}\) and its valuation at two must be even.

    6. The moduli theorem.

    For a nonprimitive vector, strictly semistable objects can occur even for a generic stability condition, and the coarse moduli space can be singular. The formula for a stable object’s tangent dimension remains useful, but it does not replace the hypotheses of the smooth projective moduli theorem.

    7. Three different statements.

    Every \(F(X)\) is deformation equivalent to a K3 Hilbert square. Birationality to an actual K3 Hilbert square is more restrictive. An isomorphism to such a Hilbert square requires additional information about ample cones.
