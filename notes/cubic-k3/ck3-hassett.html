    Chapter 5 Special cubic fourfolds and associated K3 surfaces

    The period image is now known. We use it to organize the extra integral Hodge classes of a cubic, determine which discriminants occur, and compare the orthogonal of a label with polarized K3 cohomology. The plane and quartic scroll give the first intersection calculations. The arithmetic classification of the K3 comparison is quoted precisely from Hassett; the geometric role of the K3 period theorem is then explained.

    Section 5.1 Hodge classes and labelled cubics

    For a smooth complex cubic fourfold \(X\text{,}\) recall
    \begin{gather*} A_X=H^4(X,\mathbb Z)\cap H^{2,2}(X). \end{gather*}
    Its orthogonal \(T_X=A_X^\perp\) is taken in full middle cohomology. Every class in \(A_X\) is the class of an integral algebraic cycle of codimension two, by Voisin’s integral Hodge theorem [Voi07, Theorem 18]. It permits the name algebraic lattice and the interpretation of its elements as integral combinations of surface classes. The categorical proof in [BLMNPS21, Corollary 29.8] gives another approach to the same theorem.
    The Hodge–Riemann relations make \(A_X\) positive definite. Indeed, for \(\gamma\in A_X\text{,}\) its decomposition over \(\mathbb Q\) is
    \begin{gather*} \gamma=\frac{(\gamma,h^2)}{3}h^2+\gamma_0, \end{gather*}
    where \(\gamma_0\) is primitive. The two terms are orthogonal and both have positive square when nonzero. If \(r=\operatorname{rank}A_X\text{,}\) then \(T_X\) has rank \(23-r\) and signature \((21-r,2)\text{.}\)

    Definition 5.1.

    A cubic fourfold is special if \(\operatorname{rank}A_X\geq2\text{.}\) A labelled cubic is a pair \((X,K)\text{,}\) where \(K\subset A_X\) is a saturated rank-two sublattice containing \(h^2\text{.}\) Its discriminant is \(d=\det K\text{.}\) An isomorphism of labelled cubics is a projective isomorphism whose pullback sends one specified sublattice to the other.
    The locus \(\mathcal C_d\subset\mathcal C\) consists of the unlabelled cubics \(X\) for which such a sublattice of discriminant \(d\) exists.
    We use \(K_d\) when its discriminant is being emphasized. Specialness is an existence property: the integral Hodge theorem makes it equivalent to the existence of a surface class independent of \(h^2\text{.}\) For example, an integral combination outside \(\mathbb Qh^2\) has at least one surface component outside that line.
    Since \(h^2\) is primitive, choose a basis \(h^2,\gamma\) of \(K\text{.}\) If \(a=(h^2,\gamma)\) and \(b=\gamma^2\text{,}\) its Gram matrix is
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix}3&a\\a&b\end{pmatrix}. \end{align*}
    Thus \(d=3b-a^2\text{.}\) The characteristic property of \(h^2\) gives \(b\equiv a\pmod2\text{,}\) hence \(d\equiv0,2\pmod6\text{.}\) Changing \(\gamma\) by a multiple of \(h^2\text{,}\) and changing its sign if needed, gives
    \begin{align*} K_{6n}=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&2n\end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
    in the first congruence class. In the second class the matrix is
    \begin{align*} K_{6n+2}=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&2n+1\end{pmatrix}. \end{align*}
    Thus positive labels have one of these two normal forms.

    Section 5.2 Which discriminants occur

    Use the abstract pair \((L,c)\text{,}\) the domain component \(\mathcal D^+\text{,}\) and the group \(\Gamma\) fixed in Chapter 3. For a positive saturated rank-two sublattice \(K\subset L\) containing \(c\text{,}\) let \(\mathcal D_K^+\) be the periods orthogonal to \(K\cap L_0\text{.}\) It has dimension nineteen. Two such sublattices are \(\Gamma\)-conjugate exactly when their discriminants agree [Has00, Proposition 3.2.4]. The image of \(\mathcal D_K^+\) in the arithmetic quotient is an irreducible algebraic divisor [Has00, proof of Theorem 3.1.2]. We use these orbit and algebraicity results here.
    The required embeddings exist for every positive \(d\equiv0,2\pmod6\text{.}\) One can see this directly in the model
    \begin{gather*} L\simeq E_8^{\oplus2}\oplus U^{\oplus2}\oplus I_3, \end{gather*}
    with \(c=\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2+\epsilon_3\text{,}\) where the \(\epsilon_i\) are orthonormal vectors of square one [Loo09, Section 2]. Choose a basis \(e,f\) of one hyperbolic summand with \(e^2=f^2=0\) and \((e,f)=1\text{.}\) For \(d=6n\text{,}\) the vectors \(c,e+nf\) give the first normal form. For \(d=6n+2\text{,}\) use \(c,\epsilon_1+e+nf\text{.}\) Each span is saturated: integrality of the \(\epsilon_2\) coordinate forces the coefficient of \(c\) to be integral, and the \(e\) coordinate forces the other coefficient to be integral. These formulas also explain the explicit representatives in [Huy23, Chapter 6, Section 5.2] after reversing his pairing convention.
    The image theorem Theorem 4.1 excludes precisely the hyperplanes with saturated label discriminants two and six. For any larger discriminant, \(\mathcal D_K^+\) is not one of those hyperplanes. A point outside their intersections therefore gives a smooth cubic carrying the label. The orbit and algebraicity statement gives irreducibility and codimension one. This proves the existence criterion in the form established earlier by Hassett [Has00, Theorems 3.2.3 and 4.3.1].

    Theorem 5.2.

    The locus \(\mathcal C_d\) is a nonempty irreducible divisor in the moduli space of smooth cubic fourfolds if and only if \(d\gt6\) and \(d\equiv0,2\pmod6\text{.}\) The nonempty loci are called Hassett divisors, or Noether–Lefschetz divisors for cubic fourfolds.
    Every special cubic carries a label: saturate the span of \(h^2\) and an independent integral Hodge class. Thus the special locus is the countable union of the nonempty \(\mathcal C_d\text{.}\)
    At a very general point of \(\mathcal C_d\text{,}\) the chosen label is all of \(A_X\text{.}\) Additional independent integral Hodge conditions occur on proper subvarieties. Thus \(\operatorname{rank}A_X=2\) there, while intersections with further independent Hodge conditions have larger rank.
    For a proposed algebraic lattice of larger rank, realization requires the whole-lattice restriction from the period image, in addition to positivity and a saturated embedding containing \(c\text{.}\) One allowed rank-two label does not suffice.

    Section 5.3 The plane and the quartic scroll

    A surface \(S\subset X\) has degree \((h^2,[S])\) and self-intersection \([S]^2\text{.}\) Their values determine the Gram matrix with \(h^2\text{.}\) We compute them for the plane and the quartic scroll, and check saturation using the existence criterion.
    For a plane \(P\simeq\mathbb P^2\) inside the smooth cubic, the normal bundles fit into
    \begin{gather*} 0\longrightarrow N_{P/X}\longrightarrow\mathcal O_P(1)^{\oplus3}\longrightarrow\mathcal O_P(3)\longrightarrow0. \end{gather*}
    The last map is surjective because the cubic is smooth along the plane. With \(H=h|_P\text{,}\) this gives
    \begin{gather*} c(N_{P/X})=\frac{(1+H)^3}{1+3H}=1+3H^2. \end{gather*}
    The self-intersection formula yields \([P]^2=3\text{.}\) Since \((h^2,[P])=1\text{,}\) the Gram matrix is
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}, \end{align*}
    of determinant eight. A proper saturation would have index two and discriminant two, excluded for smooth cubics. Thus the plane gives a label of discriminant eight.
    For any smooth surface \(S\subset X\text{,}\) let \(H=h|_S\) and let \(K_S\) be its canonical class. Expanding \(c(\mathcal T_X|_S)=(1+H)^6/(1+3H)\) gives \(c_1(\mathcal T_X|_S)=3H\) and \(c_2(\mathcal T_X|_S)=6H^2\text{.}\) The tangent and normal sequence gives the degree formula
    \begin{gather*} [S]^2=\int_Sc_2(N_{S/X})=6H^2+3H\cdot K_S+K_S^2-c_2(S). \end{gather*}
    This is the normal bundle calculation used for the later rationality constructions.
    1
    For the balanced quartic scroll, take \(\Sigma=\mathbb P^1\times\mathbb P^1\) embedded by \(\mathcal O(1,2)\text{.}\) If \(s,f\) are its ruling classes, then \(H=s+2f\) and \(K_\Sigma=-2s-2f\text{.}\) The identities \(s^2=f^2=0\) and \(s\cdot f=1\) give \(H^2=4\text{,}\) \(H\cdot K_\Sigma=-6\text{,}\) \(K_\Sigma^2=8\text{,}\) and \(c_2(\Sigma)=4\text{.}\) Hence \([\Sigma]^2=24-18+8-4=10\text{.}\) The resulting matrix is
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix}3&4\\4&10\end{pmatrix}. \end{align*}
    Its determinant is fourteen, so its span is saturated.
    The other smooth quartic rational normal scroll is \(\mathbb F_2\) embedded with hyperplane class \(H=s+3f\text{,}\) where \(s^2=-2\text{,}\) \(s\cdot f=1\text{,}\) and \(f^2=0\text{.}\) Since \(K=-2s-4f\text{,}\) the same four numerical invariants follow, giving the same label. The two embeddings have different geometry but the same intersection matrix. The rational maps associated with both scrolls are treated in Section 8.1.

    Section 5.4 Association with a polarized K3 surface

    For a label \(K_d\text{,}\) its orthogonal in full middle cohomology has rank twenty-one and signature \((19,2)\text{.}\) A primitive ample polarization \(\ell\) of square \(d\) on a K3 surface has primitive cohomology \(\ell^\perp\) of signature \((2,19)\text{.}\) The comparison reverses that K3 pairing and shifts its Hodge types.

    Definition 5.3.

    A polarized K3 surface \((S,\ell)\text{,}\) with \(\ell\) primitive and ample, is associated to the labelled cubic \((X,K_d)\) if \(\ell^2=d\) and there exists an integral Hodge isometry
    \begin{gather*} K_d^\perp\simeq H^2(S,\mathbb Z)_{\mathrm{prim}}(-1). \end{gather*}
    On the right the pairing is the negative of the K3 cup product. The primitive group is \(\ell^\perp\subset H^2(S,\mathbb Z)\text{.}\)
    An unlabelled cubic admits an associated polarized K3 surface if there exist a label and a polarized K3 surface satisfying this condition [Has17, Section 3.2].
    Under the displayed comparison, the K3 \((2,0)\) line corresponds to the cubic \((3,1)\) line. The negative sign of the pairing is specified separately from this Hodge shift, as in the Abel–Jacobi comparison.2
    Here is the precise lattice result used in the association theorem. Let \(\Lambda_d\) denote the orthogonal of a primitive vector of square \(d\) in the K3 cohomology lattice. If \(d\gt0\) and \(d\equiv0,2\pmod6\text{,}\) and \(K_d\subset L\) is the saturated label of that discriminant, then
    \begin{gather*} K_d^\perp\simeq\Lambda_d(-1) \end{gather*}
    as integral lattices if and only if \(d\) is divisible by neither four nor nine nor any odd prime congruent to two modulo three [Has00, Proposition 5.1.4]. We quote this arithmetic classification, including its integral embedding and discriminant-form arguments.

    Theorem 5.4.

    A labelled smooth cubic fourfold \((X,K_d)\) admits an associated polarized K3 surface if and only if \(4\nmid d\text{,}\) \(9\nmid d\text{,}\) and no odd prime \(p\equiv2\pmod3\) divides \(d\text{.}\) A discriminant satisfying these conditions and the smooth existence conditions is called admissible.
    Hassett proves this in [Has00, Theorem 5.1.3]. Necessity follows from the stated integral lattice classification. For sufficiency, transport the cubic period to \(\Lambda_d\text{.}\) The K3 period theorem supplies a K3 surface with a primitive nef class of square \(d\) and the prescribed primitive Hodge structure. We use the polarized K3 period theorem in the form used in that proof: the class can be made ample when its orthogonal contains no integral \((1,1)\) class of square minus two. Such a class would correspond to a primitive cubic Hodge class of square two, which the smooth period image excludes. The resulting polarization is ample. This geometric reduction is also explained in [Huy23, Chapter 6, Section 5.2].
    The first admissible smooth discriminants are \(14,26,38,42\text{.}\) Discriminant eight occurs for smooth cubics but is not admissible. One arithmetic obstruction can be seen directly: when \(d\) is divisible by nine, the discriminant group of \(K_d^\perp\) is noncyclic, while that of primitive degree-\(d\) K3 cohomology is cyclic [Has00, Proposition 3.2.5].
    At a very general point with \(A_X=K_d\text{,}\) any associated surface has Picard rank one and the isometry restricts to \(T_X\simeq T_S(-1)\text{.}\) At a point with more algebraic classes, \(K_d^\perp\) contains Hodge classes and is larger than \(T_X\text{.}\) Taking the orthogonal of all algebraic classes on both sides still gives the same transcendental comparison. An isomorphism between rational transcendental spaces alone is a weaker datum than the integral association defined here.
    For \(X\in\mathcal C_8\cap\mathcal C_{14}\text{,}\) a label of discriminant fourteen gives an associated degree-fourteen K3 surface, although the plane label has discriminant eight and fails the association criterion.

    Section 5.5 Intersections of Hassett divisors

    Several labels on one cubic can impose fewer independent Hodge conditions than their number suggests. Let
    \begin{gather*} \mathscr D=\{d\in\Z:d\gt6,\ d\equiv0,2\pmod6\},\qquad \mathcal Z=\bigcap_{d\in\mathscr D}\mathcal C_d. \end{gather*}
    A cubic in \(\mathcal Z\) is called Hassett maximal. The labels witnessing its membership may overlap inside the same algebraic lattice.
    For a positive definite lattice \(M\) with an element \(o\) of square three, let \(\mathcal C_{(M,o)}\) consist of cubics admitting a primitive isometric embedding \(M\hookrightarrow A_X\) sending \(o\) to \(h^2\text{.}\) Yang and Yu prove that this locus is nonempty, irreducible, and closed of codimension \(r-1\) if \(r=\operatorname{rank}M\geq2\text{,}\) \(o^\perp\subset M\) is even, \(M\) has no vector of square two, and \(r+\ell(M^\vee/M)\leq20\) [YY23, Theorem 5.1]. At a very general point, \(A_X\simeq M\text{.}\) For two distinct nonempty Hassett divisors, their intersection is a finite union of such rank-three loci. Their components have dimension eighteen; the algorithm determining them includes possible overlattices of the span of the two labels [YY23, Corollary 7.5 and Algorithm 7.6].

    Theorem 5.5.

    The Fermat cubic \(\{x_0^3+\cdots+x_5^3=0\}\) belongs to every \(\mathcal C_d\) with \(d\in\mathscr D\text{.}\) Moreover, \(\dim\mathcal Z=16\text{.}\)
    The first assertion is Yang and Yu’s [YY23, Theorem 1.2]; the dimension is Gal and Nuer’s [GN25, Theorem 6.2]. The Fermat example has algebraic rank twenty-one [YY23, Section 6.1], whereas very general cubics on a sixteen-dimensional component have rank five. The arithmetic behind the dimension uses the quadratic form
    \begin{gather*} q_M(\overline v)=3(v,v)-(o,v)^2,\qquad \overline v\in M/\Z o. \end{gather*}
    Replacing \(v\) by \(v+no\text{,}\) for \(n\in\Z\text{,}\) leaves this value fixed, and primitivity of its quotient class is equivalent to saturation of \(\Z o+\Z v\text{.}\) A primitive class \(\overline v\) therefore gives a saturated label of discriminant \(q_M(\overline v)\text{.}\) Thus one lattice can supply infinitely many labels. Gal and Nuer show that the smallest rank of a positive definite integer-matrix quadratic form with primitive image \(\mathscr D\) is four. The corresponding algebraic lattice has rank five and its period locus has codimension four [GN25, Corollary 4.2 and Proposition 6.1].
    Gal and Nuer’s 2026 preprint gives equations for the general member of one sixteen-dimensional irreducible component of \(\mathcal Z\text{.}\) After a projective change of coordinates, its cubic equation satisfies
    \begin{gather*} f\in(x,y,z)\cap(x,y,u)\cap(x,z,v)\cap(v-by,u-az,w),\qquad a,b\in\C. \end{gather*}
    The four ideals define planes. For general parameters, the first plane meets the second and third in lines, the second meets the third in a point, and the fourth is disjoint from the other three. Their classes together with \(h^2\) generate the rank-five lattice used in the construction. Primitive representations by \(q_M\) establish membership in every Hassett divisor [GN26, Theorem 1.1 and Sections 2–3]. This describes one component; the dimension theorem concerns the entire intersection. Every cubic in \(\mathcal Z\) is rational, since \(\mathcal Z\subset\mathcal C_{14}\) and Theorem 8.4 applies.
