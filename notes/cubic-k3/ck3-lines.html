    Chapter 2 Variety of lines

    Section 2.1 Lines as a zero locus

    Let \(G=\operatorname{Gr}(2,V)\) and let \(\mathcal U\subset V\otimes\mathcal O_G\) be the tautological bundle. A point of \(G\) is a two-dimensional vector subspace \(U\subset V\text{,}\) corresponding to the projective line \(\mathbb P(U)\text{.}\) Restricting \(f\) to \(U\) gives a section of the rank-four bundle \(\operatorname{Sym}^3\mathcal U^\vee\text{.}\) Its zero scheme is
    \begin{gather*} F(X)=\{\ell\in G:\ell\subset X\}. \end{gather*}
    Thus the scheme structure of the variety of lines comes directly from the requirement that four coefficients of a binary cubic vanish.
    The Grassmannian has dimension eight, so four independent equations would leave dimension four. Independence must be checked for every line, rather than only for a general section of the bundle. The assumption that the cubic itself is smooth gives precisely this check.1

    Proposition 2.1.

    For a smooth complex cubic fourfold \(X\text{,}\) the variety \(F(X)\) is nonempty and smooth of dimension four.

    Proof.
    There is a contained line through every point \(x\in X\text{.}\) Choose coordinates with \(x=[1:0:\cdots:0]\text{.}\) The equation has the form \(x_0^2\ell(x_1,\ldots,x_5)+x_0q(x_1,\ldots,x_5)+r(x_1,\ldots,x_5)=0\text{,}\) with \(\ell\ne0\) by smoothness. Directions of lines through \(x\) lie in \(\{\ell=0\}\simeq\mathbb P^3\) and satisfy \(q=r=0\text{.}\) Two homogeneous equations of positive degree in \(\mathbb P^3_{\mathbb C}\) have a common zero, so such a line exists.
    Choose coordinates on a line \(\ell\simeq\mathbb P^1\text{,}\) with four normal coordinates in \(\mathbb P^5\text{.}\) To first order in the normal directions, the cubic equation is \(\sum_{i=1}^4 y_iq_i\text{,}\) where the \(q_i\) are binary quadrics. Moving the line by four linear forms \(a_i\) changes the restricted cubic to \(\sum_i a_iq_i\text{.}\) The derivative of the section is therefore the map
    \begin{gather*} H^0(\mathcal O_\ell(1))^{\oplus4}\longrightarrow H^0(\mathcal O_\ell(3)) \end{gather*}
    that sends \((a_i)\) to \(\sum_i a_iq_i\text{.}\) Smoothness of \(X\) implies that the quadrics have no common zero on \(\ell\text{:}\) a common zero would make every derivative of \(f\) vanish there.
    Choose a nonzero quadric in their span, then another whose zeros avoid its finitely many zeros. These two quadrics generate a pencil without base points. Its Koszul sequence, twisted by \(\mathcal O_\ell(3)\text{,}\) is
    \begin{gather*} 0\longrightarrow\mathcal O_\ell(-1)\longrightarrow\mathcal O_\ell(1)^{\oplus2}\longrightarrow\mathcal O_\ell(3)\longrightarrow0. \end{gather*}
    Since \(H^1(\mathcal O_\ell(-1))=0\text{,}\) the induced map on global sections is surjective. The derivative for all four quadrics is then surjective as well. Its kernel has dimension \(8-4=4\text{,}\) proving smoothness and the asserted dimension at every line.
    The same derivative is the map on global sections in the normal-bundle sequence
    \begin{gather*} 0\longrightarrow N_{\ell/X}\longrightarrow\mathcal O_\ell(1)^{\oplus4}\longrightarrow\mathcal O_\ell(3)\longrightarrow0. \end{gather*}
    It identifies \(T_{[\ell]}F(X)\) with \(H^0(N_{\ell/X})\) and proves \(H^1(N_{\ell/X})=0\text{.}\) This is the deformation-theoretic meaning of the calculation: all first-order motions of a line that satisfy the cubic equation are unobstructed.
    Let \(g_G=c_1(\det\mathcal U^\vee)\) on the Grassmannian, and let \(g=g_G|_{F(X)}\) be the Plücker class. The Grassmannian tangent bundle is \(\mathcal U^\vee\otimes(V/\mathcal U)\text{,}\) whose first Chern class is \(6g_G\text{.}\) Thus \(K_G=\mathcal O_G(-6)\text{.}\) If \(x,y\) are the Chern roots of \(\mathcal U^\vee\text{,}\) the roots of its third symmetric power are \(3x,2x+y,x+2y,3y\text{.}\) Their sum is \(6(x+y)\text{.}\) Adjunction gives
    \begin{gather*} K_{F(X)}=(K_G\otimes\det(\operatorname{Sym}^3\mathcal U^\vee))|_{F(X)}\simeq\mathcal O_{F(X)}. \end{gather*}
    The equality of the two coefficients six is specific to this Grassmannian and this symmetric power.

    Section 2.2 The incidence correspondence and the two-form

    The universal line is the incidence variety \(P=\{(\ell,x):x\in\ell\subset X\}\text{,}\) with projections \(p:P\to F(X)\) and \(q:P\to X\text{.}\) The first projection is a projective-line bundle. Pullback followed by integration along its fibres defines the Abel–Jacobi correspondence
    \begin{gather*} \operatorname{AJ}=p_*q^*:H^4(X,\mathbb Z)\longrightarrow H^2(F(X),\mathbb Z). \end{gather*}
    The degree decreases by two because the fibres of \(p\) have complex dimension one. Hodge bidegrees decrease by \((1,1)\text{.}\) In particular, the period line \(H^{3,1}(X)\) gives a holomorphic two-form on the variety of lines.
    Write \(q_F\) for the primitive integral Beauville–Bogomolov form on \(H^2(F(X),\mathbb Z)\text{,}\) normalized to be positive on a Kähler class. We use this form and the Fujiki relation with the normalization in [Bea83, Sections 4 and 6] and [Fuj87]. Beauville and Donagi prove the following result for the variety of lines.

    Theorem 2.2.

    For every smooth complex cubic fourfold \(X\text{,}\) the variety \(F(X)\) is an irreducible holomorphic symplectic fourfold deformation equivalent to \(S^{[2]}\) for a K3 surface \(S\text{.}\) The incidence map \(\operatorname{AJ}:H^4(X,\mathbb Z)\to H^2(F(X),\mathbb Z)\) is an isomorphism of integral groups and Hodge structures with indices lowered by \((1,1)\text{,}\) sends \(h^2\) to \(g\text{,}\) and restricts to the primitive lattice comparison stated below.
    We use Beauville–Donagi’s theorem in this form [BD85, Propositions 1, 2, 4, and 6]. The next two sections explain its cohomological and Pfaffian mechanisms. Huybrechts develops these complementary approaches in [Huy23, Chapter 6, Sections 3.2 and 3.4].
    The primitive comparison includes a sign that should remain explicit. Set \(H^2(F(X),\mathbb Z)_{\mathrm{prim}}=g^\perp\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{gather*} \operatorname{AJ}:L_X^0(1)\xrightarrow{\sim}H^2(F(X),\mathbb Z)_{\mathrm{prim}} \end{gather*}
    satisfies
    \begin{gather*} q_F(\operatorname{AJ}(a),\operatorname{AJ}(b))=-(a,b)_X. \end{gather*}
    This is the primitive lattice comparison in [BD85, Proposition 6]. The signature changes from \((20,2)\) to \((2,20)\text{,}\) as a weight-two period domain requires.
    3
    The cohomological proof explains exactly what is needed beyond \(K_F\simeq\mathcal O_F\text{.}\) Its additional cohomology calculation gives \(H^0(F,\Omega_F^2)=\mathbb C\sigma\) with \(\sigma\wedge\sigma\ne0\text{.}\) For a connected projective fourfold with trivial canonical bundle, a nonzero section of that bundle is nowhere zero: after a trivialization it is a nonzero constant holomorphic function. Thus \(\sigma\wedge\sigma\) is nowhere zero and \(\sigma\) is nondegenerate. The cohomology calculation is the external input in this alternative proof [Huy23, Chapter 6, Section 3.2]. Connectedness can already be obtained from the zero-locus description [AK77, Theorem 1.16(i)].
    Holomorphic forms on the projective manifold \(F(X)\) are closed. The simple connectedness assertion, needed for irreducibility, is included in the theorem above.

    Section 2.3 A Pfaffian cubic and a Hilbert square

    Use a six-dimensional space \(W\subset\bigwedge^2V^\vee\) of alternating forms. The Pfaffian of a \(6\times6\) skew matrix has degree three. For general \(W\text{,}\) the locus
    \begin{gather*} X=\{[\omega]\in\mathbb P(W):\operatorname{rank}\omega\leq4\} \end{gather*}
    is a smooth cubic fourfold. Its associated surface is the orthogonal linear section
    \begin{gather*} S=\operatorname{Gr}(2,V)\cap\mathbb P(W^\perp)\subset\mathbb P(\bigwedge^2V). \end{gather*}
    A point \(U\in S\) is a two-dimensional subspace on which every alternating form in \(W\) vanishes. This common isotropy condition explains the relation between the two varieties.
    For general \(W\text{,}\) the surface \(S\) is a smooth intersection of six hyperplanes in the eight-dimensional Grassmannian. Adjunction gives \(K_S\simeq\mathcal O_S\text{,}\) and the hyperplane theorem gives connectedness and \(H^1(S,\mathcal O_S)=0\text{.}\) Thus \(S\) is a K3 surface. Its polarization has square fourteen, the degree of \(\operatorname{Gr}(2,6)\text{.}\) For example, the Grassmannian coefficient formula gives that degree as \(\binom84-\binom85=14\text{.}\)
    On the open subset of \(S^{[2]}\) parametrizing distinct pairs, take two points \(U_1,U_2\in S\) with direct sum \(A=U_1\oplus U_2\text{.}\) Restriction defines a map from \(W\) to alternating forms on \(A\text{.}\) Since these forms already vanish on each \(U_i\text{,}\) their restrictions lie in the four-dimensional space of cross terms. The kernel is generically two-dimensional. Its projectivization is a line in \(\mathbb P(W)\text{.}\) Every form in that pencil vanishes on the four-dimensional subspace \(A\text{.}\) A nondegenerate alternating form on a six-dimensional space has isotropic subspaces of dimension at most three, so every member of the pencil has rank at most four. The line is therefore contained in \(X\text{.}\)4
    For the inverse, let \(\Lambda=\langle\omega_0,\omega_1\rangle\subset W\) be a pencil representing a line of \(X\text{.}\) Work where both forms have rank four and their two-dimensional kernels \(K_i=\ker\omega_i\) satisfy \(K_0\cap K_1=0\text{.}\) If a four-dimensional subspace \(A\subset V\) is isotropic for both forms, it contains both kernels. Indeed, \(V/K_i\) is symplectic of dimension four, so the image of \(A\) there has dimension at most two. Thus \(4-\dim(A\cap K_i)\leq2\text{,}\) forcing \(K_i\subset A\text{.}\) Consequently the common space, when it exists, is recovered by
    \begin{gather*} A=\ker\omega_0\oplus\ker\omega_1. \end{gather*}
    The rank and disjointness conditions are open. For forms vanishing on a fixed \(A\text{,}\) the mixed blocks give maps \(A\to(V/A)^\vee\text{;}\) two general surjective maps have complementary kernels, so this condition occurs on the general pairs used above.
    Once \(A\) has been recovered, restrict to the open set where \(W\to\bigwedge^2A^\vee\) has rank four. Then \(\mathbb P(W^\perp\cap\bigwedge^2A)\) is a line in \(\mathbb P(\bigwedge^2A)\text{.}\) Its intersection with the quadric \(\operatorname{Gr}(2,A)\) is a length-two subscheme of \(S\text{,}\) provided the line is not contained in the quadric. On the locus where this consists of two distinct complementary planes \(U_1,U_2\text{,}\) the restriction map has kernel exactly \(\Lambda\text{.}\) The resulting unordered pair therefore reverses the construction from \(\{U_1,U_2\}\) to \(\mathbb P(\Lambda)\text{.}\)
    For general \(W\text{,}\) Beauville and Donagi choose \(S\) and \(X\) smooth, with \(S\) containing no line and \(X\) containing no plane. Their pencil lemma supplies the common isotropic four-space; their construction over the limiting length-two schemes gives the isomorphism \(S^{[2]}\simeq F(X)\) [BD85, Section 2, Proposition 5(i), p. 704]. The hypothesis that \(S\) contains no line ensures that the intersection above has length two. The kernel formula explains the inverse on the stated open set; existence for the remaining pencils and extension over the limits are supplied by their theorem. See also [Huy23, Chapter 6, Section 2].
    If \(H_2\) denotes the divisor on \(S^{[2]}\) induced by the degree-fourteen polarization of \(S\text{,}\) and \(2\delta\) is the exceptional divisor of the Hilbert–Chow morphism, the Plücker polarization becomes \(2H_2-5\delta\) [Huy23, Chapter 6, Section 2]. This statement concerns the general Pfaffian isomorphism just described. For every K3 surface, the second cohomology of its Hilbert square has the orthogonal decomposition
    \begin{gather*} H^2(S^{[2]},\mathbb Z)=H^2(S,\mathbb Z)\oplus\mathbb Z\delta \end{gather*}
    in which \(q_F(\delta)=-2\text{,}\) and the form on the K3 summand is its intersection form [Bea83, Section 6]. It follows that \(q_F(2H_2-5\delta)=4\cdot14-25\cdot2=6\text{.}\) The class is not the divisor induced by the original K3 polarization alone.

    Section 2.4 From one cubic to every smooth cubic

    The smooth cubic equations form a connected open subset of \(\mathbb P^{55}\text{.}\) Their relative varieties of lines form a projective family over this open subset. The surjectivity calculation in Proposition 2.1 proves that the relative family is smooth. A path to a general Pfaffian cubic therefore connects \(F(X)\) to a Hilbert square in a smooth proper family.
    Ehresmann’s theorem identifies the underlying differentiable manifolds along this family, and smooth projective Hodge theory preserves the Hodge numbers. Beauville–Donagi’s symplectic theorem and the Pfaffian endpoint show that every \(F(X)\) has \(K3^{[2]}\) deformation type [Huy23, Chapter 6, Section 3.2].
    Every \(F(X)\) has the deformation type of a Hilbert square. Birationality or isomorphism of a fixed \(F(X)\) to a Hilbert square imposes further conditions on its Hodge structure and ample cone. The general Pfaffian cubic supplies the isomorphism constructed above.

    Section 2.5 The Plücker polarization

    The top self-intersection of \(g\) can be computed from the zero-locus class [AK77, Proposition 1.6]. The zero-locus description gives
    \begin{gather*} \int_{F(X)}g^4=\int_G(x+y)^4\,9xy(2x+y)(x+2y). \end{gather*}
    For a symmetric polynomial of degree eight in the Chern roots on \(\operatorname{Gr}(2,6)\text{,}\) integration is the coefficient of \(x^5y^4\) after multiplying by \(x-y\text{.}\) In this instance the coefficients of \(x^4y^4\) and \(x^5y^3\) before that multiplication are \(414\) and \(306\text{.}\) Their difference gives
    \begin{gather*} \int_{F(X)}g^4=108. \end{gather*}
    This also proves that the section defining \(F(X)\) cannot be nowhere zero, since its top Chern class has a nonzero intersection.
    For a fourfold of \(K3^{[2]}\) type, the Fujiki relation is
    \begin{gather*} \int_{F(X)}a^4=3q_F(a)^2. \end{gather*}
    The coefficient is three for this deformation type [Fuj87]. Since \(g\) is ample, \(q_F(g)>0\text{.}\) The intersection calculation therefore gives \(q_F(g)=6\text{,}\) agreeing with the Pfaffian calculation.
    In the Pfaffian model, the K3 lattice is unimodular and \(H_2\) is primitive, so the pairings of \(2H_2\) with that summand generate \(2\mathbb Z\text{.}\) Its pairing with \(\delta\) is \(10\text{.}\) The whole ideal is consequently \(2\mathbb Z\text{.}\) The relatively prime coefficients two and five in \(g=2H_2-5\delta\) also prove that \(g\) itself is primitive. Parallel transport preserves the integral lattice and the Plücker class, proving these assertions on every \(F(X)\text{.}\)5
    The primitive comparison with the cubic respects these data. The lattice \(H^2(F(X),\mathbb Z)\) has discriminant of absolute value two, while \(g^\perp\) has discriminant three. Its sign-reversed isometry with \(L_X^0\) is consistent with the primitive cubic discriminant. The variety of lines therefore retains the cubic period in its second cohomology, where the quadratic form and the ample class can be studied by the geometry of holomorphic symplectic manifolds.

    1. The name Fano variety.

    Here Fano variety means a parameter space of lines, named after Gino Fano. It does not say that its anticanonical bundle is ample. The variety \(X\) is Fano in the latter sense; \(F(X)\) has trivial canonical bundle.

    2. What adjunction proves.

    A trivial canonical bundle is necessary for a holomorphic symplectic fourfold, but does not construct a two-form or prove simple connectedness. Both assertions need further arguments.

    3. The primitive qualification.

    The sign formula applies to primitive classes. One has \(\operatorname{AJ}(h^2)=g\text{,}\) but \((h^2,h^2)_X=3\) and \(q_F(g)=6\text{.}\) Thus the full cohomology map cannot be an isometry with the same sign convention.

    4. Why the Hilbert square appears.

    Unordered distinct pairs give a dense open subset of \(S^{[2]}\text{.}\) The Hilbert scheme adds length-two subschemes supported at one point, with their tangent directions. These are the limits needed when the two isotropic planes approach one another.

    5. The Plücker data.

    The class \(g\) is primitive, has \(q_F(g)=6\text{,}\) and has divisibility two. Its fourth self-intersection is \(108\text{.}\) These are the specified polarization data used in the Torelli comparison.
