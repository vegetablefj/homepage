    Chapter 2 The variety of lines

    Section 2.1 Lines as a zero locus

    Let \(G=\operatorname{Gr}(2,V)\) and let \(\mathcal U\subset V\otimes\mathcal O_G\) be the tautological bundle. A point of \(G\) is a two-dimensional vector subspace \(U\subset V\text{,}\) corresponding to the projective line \(\mathbb P(U)\text{.}\) Restricting \(f\) to \(U\) gives a section of the rank-four bundle \(\operatorname{Sym}^3\mathcal U^\vee\text{.}\) Its zero scheme is
    \begin{gather*} F(X)=\{\ell\in G:\ell\subset X\}. \end{gather*}
    Thus the scheme structure of the variety of lines comes directly from the requirement that four coefficients of a binary cubic vanish.
    The Grassmannian has dimension eight, so four independent equations would leave dimension four. Independence must be checked for every line, rather than only for a general section of the bundle. The assumption that the cubic itself is smooth gives precisely this check.1

    Proposition 2.1.

    For a smooth cubic fourfold \(X\text{,}\) the variety \(F(X)\) is smooth of dimension four.

    Proof.
    Choose coordinates on a line \(\ell\simeq\mathbb P^1\text{,}\) with four normal coordinates in \(\mathbb P^5\text{.}\) To first order in the normal directions, the cubic equation is \(\sum_{i=1}^4 y_iq_i\text{,}\) where the \(q_i\) are binary quadrics. Moving the line by four linear forms \(a_i\) changes the restricted cubic to \(\sum_i a_iq_i\text{.}\) The derivative of the section is therefore the map
    \begin{gather*} H^0(\mathcal O_\ell(1))^{\oplus4}\longrightarrow H^0(\mathcal O_\ell(3)) \end{gather*}
    that sends \((a_i)\) to \(\sum_i a_iq_i\text{.}\) Smoothness of \(X\) implies that the quadrics have no common zero on \(\ell\text{:}\) a common zero would make every derivative of \(f\) vanish there.
    Choose a nonzero quadric in their span, then another whose zeros avoid its finitely many zeros. These two quadrics generate a pencil without base points. Its Koszul sequence, twisted by \(\mathcal O_\ell(3)\text{,}\) is
    \begin{gather*} 0\longrightarrow\mathcal O_\ell(-1)\longrightarrow\mathcal O_\ell(1)^{\oplus2}\longrightarrow\mathcal O_\ell(3)\longrightarrow0. \end{gather*}
    Since \(H^1(\mathcal O_\ell(-1))=0\text{,}\) the induced map on global sections is surjective. The derivative for all four quadrics is then surjective as well. Its kernel has dimension \(8-4=4\text{,}\) proving smoothness and the asserted dimension at every line. Nonemptiness follows, for example, from the nonzero top Chern-class intersection computed in Section 2.5.
    The same derivative is the map on global sections in the normal-bundle sequence
    \begin{gather*} 0\longrightarrow N_{\ell/X}\longrightarrow\mathcal O_\ell(1)^{\oplus4}\longrightarrow\mathcal O_\ell(3)\longrightarrow0. \end{gather*}
    It identifies \(T_{[\ell]}F(X)\) with \(H^0(N_{\ell/X})\) and proves \(H^1(N_{\ell/X})=0\text{.}\) This is the deformation-theoretic meaning of the calculation: all first-order motions of a line that satisfy the cubic equation are unobstructed.
    Set \(g=c_1(\det\mathcal U^\vee)|_{F(X)}\text{,}\) the Plücker class. The Grassmannian tangent bundle is \(\mathcal U^\vee\otimes(V/\mathcal U)\text{,}\) whose first Chern class is \(6g\text{.}\) Thus \(K_G=\mathcal O_G(-6)\text{.}\) If \(x,y\) are the Chern roots of \(\mathcal U^\vee\text{,}\) the roots of its third symmetric power are \(3x,2x+y,x+2y,3y\text{.}\) Their sum is \(6(x+y)\text{.}\) Adjunction gives
    \begin{gather*} K_{F(X)}=(K_G\otimes\det(\operatorname{Sym}^3\mathcal U^\vee))|_{F(X)}\simeq\mathcal O_{F(X)}. \end{gather*}
    The equality of the two coefficients six is specific to this Grassmannian and this symmetric power.

    Section 2.2 The incidence correspondence and the two-form

    The universal line is the incidence variety \(P=\{(\ell,x):x\in\ell\subset X\}\text{,}\) with projections \(p:P\to F(X)\) and \(q:P\to X\text{.}\) The first projection is a projective-line bundle. Pullback followed by integration along its fibres defines the Abel–Jacobi correspondence
    \begin{gather*} \operatorname{AJ}=p_*q^*:H^4(X,\mathbb Z)\longrightarrow H^2(F(X),\mathbb Z). \end{gather*}
    The degree decreases by two because the fibres of \(p\) have complex dimension one. Hodge bidegrees decrease by \((1,1)\text{.}\) In particular, the period line \(H^{3,1}(X)\) gives a holomorphic two-form on the variety of lines.
    Beauville and Donagi prove that this two-form is everywhere nondegenerate and determine the resulting deformation type [BD85].

    Theorem 2.2.

    The variety \(F(X)\) is an irreducible holomorphic symplectic fourfold deformation equivalent to the Hilbert scheme of two points on a K3 surface. The Abel–Jacobi map restricts to an integral Hodge isomorphism between primitive cohomology, with bidegrees shifted by \((1,1)\text{.}\)
    The primitive comparison includes a sign that should remain explicit. Let \(q_F\) denote the Beauville–Bogomolov pairing, normalized as the primitive integral form positive on an ample class, and set \(H^2(F(X),\mathbb Z)_{\mathrm{prim}}=g^\perp\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{gather*} \operatorname{AJ}:L_X^0(1)\xrightarrow{\sim}H^2(F(X),\mathbb Z)_{\mathrm{prim}} \end{gather*}
    satisfies
    \begin{gather*} q_F(\operatorname{AJ}(a),\operatorname{AJ}(b))=-(a,b)_X. \end{gather*}
    This is the primitive lattice comparison in [BD85, Proposition 6]. The signature changes from \((20,2)\) to \((2,20)\text{,}\) as a weight-two period domain requires.
    3
    A nonzero image of the period line would by itself show only the existence of a two-form. Nondegeneracy means that its pairing on \(T_{[\ell]}F(X)\) has zero kernel at every line. Beauville and Donagi establish the symplectic assertion by comparing a general Pfaffian example with a Hilbert square and then using deformation: their Proposition 2 implies Proposition 1. The construction below explains this comparison. The incidence correspondence is compatible with families, so the two-form records the moving cubic period.
    For a compact Kähler manifold, holomorphic forms are closed. In dimension four, a nondegenerate two-form has a nowhere-vanishing square, which trivializes the canonical bundle. This recovers the adjunction calculation geometrically. Irreducible holomorphic symplectic additionally means simple connectedness and that the space of holomorphic two-forms is generated by this form. Those conditions will follow from the comparison with a Hilbert square and the smooth family of varieties of lines.

    Section 2.3 A Pfaffian cubic and a Hilbert square

    Use a six-dimensional space \(W\subset\bigwedge^2V^\vee\) of alternating forms. The Pfaffian of a \(6\times6\) skew matrix has degree three. For general \(W\text{,}\) the locus
    \begin{gather*} X=\{[\omega]\in\mathbb P(W):\operatorname{rank}\omega\leq4\} \end{gather*}
    is a smooth cubic fourfold. Its associated surface is the orthogonal linear section
    \begin{gather*} S=\operatorname{Gr}(2,V)\cap\mathbb P(W^\perp)\subset\mathbb P(\bigwedge^2V). \end{gather*}
    A point \(U\in S\) is a two-dimensional subspace on which every alternating form in \(W\) vanishes. This common isotropy condition explains the relation between the two varieties.
    The surface is cut out by six hyperplanes in the eight-dimensional Grassmannian. Adjunction gives \(K_S\simeq\mathcal O_S\text{,}\) and the hyperplane theorem gives connectedness and \(H^1(S,\mathcal O_S)=0\text{.}\) We use the definition that a projective K3 surface is a smooth connected projective surface with trivial canonical bundle and \(H^1(S,\mathcal O_S)=0\text{.}\) Thus this smooth linear section is a K3 surface. Its polarization has square fourteen, the degree of \(\operatorname{Gr}(2,6)\text{.}\) For example, the Grassmannian coefficient formula gives that degree as \(\binom84-\binom85=14\text{.}\)
    The Hilbert square \(S^{[2]}\) parametrizes zero-dimensional subschemes of length two in \(S\text{.}\) On its open set of distinct pairs, take two points \(U_1,U_2\in S\) with direct sum \(A=U_1\oplus U_2\text{.}\) Restriction defines a map from \(W\) to alternating forms on \(A\text{.}\) Since these forms already vanish on each \(U_i\text{,}\) their restrictions lie in the four-dimensional space of cross terms. The kernel is generically two-dimensional. Its projectivization is a line in \(\mathbb P(W)\text{.}\) Every form in that pencil vanishes on the four-dimensional subspace \(A\text{.}\) A nondegenerate alternating form on a six-dimensional space has isotropic subspaces of dimension at most three, so every member of the pencil has rank at most four. The line is therefore contained in \(X\text{.}\)4
    The construction gives a rational map \(S^{[2]}\dashrightarrow F(X)\text{.}\) On the open set of pencils for which the common isotropic four-space is recovered uniquely, the inverse takes that space \(A\) and intersects \(\operatorname{Gr}(2,A)\subset\mathbb P(\bigwedge^2A)\) with \(\mathbb P(W^\perp)\text{.}\) The intersection is a length-two scheme: the first variety is a quadric and the remaining linear subspace is a line. Beauville–Donagi prove that, for a general Pfaffian cubic, the correspondence extends over the limiting schemes and gives an isomorphism [BD85, Section 2]. The extension is a substantive part of the result, beyond the dimension count on the open sets.
    If \(H_2\) denotes the divisor on \(S^{[2]}\) induced by the degree-fourteen polarization of \(S\text{,}\) and \(2\delta\) is the exceptional divisor of the Hilbert–Chow morphism, the Plücker polarization becomes \(2H_2-5\delta\text{.}\) The orthogonal decomposition
    \begin{gather*} H^2(S^{[2]},\mathbb Z)=H^2(S,\mathbb Z)\perp\mathbb Z\delta \end{gather*}
    has \(q_F(\delta)=-2\text{,}\) and the form on the K3 summand is its intersection form [Bea83, Section 6]. It follows that \(q_F(2H_2-5\delta)=4\cdot14-25\cdot2=6\text{.}\) The class is not the divisor induced by the original K3 polarization alone.

    Section 2.4 From one cubic to every smooth cubic

    The smooth cubic equations form a connected open subset of \(\mathbb P^{55}\text{.}\) Their relative varieties of lines form a projective family over this open subset. The surjectivity calculation in Proposition 2.1 proves that the relative family is smooth. A path to a general Pfaffian cubic therefore connects \(F(X)\) to a Hilbert square in a smooth proper family.
    Ehresmann’s theorem makes the fibres locally diffeomorphic, so their fundamental groups agree. The Hilbert square of a K3 surface is simply connected, and it has one-dimensional space of holomorphic two-forms [Bea83, Section 6]. Hodge numbers are locally constant in this smooth projective family. Together with the everywhere nondegenerate form constructed by Beauville–Donagi, these facts prove the irreducible holomorphic symplectic assertion for every smooth cubic. They also explain the phrase of \(K3^{[2]}\) type.
    Deformation equivalence, birational equivalence, and isomorphism answer different questions. Deformation equivalence permits the complex structure to move in a smooth family. Birational equivalence identifies dense open subsets of two fixed varieties. Isomorphism identifies the whole fixed varieties. Every \(F(X)\) has the same deformation type as a Hilbert square, but asking whether a given \(F(X)\) is birational, or isomorphic, to a Hilbert square imposes further conditions on its Hodge structure and ample cone. The Pfaffian isomorphism is a special geometric occurrence.5

    Section 2.5 The Plücker polarization

    The top self-intersection of \(g\) can be computed before invoking hyperkähler geometry. The zero-locus description gives
    \begin{gather*} \int_{F(X)}g^4=\int_G(x+y)^4\,9xy(2x+y)(x+2y). \end{gather*}
    For a symmetric polynomial of degree eight in the Chern roots on \(\operatorname{Gr}(2,6)\text{,}\) integration is the coefficient of \(x^5y^4\) after multiplying by \(x-y\text{.}\) In this instance the coefficients of \(x^4y^4\) and \(x^5y^3\) before that multiplication are \(414\) and \(306\text{.}\) Their difference gives
    \begin{gather*} \int_{F(X)}g^4=108. \end{gather*}
    This also proves that the section defining \(F(X)\) cannot be nowhere zero, since its top Chern class has a nonzero intersection.
    The Beauville–Bogomolov form is a quadratic form on second cohomology, not the top intersection product itself. For a fourfold of \(K3^{[2]}\) type, the Fujiki relation is
    \begin{gather*} \int_{F(X)}a^4=3q_F(a)^2. \end{gather*}
    The relation comes from the structure of cohomology of compact holomorphic symplectic manifolds; its coefficient is three for this deformation type [Fuj87]. Since \(g\) is ample, \(q_F(g)>0\text{.}\) The intersection calculation therefore gives \(q_F(g)=6\text{,}\) agreeing with the Pfaffian calculation.
    The divisibility of an integral class \(g\) is the positive generator of the ideal \(q_F(g,H^2(F(X),\mathbb Z))\subset\mathbb Z\text{.}\) In the Pfaffian model, the K3 lattice is unimodular and \(H_2\) is primitive, so the pairings of \(2H_2\) with that summand generate \(2\mathbb Z\text{.}\) Its pairing with \(\delta\) is \(10\text{.}\) The whole ideal is consequently \(2\mathbb Z\text{.}\) The relatively prime coefficients two and five in \(g=2H_2-5\delta\) also prove that \(g\) itself is primitive. Parallel transport preserves the integral lattice and the Plücker class, proving these assertions on every \(F(X)\text{.}\)6
    The primitive comparison with the cubic respects these data. The lattice \(H^2(F(X),\mathbb Z)\) has discriminant of absolute value two, while \(g^\perp\) has discriminant three. Its sign-reversed isometry with \(L_X^0\) is consistent with the primitive cubic discriminant. The variety of lines therefore retains the cubic period in its second cohomology, where the quadratic form and the ample class can be studied by the geometry of holomorphic symplectic manifolds.

    1. The name Fano variety.

    Here Fano variety means a parameter space of lines, named after Gino Fano. It does not say that its anticanonical bundle is ample. The variety \(X\) is Fano in the latter sense; \(F(X)\) has trivial canonical bundle.

    2. What adjunction proves.

    A trivial canonical bundle is necessary for a holomorphic symplectic fourfold, but does not construct a two-form or prove simple connectedness. Both assertions need further arguments. A Calabi–Yau fourfold can have trivial canonical bundle and no holomorphic two-form.

    3. The primitive qualification.

    The sign formula applies to primitive classes. One has \(\operatorname{AJ}(h^2)=g\text{,}\) but \((h^2,h^2)_X=3\) and \(q_F(g)=6\text{.}\) Thus the full cohomology map cannot be an isometry with the same sign convention.

    4. Why the Hilbert square appears.

    Unordered distinct pairs give a dense open subset of \(S^{[2]}\text{.}\) The Hilbert scheme adds length-two subschemes supported at one point, with their tangent directions. These are the limits needed when the two isotropic planes approach one another.

    5. A statement about every fibre.

    All smooth cubics lie in one deformation family. This does not make their varieties of lines pairwise birational. In particular, deformation equivalence to a Hilbert square does not by itself provide a K3 surface attached to a fixed cubic by an integral Hodge isometry.

    6. Divisibility is not a factor.

    Divisibility two does not mean \(g=2a\) in cohomology. The class \(g\) is primitive. Divisibility measures all its pairings in a lattice that is not unimodular. The three numbers \(g^4=108\text{,}\) \(q_F(g)=6\text{,}\) and \(\operatorname{div}(g)=2\) describe different data.
