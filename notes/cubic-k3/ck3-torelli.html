    Chapter 3 The Torelli theorem

    Section 3.1 Markings and the period domain

    The period of a cubic fourfold records its Hodge decomposition inside an integral lattice. A marking specifies that lattice identification. Fix a pair \((L,c)\) isometric to \((H^4(X,\mathbb Z),h^2)\) for one smooth cubic, with the cup-product form. Thus \(L\) has signature \((21,2)\text{,}\) \(c^2=3\text{,}\) and \(L_0=c^\perp\) has signature \((20,2)\text{.}\) A marking of another cubic is an integral isometry \(\mu:H^4(X,\mathbb Z)\to L\) sending \(h^2\) to \(c\text{.}\) Two marked cubics are equivalent when a projective isomorphism between them respects their markings on cohomology.
    For a marked cubic, write \(\mu(H^{3,1}(X))=\mathbb C\omega\text{.}\) Its period belongs to
    \begin{gather*} \mathcal D=\{[\omega]\in\mathbb P(L_0\otimes\mathbb C):(\omega,\omega)=0,\;(\omega,\overline\omega)\lt0\}. \end{gather*}
    This space has two connected components. If \(\omega=a+ib\text{,}\) the equations say that \(a,b\) are orthogonal real vectors of equal negative square. Their ordered span is an oriented negative definite two-plane. Multiplying \(\omega\) by a nonzero complex number preserves that orientation; complex conjugation reverses it. Choose a component \(\mathcal D^+\text{.}\)
    1
    The line determines the remaining Hodge summands. Its complex conjugate gives \(H^{1,3}\text{,}\) and the orthogonal complement of their sum inside \(L_0\otimes\mathbb C\) gives the primitive \((2,2)\) part. Adding \(\mathbb Cc\) restores full middle cohomology. Thus recording a period line does not discard the other Hodge pieces. What it does discard, unless the marking is retained, is the specified identification of this decomposition with the integral cohomology of a particular cubic.
    Parallel transport in the universal family over the parameter space of smooth cubic equations preserves \(c\) and the component of the period domain. Beauville’s monodromy theorem identifies its image with
    \begin{gather*} \Gamma=O^+(L,c)\simeq\widetilde O^+(L_0). \end{gather*}
    Here the component preserved by \(O^+\) is \(\mathcal D^+\text{.}\) The assertion is an equality with the image of geometric parallel transport, not merely an inclusion in an abstract orthogonal group [Bea86, Theorem 2]. These conventions agree with [Laz10, Notation 2.12].
    For this fixed cubic pair, restriction from the full lattice identifies the isometries fixing \(c\) with the isometries of \(L_0\) acting trivially on \(A_{L_0}\text{.}\) The index-three inclusion \(\mathbb Zc\oplus L_0\subset L\) determines this extension condition. Thus preserving the primitive form alone is insufficient to obtain a specified isometry of the full cohomology fixing \(h^2\) [Laz10, Notation 2.12].
    For this discriminant-three lattice one has \(O^+(L_0)=\{\pm1\}\times\Gamma\text{.}\) The central element \(-1\) acts trivially on the projective domain. Consequently the two groups give the same coarse period orbits, although \(\Gamma\) is the group compatible with the full cubic marking. This is the quotient convention discussed in [Huy23, Chapter 6, Section 6.4].2

    Section 3.2 Local Torelli from the Jacobian ring

    Let \(\mathcal M^{\mathrm{mar}}\) be the analytic moduli space of marked smooth cubics, and let \(p:\mathcal M^{\mathrm{mar}}\to\mathcal D\) send \((X,\mu)\) to its period line. The calculation in Section 1.4 gives a twenty-dimensional deformation space. The domain also has dimension twenty. Equality of dimensions does not yet prove that the period map detects a deformation; its derivative must be computed.
    Write \(X=V(f)\) and let \(R_f\) be its Jacobian ring. The residue identifications in Section 1.2 give \(H^{3,1}(X)\simeq(R_f)_0\) and \(H^{2,2}(X)_{\mathrm{prim}}\simeq(R_f)_3\text{.}\) The tangent space of deformations is \(H^1(X,\mathcal T_X)\simeq(R_f)_3\text{.}\) For a cubic polynomial \(\dot f\) representing a deformation direction, differentiation gives
    \begin{gather*} \left.\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\Omega}{(f+t\dot f)^2}\right|_{t=0}=-\frac{2\dot f\Omega}{f^3}. \end{gather*}
    Passing to residues and to the quotient of the Hodge filtration computes the infinitesimal movement of the period line. Changes of coordinates give the Jacobian relations, so the derivative depends on the class of \(\dot f\) in \((R_f)_3\text{.}\)
    3

    Proposition 3.1.

    The period map for marked smooth cubic fourfolds is a local biholomorphism.

    Proof.
    The tangent space of the period quadric at \([\omega]\) is \(\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb C\omega,\omega^\perp/\mathbb C\omega)\text{.}\) Under the Hodge decomposition, the second factor is \(H^{2,2}(X)_{\mathrm{prim}}\text{.}\) The derivative just computed is, up to the nonzero scalar \(-2\text{,}\) multiplication \((R_f)_3\otimes(R_f)_0\to(R_f)_3\text{.}\) Since \((R_f)_0=\mathbb C\text{,}\) this is an isomorphism. The holomorphic inverse function theorem proves the assertion.

    Section 3.3 The global statement

    The strong statement specifies the cohomology map that the geometric isomorphism must induce. It is stated at the beginning of Voisin’s paper, with the correction to its proof discussed below [Voi86, p. 577], [Voi08, Theorem 1]. Charles uses the same form in [Cha12, Theorem 1].

    Theorem 3.2.

    Let \(X,X'\) be smooth complex cubic fourfolds. Suppose that an integral cup-product isometry \(\Phi:H^4(X,\mathbb Z)\to H^4(X',\mathbb Z)\) preserves the Hodge decomposition and sends \(h_X^2\) to \(h_{X'}^2\text{.}\) There is a projective isomorphism \(f:X'\to X\) such that \(f^*=\Phi\text{.}\)
    For the marked period map, this says that equal periods give equivalent marked cubics. To see the prescribed map in this formulation, choose a marking \(\mu\) of \(X\) and mark \(X'\) by \(\mu'=\mu\Phi^{-1}\text{.}\) The Hodge condition makes their period lines equal. Equality of the marked points gives \(\mu'f^*=\mu\text{,}\) which is exactly \(f^*=\Phi\text{.}\) Conversely, a projective isomorphism respecting markings identifies their periods.
    No separate orientation hypothesis is required in the displayed theorem. A Hodge isometry sends the complex line \(H^{3,1}\) to the corresponding complex line, so it preserves the induced orientation of the negative real two-plane. The integral condition and preservation of \(h^2\) remain essential: a rational Hodge comparison, or a comparison of transcendental parts alone, does not supply the asserted full lattice map.4
    After passing to unmarked cubics, local and global Torelli identify the coarse moduli space \(\mathcal C\) with an analytic open subset of \(\Gamma\backslash\mathcal D^+\text{.}\) The moduli construction and algebraicity of the period map make this an algebraic open embedding; the algebraicity is an additional theorem, rather than a consequence of the inverse function theorem [Huy23, Chapter 6, Section 6.4]. The next chapter determines its image.

    Section 3.4 Recovering the cubic from its polarized variety of lines

    The geometric comparison uses the polarized variety \((F(X),g)\text{.}\) The Plücker line bundle supplies its embedding and an ample class for the holomorphic symplectic Torelli theorem. Its cohomological square and divisibility are six and two, as computed in Section 2.5. These numerical invariants alone do not specify the line bundle; the reconstruction below uses the specified Plücker class.
    Let \(G=\operatorname{Gr}(2,V)\subset\mathbb P(\bigwedge^2V)\text{,}\) so that \(F(X)\subset G\text{.}\) Altman and Kleiman prove two facts about this embedding: its hyperplane system is complete, and every quadric containing \(F(X)\) contains \(G\) [AK77, Theorem 1.16(ii)–(iii)]. Since the Grassmannian itself is cut out by the Plücker quadrics, these facts recover it as the common zero locus of all quadrics through the embedded variety of lines.
    The quadratic equations have a direct interpretation. A bivector represents a two-dimensional subspace exactly when it is decomposable. If \(p_{ij}\) are its coefficients in a basis of \(\bigwedge^2V\text{,}\) one of the required equations is
    \begin{gather*} p_{12}p_{34}-p_{13}p_{24}+p_{14}p_{23}=0. \end{gather*}
    The equations for all four-element index sets express that the bivector has square zero in \(\bigwedge^4V\text{.}\) They distinguish the Grassmannian from the whole projective space. The additional assertion about \(F(X)\) is that restricting to the cubic’s contained lines introduces no new quadratic equations.
    5

    Proposition 3.3.

    A polarized isomorphism between \((F(X),g_X)\) and \((F(X'),g_{X'})\text{,}\) for smooth complex cubic fourfolds, is induced by a projective isomorphism between the cubics.

    Proof.
    Completeness of the hyperplane system extends the polarized isomorphism to a projective transformation of the Plücker ambient spaces. The equality of spaces of quadrics carries \(\operatorname{Gr}(2,6)\) to itself. Chow’s theorem on Grassmannians makes this transformation come from a projective transformation of \(\mathbb P^5\text{.}\) There is no additional duality automorphism in this case, since the vector-space dimension is six rather than four.
    This transformation sends every line of \(X\) to a line of \(X'\text{.}\) The cubic is the union of these lines. For example, through a smooth point the directions of contained lines satisfy a quadratic and a cubic equation in the projectivized tangent space \(\mathbb P^3\text{;}\) these equations have a common projective zero. The transformation therefore sends \(X\) to \(X'\text{.}\) This is the reconstruction in [Cha12, Proposition 4].

    Section 3.5 Parallel transport and the Kähler condition

    The proof below follows the holomorphic symplectic route presented in [Huy23, Chapter 6, Section 3.3]. Its two external inputs are Beauville’s monodromy theorem for cubics and the following precise form of the hyperkähler Torelli theorem. If two irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifolds are deformation equivalent and an integral Hodge isometry of their second cohomology is a parallel transport operator, then it is induced by an isomorphism if and only if it sends some Kähler class to a Kähler class [Mar11, Theorem 1.3].
    For varieties of lines, \(g\) is ample and therefore Kähler. It will supply the required class. We must first realize the prescribed cubic isometry by a path of cubics and then carry that path to their varieties of lines. This makes the transport hypothesis verifiable from the cubic family.6
    The integral Abel–Jacobi isomorphism in Theorem 2.2 is compatible with families. Its primitive pairing reverses sign. On full cohomology, the rational orthogonal decomposition along \(h^2\) gives
    \begin{gather*} q_F(\operatorname{AJ}(a),\operatorname{AJ}(b))=-(a,b)_X+(a,h^2)_X(b,h^2)_X. \end{gather*}
    Indeed write \(a=a_0+(a,h^2)_Xh^2/3\) and similarly for \(b\text{,}\) then use the primitive formula, \(\operatorname{AJ}(h^2)=g\text{,}\) and \(q_F(g)=6\text{.}\) Thus a full cubic isometry preserving \(h^2\) induces a Beauville–Bogomolov isometry fixing the Plücker class.

    Proof. (Theorem 3.2)
    Let \(\Phi\) satisfy Theorem 3.2. Choose a path in the connected parameter space of smooth cubic equations from \(X\) to \(X'\text{,}\) and let \(\tau:H^4(X,\mathbb Z)\to H^4(X',\mathbb Z)\) be its transport. Both \(\tau\) and \(\Phi\) preserve \(h^2\) and the orientation component. Hence \(\tau^{-1}\Phi\in\Gamma\text{.}\) Beauville’s theorem realizes this element as the monodromy of a loop based at \(X\text{.}\) Following that loop and then the chosen path gives a path whose transport is exactly \(\Phi\text{.}\)
    Over the same parameter space, the relative variety of lines is a smooth projective family by Section 2.4. Transport in this family gives an operator \(\eta:H^2(F(X),\mathbb Z)\to H^2(F(X'),\mathbb Z)\text{.}\) The relative incidence correspondence induces a morphism of local systems, so
    \begin{gather*} \eta\circ\operatorname{AJ}_X=\operatorname{AJ}_{X'}\circ\Phi. \end{gather*}
    This compatibility is the reason that the operator on the Fano side is actual parallel transport. It is not inferred from an arbitrary pair of lattice markings. Since \(\Phi\) and the Abel–Jacobi maps respect the Hodge structures, \(\eta\) is a Hodge isometry, with \(\eta(g_X)=g_{X'}\text{.}\)
    The varieties of lines are deformation equivalent irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifolds, by Theorem 2.2. The operator \(\eta\) is parallel transport and maps an ample class to an ample class. Markman’s theorem therefore gives a polarized isomorphism \(\psi:F(X')\to F(X)\) with \(\psi^*=\eta\text{.}\) The reconstruction in Proposition 3.3 produces a projective isomorphism \(f:X'\to X\) inducing \(\psi\text{.}\) Naturality of the incidence correspondence and injectivity of Abel–Jacobi give \(f^*=\Phi\text{.}\)
    Charles’s original proof uses an incidence space \(\mathcal N\) of marked cubics and compatibly marked varieties of lines. Its projections to marked cubic moduli and the corresponding component of marked polarized holomorphic symplectic moduli are local isomorphisms. The first projection has a unique compatible marking; the second is injective by polarized reconstruction [Cha12, Section 2 and Corollary 5]. The full Abel–Jacobi identity above supplies the compatibility of the markings.7
    For a very general polarized period, the integral \((1,1)\) lattice on the symplectic side is generated by \(g\text{,}\) of positive square. In a fixed component its marked period fibre is a single point, by the precise fibre theorem [Mar11, Theorem 2.2(5)]; Charles states the required consequence in [Cha12, Proposition 3]. The incidence comparison gives generic injectivity of the cubic period map on each component.
    Cubic moduli then supplies a property absent from general marked hyperkähler moduli: it is separated. Two distinct marked cubics in one component have disjoint small neighborhoods. If their periods were equal, local Torelli would map these neighborhoods to overlapping open subsets of the domain. A very general period in the overlap would have two marked preimages, contradicting generic injectivity. Such periods are dense because the countably many additional integral Hodge conditions are proper hyperplanes. Beauville’s monodromy theorem identifies the two marked cubic components with the two components of \(\mathcal D\text{,}\) so periods cannot coincide across them. These are the final steps in [Cha12, Proposition 6 and proof of Theorem 1].

    Section 3.6 Voisin’s geometric proof and its correction

    Voisin’s original proof uses the divisor of cubics containing a plane as a geometric test locus. The decisive conclusions are injectivity in codimension one along that divisor and the assertion that no point outside it has a period lying in its period image. These are restated in [Voi08, Theorem 2]. Extending this information to all cubics requires control of the boundary, because the period map is not proper.
    The corrected global step uses GIT and a partial compactification allowing isolated simple singularities. These boundary results are external inputs, stated in [Voi08, Theorem 3 and Proposition 5]. The locus excluded from that partial compactification has dimension at most eight. A projective complete intersection of nine general hyperplanes can avoid it. Voisin uses such complete intersections to force a hypothetical second branch of the period map to meet the plane divisor, where the degree-one conclusion is already known. This repairs the use of properness in the final step of the original paper. It is a different proof of Torelli, not an application of the later complete image theorem.

    1. The sign of the period inequality.

    Here the pairing is the cubic cup product, so the inequality is negative. On the variety of lines, the Beauville–Bogomolov form is positive on the real plane underlying its holomorphic two-form. The Abel–Jacobi comparison reverses these primitive forms, as explained in Section 2.2.

    2. A prescribed primitive isometry.

    The primitive isometry \(-\operatorname{id}_{L_0}\) preserves every period line but acts nontrivially on \(A_{L_0}\text{;}\) it cannot extend while fixing \(c\text{.}\) Replacing a primitive isometry with its negative can correct this discriminant action. This preserves its projective period but changes the prescribed map. Using the larger orthogonal group in a quotient also adds an ineffective central involution to its stabilizers.

    3. The information in a derivative.

    Local Torelli excludes an infinitesimal deformation with stationary period. It does not exclude two distant marked cubics having the same period. That is the additional assertion of global Torelli.

    4. What the cubic period determines.

    The period together with the stated integral data determines the cubic up to projective isomorphism. This is a classification statement about isomorphisms. It gives no criterion for whether that cubic is birational to projective space.

    5. Recovering the ambient geometry.

    A point of an abstract fourfold does not come with a line in projective five-space. Recovering the Grassmannian restores the interpretation of its points as two-dimensional vector subspaces. The Plücker polarization makes this reconstruction possible.

    6. The class that makes Torelli geometric.

    A parallel transport Hodge isometry can give birational holomorphic symplectic manifolds whose Kähler cones differ. Mapping an ample class to an ample class is what upgrades the relevant conclusion to an isomorphism. In this argument that class is the Plücker class.

    7. Why the moduli component is specified.

    Marked holomorphic symplectic moduli can have nonseparated points. The Torelli statement used for its fibres concerns one connected component; changing an arbitrary marking is not a way to check that component. The path construction above instead supplies the required transport directly.
