    Chapter 4 The Torelli theorem

    Section 4.1 Markings and the period domain

    The period of a cubic fourfold records its Hodge decomposition inside an integral lattice. A marking specifies that lattice identification. Fix a pair \((L,c)\) isometric to \((H^4(X,\mathbb Z),h^2)\) for one smooth cubic, with the cup-product form. Thus \(L\) has signature \((21,2)\text{,}\) \(c^2=3\text{,}\) and \(L_0=c^\perp\) has signature \((20,2)\text{.}\) A marking of another cubic is an integral isometry \(\mu:H^4(X,\mathbb Z)\to L\) sending \(h^2\) to \(c\text{.}\) Two marked cubics are equivalent when a projective isomorphism between them respects their markings on cohomology.
    For a marked cubic, write \(\mu(H^{3,1}(X))=\mathbb C\omega\text{.}\) Its period belongs to
    \begin{gather*} \mathcal D=\{[\omega]\in\mathbb P(L_0\otimes\mathbb C):(\omega,\omega)=0,\;(\omega,\overline\omega)\lt0\}. \end{gather*}
    This space has two connected components. If \(\omega=a+ib\text{,}\) the equations say that \(a,b\) are orthogonal real vectors of equal negative square. Their ordered span is an oriented negative definite two-plane. Multiplying \(\omega\) by a nonzero complex number preserves that orientation; complex conjugation reverses it. Choose a component \(\mathcal D^+\text{.}\)
    1
    The line determines the remaining Hodge summands. Its complex conjugate gives \(H^{1,3}\text{,}\) and the orthogonal complement of their sum inside \(L_0\otimes\mathbb C\) gives the primitive \((2,2)\) part. Adding \(\mathbb Cc\) restores full middle cohomology. Thus recording a period line does not discard the other Hodge pieces. What it does discard, unless the marking is retained, is the specified identification of this decomposition with the integral cohomology of a particular cubic.
    Parallel transport in the universal family of smooth cubic equations acts on \(L\text{.}\) It preserves \(c\) and the component of the period domain. Beauville’s monodromy theorem identifies its image with the group
    \begin{gather*} \Gamma=O^+(L,c)\simeq\widetilde O^+(L_0). \end{gather*}
    The superscript means preservation of \(\mathcal D^+\text{.}\) The tilde means trivial action on the discriminant group \(A_{L_0}=L_0^\vee/L_0\simeq\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\text{.}\) The equality is a theorem about the image of geometric parallel transport, beyond the definition of a lattice isometry [Bea86, Theorem 2]. These conventions agree with [Laz10, Notation 2.12].
    The discriminant condition expresses an integral extension requirement. The full lattice is an overlattice of \(\mathbb Zc\perp L_0\) of index three. Its gluing fixes how their fractional classes fit together. An isometry of \(L_0\) extends to an isometry of \(L\) fixing \(c\) exactly when it acts trivially on \(A_{L_0}\text{.}\) Preserving the primitive bilinear form alone does not specify this extension.
    For period orbits there is a useful simplification. One has \(O^+(L_0)=\{\pm1\}\times\Gamma\text{,}\) and \(-1\) acts trivially on the projective domain. Hence quotienting \(\mathcal D^+\) by either group gives the same coarse period space. The group \(\Gamma\) retains the full marking convention.2

    Section 4.2 Local Torelli from the Jacobian ring

    Let \(\mathcal M^{\mathrm{mar}}\) be the analytic moduli space of marked smooth cubics, and let \(p:\mathcal M^{\mathrm{mar}}\to\mathcal D\) send \((X,\mu)\) to its period line. The calculation in Section 1.4 gives a twenty-dimensional deformation space. The domain also has dimension twenty. Equality of dimensions does not yet prove that the period map detects a deformation; its derivative must be computed.
    Write \(X=V(f)\) and let \(R_f\) be its Jacobian ring. The residue identifications in Section 1.2 give \(H^{3,1}(X)\simeq(R_f)_0\) and \(H^{2,2}(X)_{\mathrm{prim}}\simeq(R_f)_3\text{.}\) The tangent space of deformations is \(H^1(X,\mathcal T_X)\simeq(R_f)_3\text{.}\) For a cubic polynomial \(\dot f\) representing a deformation direction, differentiation gives
    \begin{gather*} \left.\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\Omega}{(f+t\dot f)^2}\right|_{t=0}=-\frac{2\dot f\Omega}{f^3}. \end{gather*}
    Passing to residues and to the quotient of the Hodge filtration computes the infinitesimal movement of the period line. Changes of coordinates give the Jacobian relations, so the derivative depends on the class of \(\dot f\) in \((R_f)_3\text{.}\)
    3

    Proposition 4.1.

    The period map for marked smooth cubic fourfolds is a local biholomorphism.

    Proof.
    The tangent space of the period quadric at \([\omega]\) is \(\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb C\omega,\omega^\perp/\mathbb C\omega)\text{.}\) Under the Hodge decomposition, the second factor is \(H^{2,2}(X)_{\mathrm{prim}}\text{.}\) The derivative just computed is, up to the nonzero scalar \(-2\text{,}\) multiplication \((R_f)_3\otimes(R_f)_0\to(R_f)_3\text{.}\) Since \((R_f)_0=\mathbb C\text{,}\) this is an isomorphism. The holomorphic inverse function theorem proves the assertion.

    Section 4.3 The global statement

    The strong statement specifies the cohomology map that the geometric isomorphism must induce. It is stated at the beginning of Voisin’s paper, with the correction to its proof discussed below [Voi86, p. 577], [Voi08, Theorem 1]. Charles uses the same form in [Cha12, Theorem 1].

    Theorem 4.2.

    Let \(X,X'\) be smooth complex cubic fourfolds. Suppose that an integral cup-product isometry \(\Phi:H^4(X,\mathbb Z)\to H^4(X',\mathbb Z)\) preserves the Hodge decomposition and sends \(h_X^2\) to \(h_{X'}^2\text{.}\) There is a projective isomorphism \(f:X'\to X\) such that \(f^*=\Phi\text{.}\)
    For the marked period map, this says that equal periods give equivalent marked cubics. To see the prescribed map in this formulation, choose a marking \(\mu\) of \(X\) and mark \(X'\) by \(\mu'=\mu\Phi^{-1}\text{.}\) The Hodge condition makes their period lines equal. Equality of the marked points gives \(\mu'f^*=\mu\text{,}\) which is exactly \(f^*=\Phi\text{.}\) Conversely, a projective isomorphism respecting markings identifies their periods.
    No separate orientation hypothesis is required in the displayed theorem. A Hodge isometry sends the complex line \(H^{3,1}\) to the corresponding complex line, so it preserves the induced orientation of the negative real two-plane. The integral condition and preservation of \(h^2\) remain essential: a rational Hodge comparison, or a comparison of transcendental parts alone, does not supply the asserted full lattice map.4
    After passing to unmarked cubics, local and global Torelli give an open embedding of the coarse moduli space \(\mathcal C\) into \(\Gamma\backslash\mathcal D^+\text{.}\) Global Torelli proves injectivity; local Torelli supplies the local inverse. The description of exactly which period points occur requires the image theorem in the next chapter.

    Section 4.4 Recovering the cubic from its polarized variety of lines

    Charles’s proof uses the variety of lines to compare cubic periods with holomorphic symplectic periods. The geometric reconstruction requires its Plücker polarization \(g\text{.}\) An isomorphism of abstract varieties of lines is a weaker datum than a polarized isomorphism.
    Let \(G=\operatorname{Gr}(2,V)\subset\mathbb P(\bigwedge^2V)\text{,}\) so that \(F(X)\subset G\text{.}\) Altman and Kleiman prove two facts about this embedding: its hyperplane system is complete, and every quadric containing \(F(X)\) contains \(G\) [AK77, Theorem 1.16(ii)–(iii)]. Since the Grassmannian itself is cut out by the Plücker quadrics, these facts recover it as the common zero locus of all quadrics through the embedded variety of lines.
    The quadratic equations have a direct interpretation. A bivector represents a two-dimensional subspace exactly when it is decomposable. If \(p_{ij}\) are its coefficients in a basis of \(\bigwedge^2V\text{,}\) one of the required equations is
    \begin{gather*} p_{12}p_{34}-p_{13}p_{24}+p_{14}p_{23}=0. \end{gather*}
    The equations for all four-element index sets express that the bivector has square zero in \(\bigwedge^4V\text{.}\) They distinguish the Grassmannian from the whole projective space. The additional assertion about \(F(X)\) is that restricting to the cubic’s contained lines introduces no new quadratic equations.
    5

    Proposition 4.3.

    A polarized isomorphism between \((F(X),g_X)\) and \((F(X'),g_{X'})\text{,}\) for smooth complex cubic fourfolds, is induced by a projective isomorphism between the cubics.

    Proof.
    Completeness of the hyperplane system extends the polarized isomorphism to a projective transformation of the Plücker ambient spaces. The equality of spaces of quadrics carries \(\operatorname{Gr}(2,6)\) to itself. Chow’s theorem on Grassmannians makes this transformation come from a projective transformation of \(\mathbb P^5\text{.}\) There is no additional duality automorphism in this case, since the vector-space dimension is six rather than four.
    This transformation sends every line of \(X\) to a line of \(X'\text{.}\) The cubic is the union of these lines. For example, through a smooth point the directions of contained lines satisfy a quadratic and a cubic equation in the projectivized tangent space \(\mathbb P^3\text{;}\) these equations have a common projective zero. The transformation therefore sends \(X\) to \(X'\text{.}\) This is the reconstruction in [Cha12, Proposition 4].

    Section 4.5 From symplectic periods to cubic periods

    The holomorphic symplectic Torelli theorem has qualifications involving connected components, parallel transport, and Kähler cones. It cannot be applied by declaring every Hodge isometry to be a geometric isomorphism. Charles instead compares marked deformation spaces and uses generic injectivity followed by separatedness.
    The comparison uses more than the equality of Hodge numbers. Beauville–Donagi’s Abel–Jacobi map is an isomorphism of integral cohomology groups, with Hodge indices lowered by \((1,1)\) [BD85, Proposition 4]. Its primitive form has the negative sign. On the full groups, orthogonal decomposition over \(\mathbb Q\) gives
    \begin{gather*} q_F(\operatorname{AJ}(a),\operatorname{AJ}(b))=-(a,b)_X+(a,h^2)_X(b,h^2)_X. \end{gather*}
    Here we use \(\operatorname{AJ}(h^2)=g\) and \(q_F(g)=6\text{.}\) This identity explains why a full cubic marking preserving \(h^2\) determines a compatible lattice marking of \(H^2(F(X),\mathbb Z)\) fixing \(g\text{.}\)
    Let \(\mathcal M^{\mathrm{symp}}\) denote the marked polarized holomorphic symplectic deformation space containing these varieties of lines. Compatibility means that the two markings agree through Abel–Jacobi on primitive cohomology, using the sign convention just specified. The incidence space \(\mathcal N\) parametrizes a marked cubic and its compatibly marked variety of lines. Its two projections to \(\mathcal M^{\mathrm{mar}}\) and \(\mathcal M^{\mathrm{symp}}\) are local isomorphisms, and both commute with the period maps. The first projection has a unique compatible marking. The second is injective by polarized reconstruction [Cha12, Section 2 and Corollary 5].6
    For a very general polarized period, the only integral classes of type \((1,1)\) on the symplectic side are multiples of \(g\text{.}\) Its square is positive. Verbitsky’s Torelli theorem and the description of nonseparated points then give a single marked point in each connected component over this period. Markman’s formulation is [Mar11, Theorem 2.2(5)]; the consequence used by Charles is [Cha12, Proposition 3]. Via the incidence space, the marked cubic period map is generically injective on each component.
    The passage from generic to everywhere injective uses the separatedness of the marked cubic moduli space. Suppose two distinct marked cubics in one component had the same period. They have disjoint small neighborhoods, and local Torelli maps both neighborhoods biholomorphically to open subsets of the domain. Their overlapping period neighborhoods would give two preimages of every sufficiently general nearby period, contradicting generic injectivity. The very general periods used here are dense: an open ball cannot be covered by the countably many proper Hodge hyperplanes where additional integral classes become algebraic. Thus an overlap contains a period at which the symplectic fibre is a single marked point. Without separatedness the neighborhoods need not be disjoint, which is precisely the difficulty with general marked holomorphic symplectic moduli.
    Beauville’s monodromy theorem supplies the last component check: marked smooth cubic moduli has two connected components, mapped to the two components of \(\mathcal D\text{,}\) with the same component-preserving subgroup. Thus periods cannot coincide across these components. This proves injectivity of the whole marked map and gives the prescribed-isometry statement. Charles records these steps in [Cha12, Proposition 6 and proof of Theorem 1].

    Section 4.6 Voisin’s geometric proof and its correction

    Voisin’s original proof uses the divisor of cubics containing a plane as a geometric test locus. The decisive conclusions are injectivity in codimension one along that divisor and the assertion that no point outside it has a period lying in its period image. These are restated in [Voi08, Theorem 2]. Extending this information to all cubics requires control of the boundary, because the period map is not proper.
    The correction uses a partial GIT compactification allowing simple singularities, over which the period map extends. Laza’s dimension bound on the remaining locus lets one choose projective complete intersections avoiding that locus. A hypothetical second branch of the period map would then meet the plane divisor and contradict the known degree-one behavior there. This repairs the final global step of the original proof; the Torelli conclusion itself is retained [Voi08, Theorem 3, Proposition 5, and conclusion].

    Section 4.7 Polarizations and categorical Torelli

    For smooth cubic fourfolds \(X\) and \(X'\text{,}\) the reconstruction result of Charles says that an isomorphism of polarized varieties
    \begin{equation*} (F(X),g)\simeq(F(X'),g') \end{equation*}
    is induced by a projective isomorphism of the cubics [Cha12, Proposition 4]. Here the polarization is the Plücker line bundle, whose first Chern class is denoted by \(g\text{.}\) Its square and divisibility, computed in Chapter 2, are six and two.
    The Plücker class also specifies an ample class in the hyperkähler Torelli theorem. For deformation equivalent irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifolds, a parallel transport operator that preserves the integral Hodge structure is induced by an isomorphism precisely when it sends some Kähler class to a Kähler class [Mar11, Theorem 1.3]. The Abel–Jacobi isometry in Section 2.2 identifies the primitive period of the cubic, after the stated Tate twist and change of sign, with the period in \(g^\perp\text{.}\) This explains why the polarized cubic and its polarized Fano variety have the same period domain.7
    The categorical formulation retains a corresponding extra structure. The Kuznetsov component and its rotation functor are constructed in Chapter 6. The rotation \(R_X\text{,}\) also called the degree shift, is tensor product with \(\cO_X(1)\) followed by projection to \(\Ku(X)\text{;}\) it satisfies \(R_X^3\simeq[2]\text{.}\) The Mukai lattice and its algebraic part \(N_X\) are defined in Chapter 7. On the distinguished lattice \(A_2=\langle\lambda_1,\lambda_2\rangle\text{,}\) the induced isometry \(R_X^H\) cyclically permutes the roots \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,-\lambda_1-\lambda_2\text{,}\) while it is the identity on \(A_2^\perp\) [Huy17, Proposition 3.12].

    Theorem 4.4.

    Let \(X\) and \(X'\) be smooth complex cubic fourfolds. They are isomorphic if and only if there is an equivalence \(\Phi:\Ku(X)\xrightarrow{\sim}\Ku(X')\) whose induced isometry \(\Phi^H:N_X\xrightarrow{\sim}N_{X'}\) satisfies
    \begin{equation*} \Phi^H\circ R_X^H=R_{X'}^H\circ\Phi^H. \end{equation*}
    This holds for every smooth cubic fourfold [LPZ20, Corollary 5.1].
    The compatibility preserves the distinguished \(A_2\text{.}\) Over \(\C\text{,}\) this lattice spans the sum of the eigenspaces of \(R_X^H\) for the two primitive cube roots of unity. Its integral structure is recovered by saturation in the Mukai lattice. Commutation on \(N_X\) therefore preserves this sublattice, including when \(N_X\) has rank greater than two. Its orthogonal complement recovers the primitive Hodge structure of the cubic, so the categorical condition is related to the classical Torelli theorem through the integral Mukai lattice. An isomorphism of cubics induces an equivalence with the stronger compatibility \(\Phi\circ R_X\simeq R_{X'}\circ\Phi\text{.}\) The theorem only asks for commutation of the induced actions on \(N_X\text{.}\) An equivalence of the underlying categories alone does not include this condition.
    Huybrechts and Rennemo give another explanation for retaining rotation. They construct a graded algebra of morphisms between the kernels representing its powers. The subalgebra generated in degree one recovers the Jacobian ring of the cubic. A Fourier–Mukai equivalence compatible with rotation at the level of these kernels induces an isomorphism of Jacobian rings, from which the cubic is recovered [HR19, Corollary 3.7, Proposition 3.9, and Corollary 3.10]. The Jacobian ring already described the infinitesimal variation of Hodge structure in Chapter 1. Here its multiplication is recovered from compositions of morphisms, so the argument reconstructs the equation rather than only the tangent space to its deformations. The categorical reconstruction uses the interaction between objects and degree shift, just as the geometric reconstruction uses the Fano variety with its Plücker polarization.

    1. The sign of the period inequality.

    Here the pairing is the cubic cup product, so the inequality is negative. On the variety of lines, the Beauville–Bogomolov form is positive on the real plane underlying its holomorphic two-form. The Abel–Jacobi comparison reverses these primitive forms, as explained in Section 2.2.

    2. A prescribed primitive isometry.

    The primitive isometry \(-\operatorname{id}_{L_0}\) preserves every period line but acts nontrivially on \(A_{L_0}\text{;}\) it cannot extend while fixing \(c\text{.}\) Replacing a primitive isometry with its negative can correct this discriminant action. This preserves its projective period but changes the prescribed map. Using the larger orthogonal group in a quotient also adds an ineffective central involution to its stabilizers.

    3. The information in a derivative.

    Local Torelli excludes an infinitesimal deformation with stationary period. It does not exclude two distant marked cubics having the same period. That is the additional assertion of global Torelli.

    4. What the cubic period determines.

    The period together with the stated integral data determines the cubic up to projective isomorphism. This is a classification statement about isomorphisms. It gives no criterion for whether that cubic is birational to projective space.

    5. Recovering the ambient geometry.

    A point of an abstract fourfold does not come with a line in projective five-space. Recovering the Grassmannian restores the interpretation of its points as two-dimensional vector subspaces. The Plücker polarization makes this reconstruction possible.

    6. Components are part of the comparison.

    The incidence construction follows compatible markings in fixed connected components. An arbitrary abstract isometry between two holomorphic symplectic cohomology lattices need not be parallel transport. Even a parallel-transport Hodge isometry must respect a Kähler class to induce an isomorphism [Mar11, Theorem 1.3].

    7. The specified ample class.

    The reconstruction statement concerns the pair \((F(X),g)\text{.}\) For an abstract isomorphism of Fano varieties, one must check its action on the Plücker class before applying this statement. For a birational map, one must also keep track of the ample cones.
