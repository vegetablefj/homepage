    Appendix A. Lattices, discriminants, and norm conditions

    The arithmetic conditions in the K3 correspondence concern integral lattices. This appendix explains saturation, discriminant gluing, isotropic vectors, and the norm calculation that accounts for the primes congruent to two modulo three. The geometric realization of these lattice conditions is the content of Chapter 3 and Chapter 7.

    Section A.1 Dual lattices and saturation

    A lattice is a free abelian group \(L\) of finite rank with a nondegenerate integral symmetric pairing. Its determinant is the determinant of a Gram matrix, and its discriminant group is
    \begin{equation*} A_L=L^\vee/L, \end{equation*}
    where \(L^\vee=\{x\in L\otimes\Q:(x,L)\subset\Z\}\text{.}\) The order of \(A_L\) is \(|\det L|\text{.}\) A lattice is unimodular if \(L=L^\vee\text{.}\) It is even if \(x^2\) is even for every integral vector \(x\text{.}\)
    For a sublattice \(K\subset L\text{,}\) its saturation is \(\overline K=(K\otimes\Q)\cap L\text{.}\) The embedding is primitive if \(K=\overline K\text{,}\) equivalently if \(L/K\) has no torsion. If \([\overline K:K]=m\text{,}\) a change-of-basis determinant gives
    \begin{equation*} \det K=m^2\det\overline K. \end{equation*}
    Thus two sublattices with the same rational span can have different discriminants. Hassett’s definition uses a saturated label.
    1
    For an even lattice, the pairing induces a finite quadratic form \(q_L:A_L\to\Q/2\Z\text{,}\) given by \(q_L(\bar x)=x^2\bmod2\Z\text{.}\) The group order records the determinant, while the quadratic form retains the self-intersections of fractional classes. Equal ranks, signatures, and determinants do not generally determine an integral lattice.

    Proposition A.1.

    Let \(L\) be unimodular and let \(K\subset L\) be primitive and nondegenerate, with orthogonal complement \(T\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} A_K\simeq A_T. \end{equation*}
    If \(L\) is even, this isomorphism identifies the two discriminant quadratic forms with opposite signs. In particular, \(|\det K|=|\det T|\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Restriction of pairings gives a map \(L\to K^\vee\text{.}\) Every homomorphism \(K\to\Z\) extends to \(L\text{,}\) because \(K\) is primitive and the quotient is free. Unimodularity identifies such extensions with elements of \(L\text{.}\) The map is therefore surjective, with kernel \(T\text{.}\) Reducing modulo \(K\) gives
    \begin{equation*} L/(K\oplus T)\simeq K^\vee/K. \end{equation*}
    Interchanging \(K\) and \(T\) gives the other identification. A glued pair \((x,y)\in K^\vee\oplus T^\vee\) lies in the even lattice \(L\text{,}\) so \(x^2+y^2=0\bmod2\Z\text{.}\) This proves the sign assertion.
    The determinant conclusion also applies to the odd unimodular middle lattice of a cubic fourfold. The statement about quadratic forms modulo \(2\Z\) requires an even ambient lattice; in the odd case the corresponding finite bilinear forms remain available.

    Section A.2 Isotropic vectors and the hyperbolic plane

    The hyperbolic plane \(U\) has a basis \(e,f\) with \(e^2=f^2=0\) and \((e,f)=1\text{.}\) Its Gram matrix is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\text{.}\) For a nonzero vector \(v\in N\text{,}\) its divisibility in \(N\) is the positive generator \(\operatorname{div}_N(v)\) of the ideal \((v,N)\subset\Z\text{.}\)

    Proposition A.2.

    An even lattice \(N\) contains a copy of \(U\) if and only if it contains an isotropic vector \(v\) with \(\operatorname{div}_N(v)=1\text{.}\) Such a vector is primitive.

    Proof.
    The isotropic basis vector \(e\in U\) pairs to one with \(f\text{,}\) so has divisibility one. Conversely, choose \(w\in N\) with \((v,w)=1\text{.}\) Evenness lets us write \(w^2=2a\text{.}\) Then \(w'=w-av\) has square zero and \((v,w')=1\text{.}\) The span of \(v,w'\) is \(U\text{.}\) Divisibility one excludes a nontrivial integral factor of \(v\text{.}\)
    An embedding of \(U\) into an integral lattice is automatically primitive and splits off orthogonally. For \(x\in N\text{,}\) subtract \((x,f)e+(x,e)f\text{;}\) the remainder is integral and orthogonal to \(U\text{.}\) Hence \(N=U\perp U^\perp\text{.}\) This argument uses the unimodularity of \(U\text{.}\)2
    A weaker assertion holds without divisibility one. Suppose that \(N\) is even and nondegenerate and contains a nonzero isotropic vector \(v\text{.}\) Choose \(w\) with \((v,w)=n\ne0\text{,}\) and write \(w^2=2a\text{.}\) The vector \(z=nw-av\) satisfies \(z^2=0\) and \((v,z)=n^2\text{.}\) Thus \(N\) contains a copy of \(U(n^2)\text{,}\) where the pairing of the two isotropic generators is \(n^2\text{.}\) This embedding need not be primitive. Conversely, every embedded \(U(m)\) contains a nonzero isotropic vector. This elementary calculation explains the lattice condition in [Huy17, Lemma 2.6].
    The lattice \(U(2)\) gives an example of the distinction. Its basis vectors are primitive and isotropic, but every pairing is even. It contains no \(U\text{.}\) On a K3 category, the existence of an isotropic direction permits a twisted K3 realization. The existence of \(U\) is the stronger condition for an untwisted realization.

    Section A.3 The two forms of a cubic label

    Let \(c=h^2\) in the middle lattice of a cubic fourfold. Then \(c^2=3\text{,}\) so \(c\) is primitive: an equality \(c=mv\) with \(|m|>1\) would force \(m^2\) to divide three. The vector \(c\) is characteristic, meaning that \(x^2\equiv(x,c)\pmod2\) for every integral middle class \(x\text{.}\) To see this, the tangent sequence gives
    \begin{equation*} c(\mathcal T_X)=\frac{(1+h)^6}{1+3h}, \end{equation*}
    so \(c_1(X)=3h\) and \(c_2(X)=6h^2\text{.}\) Their reductions give \(w_2(X)=h\bmod2\) and \(w_4(X)=0\text{.}\) The oriented Wu formula is \(v_4=w_4+w_2^2\text{,}\) hence \(v_4=h^2\bmod2\text{,}\) as required.
    Extend \(c\) to a basis \(c,t\) of a rank-two label. Write \((c,t)=b\text{.}\) Replacing \(t\) by \(t+mc\) changes \(b\) by a multiple of three; replacing \(t\) by its negative changes its sign. Thus one may arrange \(b=0\) or \(b=1\text{.}\) The characteristic property determines the parity of \(t^2\text{.}\) The two Gram matrices are
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&2k\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{or}\quad\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&2k+1\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Their discriminants are \(6k\) and \(6k+2\text{.}\)
    This proves the congruence restriction \(d\equiv0,2\pmod6\text{.}\) The additional inequality \(d>6\) and the realization of every permitted discriminant by smooth cubics use the image of the cubic period map and Hassett’s existence theorem. A positive rank-two Gram matrix alone does not prove that the corresponding Hodge classes occur on a smooth cubic.3

    Section A.4 Why the primes congruent to two modulo three occur

    Let \(\omega\) be a primitive cube root of unity. The Eisenstein integers \(\Z[\omega]\) have norm
    \begin{equation*} N(a+b\omega)=a^2-ab+b^2. \end{equation*}
    This is the positive quadratic form underlying the \(A_2\) lattice, whose squared lengths are twice these norms. The ring is Euclidean for \(N\text{:}\) after writing a complex number in the real basis \(1,\omega\text{,}\) round both coordinates to integers. The squared norm of the error is at most \(3/4<1\text{.}\) Euclidean division follows, and hence unique factorization.
    A rational prime \(p\equiv2\pmod3\) remains prime in \(\Z[\omega]\text{;}\) its norm is \(p^2\text{.}\) A prime \(p\equiv1\pmod3\) splits as a product of conjugate primes of norm \(p\text{.}\) Finally, three is ramified, and \(N(1-\omega)=3\text{.}\) The inert and split cases follow by factoring \(t^2+t+1\) over \(\mathbb F_p\text{:}\) its nontrivial roots are elements of order three in \(\mathbb F_p^*\text{.}\) In the split case, each prime ideal has quotient of cardinality \(p\text{.}\) Euclideanity makes the ideal principal, so its generator has norm \(p\text{.}\)

    Proposition A.3.

    A positive integer \(m\) is a norm from \(\Z[\omega]\) if and only if \(v_p(m)\) is even for every prime \(p\equiv2\pmod3\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    In an inert prime factor, conjugation fixes the prime, so its contributions to \(z\overline z\) occur with even exponent. There is no restriction at a split prime, since one can use either conjugate factor, or at three, since \(1-\omega\) has norm three. Conversely, factor \(m\) in \(\Z\text{.}\) For each inert factor \(p^{2r}\text{,}\) use \(p^r\text{;}\) for each split factor \(p^r\text{,}\) choose one Eisenstein prime of norm \(p\) and take its \(r\)th power; use the required power of \(1-\omega\) at three. Their product has norm \(m\text{.}\)
    A representation \(m=a^2-ab+b^2\) is primitive when \(\gcd(a,b)=1\text{.}\) This is an additional requirement on the representation.

    Proposition A.4.

    A positive integer \(m\) has a primitive Eisenstein norm representation if and only if no prime \(p\equiv2\pmod3\) divides \(m\) and \(9\nmid m\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    If an inert prime divides \(N(a+b\omega)\text{,}\) reduction to the field \(\mathbb F_{p^2}\) forces \(a+b\omega=0\text{,}\) so it divides both coefficients. If \(9\) divides the norm, reduction modulo three first gives \(b\equiv-a\text{.}\) Writing \(b=-a+3k\) gives \(N(a+b\omega)\equiv3a^2\pmod9\text{,}\) hence three divides both coefficients. Both possibilities contradict primitivity.
    Conversely, factor \(m\) using split primes and at most one factor of three. At each split prime choose only one of the two conjugate factors, with the full required exponent, and multiply these choices. No rational prime divides the resulting Eisenstein integer: at a split prime this would require both conjugate factors, and at three it would require two ramified factors. Thus its two coefficients are coprime.
    Apply these criteria to \(m=d/2\text{.}\) The integral norm condition is precisely the prime-exponent condition for a twisted K3 category. A primitive norm representation excludes the prime two from \(d/2\text{,}\) excludes all odd primes congruent to two modulo three, and excludes a square of three. These are the admissibility restrictions for an untwisted K3 category. Their relation to the saturated rank-three lattice containing \(A_2\) is established in [Huy17, Proposition 2.10] and in the lattice comparison of Chapter 7.4
    For example, \(7=3^2-3+1\) is a primitive norm, giving the admissible discriminant fourteen. The norm \(9=N(3)\) is imprimitive, so eighteen satisfies the twisted condition but not admissibility. Six has an odd valuation at the inert prime two, so the discriminant twelve fails the twisted condition. These computations classify the numerical conditions on the labels. At intersections of Hassett divisors, a different label may provide a different realization.
