Lemma 4.1.

The set \(H_j\) has cardinality \(N_j\text{,}\) and a choice of one section for each distinct initial exponent is a basis of \(R_j\text{.}\) There is a constant \(C>0\text{,}\) independent of \(j\) and of the nonzero section \(s\in R_j\text{,}\) such that
\begin{equation} \mult_x(s)\leq Cj.\tag{4.1} \end{equation}
Moreover, \(H_j+H_q\subset H_{j+q}\text{.}\)

🔗

Proof.
The Taylor expansion map on \(R_j\) is injective. A section whose germ is zero vanishes on a nonempty open subset of the integral variety \(X\text{,}\) and hence vanishes everywhere. Each Taylor coefficient is a scalar. Choose the smallest exponent occurring in the section space and a section with nonzero coefficient there. Eliminate that coefficient by passing to the subspace on which it vanishes. Repeating this operation decreases the dimension by one each time and yields a basis with distinct initial exponents. In a nonzero linear combination of that basis, the smallest initial exponent among its nonzero summands cannot cancel. The set of initial exponents of all sections is therefore exactly the set obtained from the basis.

Its local intersection multiplicity with the divisor is at least \(\mult_x(s)\text{,}\) whereas its total intersection degree is \(jk^{n-1}L^n\text{.}\) Thus one may take \(C=k^{n-1}L^n\text{.}\)
