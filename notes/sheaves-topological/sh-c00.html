    Chapter 0 Conventions and the local-to-global problem

    A sheaf records objects defined on open subsets together with their restrictions and a rule for identifying compatible local objects with a global one. Sections can be functions, solutions of equations, sections of a map, or elements of a module with a prescribed local meaning. Cohomology measures some of the obstructions that remain when a construction is locally possible but cannot be made globally compatible.
    These notes develop sheaves on topological spaces from their definitions through their basic derived operations and cohomology. The constructions use arbitrary spaces whenever possible. Additional topological conditions enter in comparison theorems, the classification of local systems, and specific calculations. They are stated where they are used.

    Section 0.1 Spaces, coefficients, and size

    A space means a topological space, and a map of spaces means a continuous map. A subset is given the subspace topology. A neighbourhood of a point need not be open, but the neighbourhoods indexing a stalk will always be open. Hausdorffness, local compactness, paracompactness, and local connectedness are separate hypotheses. None is included in the word space.
    We fix a universe for sets, indexing families, and open covers. The underlying set of each space and its set of open subsets belong to that universe. Categories of sheaves are formed at the corresponding scale, passing to a larger universe for categories when necessary. Products indexed by all points of a fixed space are therefore set-indexed products.1
    We first distinguish set-valued sheaves from abelian sheaves. For linear operations, \(\Lambda\) is a fixed commutative ring with identity, and modules are unital. The zero ring is allowed. The symbols \(\Set\text{,}\) \(\Ab\text{,}\) and \(\Mod(\Lambda)\) denote sets, abelian groups, and \(\Lambda\)-modules. The notation \(\Sh(X;\Lambda)\) means sheaves of \(\Lambda\)-modules on \(X\text{;}\) \(\Sh(X)\) means sheaves of sets. Taking \(\Lambda=\Z\) recovers abelian sheaves.
    The scalar ring is fixed while the space varies. A sheaf of \(\Lambda\)-modules is equivalently a module over the constant ring sheaf \(\Lambda_X\text{.}\) It is not being treated as a module over a sheaf of smooth or holomorphic functions. These are different linear structures, even when the underlying abelian sheaf is the same.
    We use \(\mathcal F(U)\) and \(\Gamma(U,\mathcal F)\) for sections over an open subset \(U\text{.}\) Restriction to an open subspace is denoted \(\mathcal F|_U\text{;}\) the germ of a section \(s\) at \(x\) is \(s_x\text{.}\) The constant sheaf associated with a set, group, or module \(A\) is \(A_X\text{.}\) A constant presheaf will always be explicitly called a presheaf.

    Section 0.2 Inverse images and the two Hom constructions

    For \(f:X\to Y\text{,}\) the inverse image of a sheaf is written \(f^{-1}\mathcal G\text{,}\) and the direct image is \(f_*\mathcal F\text{.}\) Their adjunction is
    \begin{equation*} \Hom_{\Sh(X;\Lambda)}(f^{-1}\mathcal G,\mathcal F)\simeq\Hom_{\Sh(Y;\Lambda)}(\mathcal G,f_*\mathcal F). \end{equation*}
    In this fixed-coefficient setting, \(f^{-1}\) is exact. It includes the restriction of a sheaf to a subspace, not just to an open subset. The special case of open restriction can also be described directly on sections.
    The symbol \(f^*\) usually denotes inverse image with extension of scalars for a morphism of ringed spaces. Here \(f^{-1}\Lambda_Y\simeq\Lambda_X\text{,}\) so that fixed-coefficient ringed inverse image agrees with \(f^{-1}\text{.}\) We retain the latter notation to make its exactness and its underlying topological meaning visible.2
    For two sheaves of modules, \(\Hom_X(\mathcal F,\mathcal G)\) is the \(\Lambda\)-module of morphisms. The internal Hom \(\sheafHom_\Lambda(\mathcal F,\mathcal G)\) is a sheaf on \(X\text{.}\) Its sections on \(U\) are morphisms between the restrictions to \(U\text{.}\) The identity
    \begin{equation*} \Gamma\bigl(X,\sheafHom_\Lambda(\mathcal F,\mathcal G)\bigr)=\Hom_X(\mathcal F,\mathcal G) \end{equation*}
    relates the two constructions. It does not identify their target categories.
    The symbols \(\RsheafHom_\Lambda\) and \(\RHom_X\) denote derived internal and global Hom. For sheaves placed in degree zero, global Ext is
    \begin{equation*} \Ext_X^n(\mathcal F,\mathcal G)=\Hom_{\D(X;\Lambda)}(\mathcal F,\mathcal G[n]). \end{equation*}
    Sheaf Ext is the cohomology sheaf of derived internal Hom. A local-to-global spectral sequence compares these groups and sheaves in Chapter 8.

    Section 0.3 Complexes, degrees, and derived categories

    Complexes have cohomological indices. A differential has the form \(d^n:K^n\to K^{n+1}\text{.}\) Translation is defined by \(K[r]^n=K^{n+r}\text{,}\) with differential \((-1)^r d_K\text{.}\) Thus \(\mathcal H^n(K[r])=\mathcal H^{n+r}(K)\) for a complex of sheaves. A sheaf regarded as a complex is placed in degree zero unless another degree is stated.
    We write \(\D(X;\Lambda)=\D(\Sh(X;\Lambda))\text{.}\) In \(\D^+(X;\Lambda)\text{,}\) cohomology sheaves vanish in sufficiently negative degrees; in \(\D^-(X;\Lambda)\text{,}\) they vanish in sufficiently positive degrees. The notation \(\D^b(X;\Lambda)\) means bounded cohomology in both directions. Boundedness refers to cohomology; a chosen representative may have terms outside that range.
    For a complex of sheaves \(K\text{,}\) \(\mathcal H^q(K)\) denotes its cohomology sheaf. Its hypercohomology is \(H^n(X,K)=H^n(R\Gamma(X,K))\text{;}\) the \(H^n\) on the right takes cohomology of a complex of modules. A sheaf \(\mathcal F\) in degree zero gives the usual \(H^n(X,\mathcal F)\text{.}\) Some references use \(\mathbb H^n\) to emphasize a complex as coefficient. Cohomology of the raw complex of sections need not equal hypercohomology. A resolution or an acyclicity theorem is needed to make this computation.
    Derived tensor products use the usual signs on cochain complexes and direct sums along diagonals. Hom complexes use products. The accompanying chapter on complexes fixes these signs, and its unbounded resolutions chapter explains the replacement conditions. We apply those results here after verifying the required properties of sheaf categories.
    A quasi-isomorphism is a map of complexes inducing isomorphisms on all cohomology sheaves. On a topological space it can be tested at every stalk, because stalks are exact. An arbitrary complex of injective sheaves need not be a replacement suitable for all unbounded calculations; K-injectivity supplies the needed condition. The distinction is part of Chapter 7.

    Section 0.4 Dependencies and sources

    Chapter 1 through Chapter 6 establish the sheaf-theoretic constructions. Chapter 7 and Chapter 8 apply homological algebra. Chapter 9 compares Čech and derived cohomology. The last two chapters give low-degree interpretations, calculations, and comparison with singular cohomology. Ordinary category theory, modules, and the stated homological algebra results are prerequisites; Grothendieck topologies are not.
    The principal topological references are [Bre97, Chapters I–III], [Ive86, Chapter II], and [KS90, Chapter II]. Their global conventions differ. A theorem stated under local compactness or finite homological dimension in one source may have a proof that does not need those assumptions, but removing them requires a separate check.
    For individual constructions and precise general hypotheses, we also use [Stacks, Sheaves on Spaces and Cohomology of Sheaves]. General-site statements are used only after specializing them to the open subsets of a space. [KS06, Chapters 16–18] and [Sch26] provide comparisons with that broader language. A reference to a chapter identifies the source of an exposition; a technical existence or comparison theorem is cited more precisely where it is used.
    Proofs are included when they explain a construction, its naturality, or the role of a hypothesis. General homological algebra has its own development. Here the statements used from it are recalled with their applicable assumptions and a reference. Footnotes clarify terminology, notation, and useful variants. They do not carry assumptions needed for the truth of a displayed result.
    Support conditions, direct image with proper support, extraordinary inverse image, and Verdier duality form a further topological development. General sites and variable structure sheaves form another one. The present results provide their sheaf and cohomology prerequisites.
