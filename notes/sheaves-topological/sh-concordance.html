    Appendix A. Terminology and hypotheses

    This appendix collects distinctions used in the statements of the note. The references lead to definitions, proofs, and examples; the conventions listed here do not add hypotheses to those statements.

    Section A.1 Objects and notation

    Associated sheaf. The notation \(aF\) in Definition 2.6 and \(F^\#\) in the inverse-image construction denote sheafification. The two constructions agree by their universal property. A presheaf-to-sheaf map that induces isomorphisms on stalks need not be a sectionwise isomorphism before sheafification; see Proposition 2.7 and Proposition 2.11.
    Constant and locally constant. The constant sheaf \(M_X\) consists of locally constant functions to the discrete module \(M\text{.}\) Its sections on a disconnected open set need not be one copy of \(M\text{.}\) A locally constant sheaf has constant models locally, with possibly nontrivial monodromy globally. Isomorphic stalks do not supply these local trivializations or determine the gluing. See Definition 5.1, Definition 5.4, and the examples in Section 5.5.
    Local system and finite rank. Here a local system means an arbitrary locally constant \(\Lambda\)-module sheaf. Finite rank over a field, finite local freeness over a ring, and finite generation of a local coefficient module are separate conditions. Some sources incorporate finiteness into the words local system. Our finite-rank assertions state it explicitly; over the zero ring rank is not an invariant. Morphisms between unrestricted constant module sheaves can also require a distinction between local constancy after evaluation on each element and local constancy of the entire homomorphism; see Proposition 5.5.
    Étale terminology. An étalé space in Theorem 2.13 is a local homeomorphism over \(X\text{.}\) A covering map requires simultaneous trivialization of the entire inverse image of a neighborhood, and corresponds to a locally constant sheaf of sets; see Proposition 5.9. These terms do not introduce the étale topology of a scheme.

    Section A.2 Operations and exactness

    Inverse images. The notation \(f^{-1}G\) always includes sheafification. The intermediate presheaf is denoted \(f^\dagger G\text{;}\) see Definition 4.2. Some topological sources write \(f^*\) for our exact inverse image. A general ringed inverse image additionally extends scalars and need not be exact. Fixed coefficients satisfy \(f^{-1}\Lambda_Y\simeq\Lambda_X\text{,}\) so the two functors coincide in this special case. We retain \(f^{-1}\) to display that setting.
    Internal and global Hom. The module \(\Hom_X(F,G)\) consists of global sheaf morphisms; the sheaf \(\sheafHom_\Lambda(F,G)\) assigns morphisms of restrictions to each open set. Derived global Hom \(\RHom_X(K,L)\) is an object of \(\D(\Lambda)\text{;}\) derived internal Hom \(\RsheafHom_\Lambda(K,L)\) is an object of \(\D(X;\Lambda)\text{.}\) Their cohomologies are global Ext groups and Ext sheaves, respectively. Derived global sections relate them in Proposition 8.14. The stalk of internal Hom or an Ext sheaf need not be Hom or Ext of the stalks; see Definition 6.5 and Definition 8.13.
    Exactness. Exactness of module sheaves is stalkwise exactness, not exactness of their sections on every open set. Kernels are sectionwise kernels; cokernels require sheafification. An epimorphism therefore permits local lifts but need not permit a global lift. Global sections and direct image are left exact; inverse image for fixed coefficients is exact. See Section 3.3, Proposition 4.5, and the connecting classes in Section 10.1.
    Flabby, injective, and acyclic. Flabby and flasque mean the same extension property for sections. Injectivity is an extension property for morphisms across arbitrary monomorphisms. An injective sheaf is flabby; flabby sheaves are acyclic for sections on every open set, but need not be injective. The Godement resolution is flabby, rather than automatically injective. For unbounded complexes, injective or acyclic terms alone do not replace the K-injective condition. See Proposition 7.5, Proposition 7.7, and Example 7.15.

    Section A.3 Cohomology and covering choices

    Three cohomology constructions. The groups \(\check H^n(\mathcal U,F)\) belong to a fixed covering. The groups \(\check H^n(X,F)\) are their colimit under refinement. Derived sheaf cohomology is \(H^n(X,F)=H^n(R\Gamma(X,F))\text{.}\) These definitions are distinct. Acyclicity of every finite intersection gives an all-degree fixed-cover comparison. On arbitrary spaces, the all-cover comparison is an isomorphism in degrees zero and one and an injection in degree two; see Theorem 9.7, Corollary 9.8, and Theorem 9.10.
    Complexes and sheaves. Hypercohomology means cohomology of derived sections of a complex; we write \(H^n(X,K)\) for it. This is a module, whereas \(\mathcal H^n(K)\) is a sheaf. Bounded-below hypotheses on the spectral sequences in Theorem 8.8 ensure the stated convergence. Existence of unbounded derived sections does not remove those hypotheses.

    Section A.4 Which topology assumptions are being used?

    The basic theory uses arbitrary topological spaces. The monodromy classification in Theorem 5.12 adds local path connectedness and semilocal simple connectedness; the fundamental-group form also chooses a point in a path-connected space.
    The explicit local-coefficient singular comparison in Theorem 11.8 assumes a basis of contractible neighborhoods and paracompact Hausdorff open subspaces. Local contractibility gives exactness on stalks; the covering condition supports the flabby-resolution proof. Broader constant-coefficient comparison theorems in Theorem 11.11 replace these assumptions with a specified local vanishing condition. They do not justify deleting assumptions from the earlier proof.
    Homotopy invariance in Theorem 11.14 holds on arbitrary spaces for locally constant sheaves, independently of singular comparison. A homotopy supplies an isomorphism between the two pullback coefficient sheaves; the induced maps agree after that transport. Arbitrary sheaves do not have such transport, as the skyscraper counterexample Example 11.18 shows. Contractibility consequently gives acyclicity for constant coefficients, not for every sheaf.
