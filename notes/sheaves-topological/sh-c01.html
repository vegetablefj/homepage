    Chapter 1 Presheaves, sheaves, and local data

    A function can be specified on an open cover if the local functions agree on overlaps. The same principle applies to many objects whose sections are not functions on the base. The definition of a sheaf records precisely this agreement and gluing. The open cover, its overlaps, and the restriction maps are all part of the data.
    This chapter treats sets and modules in parallel. Set-valued sheaves give the general local construction; modules add algebra needed for cohomology. No separation or compactness assumption on the space is used.

    Section 1.1 Restrictions and morphisms

    Definition 1.1.

    Let \(X\) be a space. Write \(\operatorname{Op}(X)\) for the category whose objects are open subsets and whose arrows are inclusions. A presheaf of sets is a functor \(\mathcal F:\operatorname{Op}(X)^{\op}\to\Set\text{.}\) A presheaf of \(\Lambda\)-modules has target \(\Mod(\Lambda)\text{.}\) For \(V\subseteq U\text{,}\) write \(\rho^U_V:\mathcal F(U)\to\mathcal F(V)\) for restriction and \(s|_V\) for \(\rho^U_V(s)\text{.}\) Functoriality means
    \begin{equation*} \rho^U_U=\id,\qquad \rho^V_W\rho^U_V=\rho^U_W\quad(W\subseteq V\subseteq U). \end{equation*}
    A morphism is a natural transformation. The corresponding presheaf categories are \(\PSh(X)\) and \(\PSh(X;\Lambda)\text{.}\)
    A section is an element of \(\mathcal F(U)\text{.}\) It is not assumed to be a function from \(U\text{.}\) For example, sections may be equivalence classes of functions or sections of a map with nontrivial fibres. A morphism \(u:\mathcal F\to\mathcal G\) assigns maps \(u_U\) compatible with restrictions. Compatibility on every inclusion is necessary even if the two presheaves have isomorphic values on every open set.
    Restriction maps need not be injective or surjective. Distinct functions on \(U\) may agree on a smaller open set. A function on a smaller open set may have no extension to \(U\text{.}\) These possibilities will remain present for sheaves.
    The empty open subset is an object of \(\operatorname{Op}(X)\text{.}\) We impose no normalization on the value of a presheaf at the empty set. For a sheaf, this value is forced by the empty cover below.1
    Limits and colimits of presheaves are computed on each open subset, since these are functor categories. The same statement for sheaves is only partly true: limits remain sectionwise, whereas colimits generally require sheafification. The distinction is proved in Chapter 3.

    Section 1.2 Locality and gluing

    Definition 1.3.

    Let \(\mathcal F\) be a presheaf of sets or modules on \(X\text{,}\) and let \(U=\bigcup_{i\in I}U_i\) be an open cover. A family \((s_i)\text{,}\) with \(s_i\in\mathcal F(U_i)\text{,}\) is matching if \(s_i|_{U_i\cap U_j}=s_j|_{U_i\cap U_j}\) for all \(i,j\text{.}\) The presheaf is separated if a section on \(U\) is determined by all its restrictions to any such cover. It is a sheaf if every matching family is the family of restrictions of a unique section on \(U\text{.}\) Empty covers are included. Morphisms of sheaves are presheaf morphisms; \(\Sh(X)\) and \(\Sh(X;\Lambda)\) are the resulting full subcategories.

    Proposition 1.4.

    A presheaf of sets or modules is a sheaf if and only if, for every open cover of every \(U\text{,}\) the diagram
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal F(U)\longrightarrow\prod_i\mathcal F(U_i)\rightrightarrows\prod_{i,j}\mathcal F(U_i\cap U_j) \end{equation*}
    is an equalizer. The two parallel maps restrict from the \(i\)-th and \(j\)-th factors. In the module case the condition is equivalently exactness of
    \begin{equation*} 0\longrightarrow\mathcal F(U)\longrightarrow\prod_i\mathcal F(U_i)\xrightarrow{\delta}\prod_{i,j}\mathcal F(U_i\cap U_j), \end{equation*}
    where \(\delta(s)_{ij}=s_j|_{U_i\cap U_j}-s_i|_{U_i\cap U_j}\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    The equalizer is the set or module of matching families. The asserted isomorphism to it is precisely existence and uniqueness of gluing. For modules, an equalizer of two linear maps is the kernel of their difference.
    For the empty cover of the empty set, the products are terminal objects. Thus a sheaf of sets has exactly one section on the empty set; an abelian or module sheaf has value zero there. A separated set-valued presheaf has at most one section on the empty set. The existence of this section is part of the sheaf condition.
    If \(U\) is a disjoint union of open subsets \(U_i\text{,}\) matching imposes no condition beyond the unique restrictions to the empty set. Hence \(\mathcal F(U)\simeq\prod_i\mathcal F(U_i)\text{.}\) An infinite disjoint union gives a product, not a direct sum. This difference already explains several failures of naive sheaf constructions.

    Proposition 1.5.

    Let \(\mathcal F,\mathcal G\) be sheaves. A family of maps between their sections on open subsets, defined locally on an open cover of \(X\) and compatible on overlaps and with restriction, glues uniquely to a sheaf morphism \(\mathcal F\to\mathcal G\text{.}\) If two sheaf morphisms agree on the members of an open cover, they agree globally.

    Proof.
    For \(s\in\mathcal F(U)\text{,}\) apply the local maps to \(s|_{U\cap U_i}\text{.}\) Compatibility makes these images a matching family in \(\mathcal G\text{,}\) which has a unique gluing. The gluing respects restriction and the algebraic operations, because these identities can be tested on the cover. Uniqueness follows from separatedness of \(\mathcal G\text{.}\)
    A property of sections is local if it can be checked after restriction to an open cover. This assertion includes both directions: a section with the property restricts to sections with the property, and compatible sections with the property glue to one having it. When only the first direction holds, the resulting subpresheaf need not be a sheaf.

    Section 1.3 Functions and sections of maps

    Example 1.6.

    For any space \(T\text{,}\) the assignment \(U\mapsto C(U,T)\) of continuous maps to \(T\) is a sheaf of sets. If \(T\) is a topological abelian group or a topological \(\Lambda\)-module with continuous operations, it is a sheaf with that algebraic structure.
    Solution.
    Matching maps have a unique set-theoretic gluing. The gluing is continuous because continuity is local on an open cover of the domain. The operations are pointwise, and equality of the required identities is also local.
    On a smooth manifold, smooth real-valued functions form a sheaf; on a complex manifold, holomorphic functions do so. These examples use the corresponding local regularity theorem, not a separation hypothesis on a general sheaf. Their underlying real or complex vector spaces can be studied here without developing their module theory over a variable function sheaf.

    Example 1.7.

    Let \(p:E\to X\) be continuous. The assignment
    \begin{equation*} U\longmapsto\{s:U\to E\text{ continuous}:p\circ s=\id_U\} \end{equation*}
    is a sheaf of sets, called the sheaf of sections of \(p\text{.}\)
    Solution.
    Compatible local sections glue as continuous maps. Their composite with \(p\) is the identity because that equality holds on the covering opens.
    There is no local triviality assumption on \(p\text{.}\) Its sheaf of sections nevertheless exists. Conversely, a later construction realizes every set-valued sheaf as the sheaf of sections of a particular local homeomorphism. This converse does require the topology on the total space to be chosen from germs, rather than from an arbitrary bundle model.

    Example 1.8.

    Let \(u:\mathcal F\to\mathcal G\) be a morphism of sheaves of modules. Then \(U\mapsto\ker(u_U)\) is a sheaf. More generally, for two set-valued morphisms \(u,v:\mathcal F\to\mathcal G\text{,}\) their sectionwise equalizer is a sheaf.
    Solution.
    Glue a matching family in the equalizer as sections of \(\mathcal F\text{.}\) Its two images agree locally and therefore agree in \(\mathcal G\text{.}\) The glued section lies in the equalizer, with uniqueness inherited from \(\mathcal F\text{.}\)
    This gives a general reason that solutions of a linear local equation form a sheaf when its operator is a morphism of sheaves. For instance, the derivative of a smooth function restricts compatibly to smaller opens, and its kernel consists of locally constant functions. A condition on the existence of a global primitive is a different matter: primitives on two open sets may differ by a nonzero constant on their overlap.

    Example 1.9.

    For an open subset \(W\subseteq X\text{,}\) define \(h_W(U)\) to be a singleton if \(U\subseteq W\) and empty otherwise. Then \(h_W\) is a sheaf. It is represented by \(W\) in the category of open subsets.
    Solution.
    A section on each member of a cover exists exactly when every member is contained in \(W\text{,}\) which is equivalent to their union being contained in \(W\text{.}\) If it exists, it is unique.
    Representable sheaves turn statements about maps into statements about sections: \(\Hom(h_U,\mathcal F)\simeq\mathcal F(U)\text{.}\) A map takes the unique section of \(h_U(U)\) to a section of \(\mathcal F(U)\text{,}\) and restrictions determine all its other components.2

    Section 1.4 Conditions that do not glue

    Example 1.10.

    The bounded continuous real-valued functions on each open subset form a separated presheaf on \(\R\text{,}\) but not a sheaf.
    Solution.
    Restrictions of bounded functions are bounded, and continuous functions agreeing on a cover are equal. On the cover \(\R=\bigcup_{n\geq1}(-n,n)\text{,}\) the functions \(t\mapsto t\) are bounded on each member and agree on all overlaps. Their only possible gluing is the unbounded function \(t\mapsto t\text{.}\)
    The missing ingredient is a single global bound. A bound that may vary with the member of the cover is a local condition. The distinction is not repaired by checking only finite covers, since the sheaf condition is imposed on arbitrary covers.

    Example 1.11.

    On an infinite discrete space \(X\text{,}\) the functions \(U\to\Z\) with finite image form a separated presheaf that is not a sheaf.
    Solution.
    Every function on a singleton has finite image. A family assigning distinct integers to countably many different points is a matching family on the singleton cover, but its gluing has infinite image.
    Likewise, on a countably infinite discrete space, finitely supported functions with values in a nonzero module do not form a sheaf. Their value on the whole space is a direct sum, whereas the singleton cover requires the product of the point values. The sheaf of all functions has that product.

    Example 1.12.

    The constant presheaf with value a set \(A\) takes \(A\) on every open subset and uses identity restriction maps. Its associated sheaf has a different description: it consists of locally constant \(A\)-valued functions. If \(A\) has at least two elements and \(U\) is a union of two nonempty disjoint open subsets, the constant presheaf fails the gluing condition on that cover after normalization at the empty set.
    Solution.
    For the normalized presheaf, choose different constant values on the two components. The restrictions agree on the empty intersection, but no single element of \(A\) gives both values. The unnormalized constant functor already fails the required value on the empty set unless \(A\) is a singleton. The locally constant functions do glue, because local constancy is local.
    Both conventions yield the same associated constant sheaf. The universal property is established in Chapter 2, and local systems are studied in Chapter 5.3
    Sheafification repairs gluing, not global analytic bounds. For bounded continuous functions, every continuous function is locally bounded, so sheafification recovers all continuous functions. For finite-image functions, it recovers locally finite-image functions; on a discrete space these are all functions. These assertions follow from the local description of sheafification in Section 2.3.

    Section 1.5 Sheaves on a basis

    A basis can reduce the number of opens needed to specify a sheaf. Its elements need not be closed under intersections. If \(B,C\) belong to the basis, their intersection is covered by basis elements, and compatibility has to be checked on those elements.4

    Definition 1.13.

    Let \(\mathcal B\) be a basis for \(X\text{.}\) A presheaf on \(\mathcal B\) assigns sets or modules to basis opens, with restriction maps for inclusions between them. It is a sheaf on the basis if, for every cover \(B=\bigcup_i B_i\) by basis opens, a family \(s_i\in\mathcal F(B_i)\) glues uniquely whenever its restrictions agree on every basis open contained in \(B_i\cap B_j\text{.}\) If the empty set belongs to the basis, the empty cover is included.

    Proposition 1.14.

    Restriction to \(\mathcal B\) induces an equivalence between sheaves on \(X\) and sheaves on \(\mathcal B\text{.}\) For a sheaf \(\mathcal F\) on the basis, the extension to an open \(U\) consists of the compatible families
    \begin{equation*} \widetilde{\mathcal F}(U)=\left\{(s_B)_{B\subseteq U}:s_B\in\mathcal F(B),\ s_B|_C=s_C\text{ for }C\subseteq B\subseteq U,\ B,C\in\mathcal B\right\}. \end{equation*}
    The same construction works for morphisms and for module-valued sheaves.

    Proof.
    Restriction of a sheaf satisfies the basis condition: agreement on basis subopens of an intersection implies agreement on the whole intersection, by separatedness. A matching family on a basis cover therefore glues in the original sheaf.
    Now start with a sheaf on the basis. A compatible family on \(U\) restricts by forgetting components. If \(U\in\mathcal B\text{,}\) its component at \(U\) determines all the others, so extension followed by restriction recovers \(\mathcal F\text{.}\)
    To check the sheaf condition for the extension, suppose compatible families are given on an arbitrary open cover \(U=\bigcup_i U_i\text{,}\) agreeing on overlaps. For each basis open \(B\subseteq U\text{,}\) cover \(B\) by basis opens contained in some \(U_i\cap B\text{.}\) The given local components agree on basis refinements of intersections. The basis sheaf condition gives a unique \(s_B\in\mathcal F(B)\text{.}\) Its restrictions to smaller basis opens are the corresponding components, by uniqueness. Thus the \(s_B\) form a compatible family on \(U\text{.}\) The same argument gives uniqueness of its gluing.
    Finally, a sheaf on \(X\) is recovered from all its restrictions to basis opens: compatible sections on them glue on their union. These constructions commute with morphisms, so the two restriction and extension functors are quasi-inverse.
    Basis neighbourhoods of a point are cofinal among all open neighbourhoods. Consequently they compute the same stalk. Using a basis is therefore compatible both with the gluing description above and with the germ description in the next chapter.
    For a basis closed under finite intersections, compatibility takes the simpler form of equality on \(B_i\cap B_j\) itself. The more general definition is useful for balls, local coordinate neighbourhoods, and other bases whose intersections need not have the chosen shape. Replacing the intersection by a basis refinement is part of the definition, not an extra geometric assumption.

    Section 1.6 Testing the sheaf condition

    Proposition 1.15.

    Let \(\mathcal F\) be a presheaf on \(X\) and let \(X=\bigcup_i U_i\) be an open cover. Suppose every restricted presheaf \(\mathcal F|_{U_i}\) is a sheaf. Then \(\mathcal F\) is a sheaf if, for each open \(V\subseteq X\text{,}\) matching sections on the cover \((V\cap U_i)_i\) glue uniquely in \(\mathcal F(V)\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Let a matching family be given on an arbitrary cover \(V=\bigcup_a V_a\text{.}\) Within each \(V\cap U_i\text{,}\) glue its restrictions to \(V_a\cap U_i\text{,}\) using the sheaf condition there. The resulting sections on \(V\cap U_i\) agree on their intersections because they agree after further restriction to the cover by the \(V_a\text{.}\) The assumed gluing condition for \((V\cap U_i)_i\) yields the required section on \(V\text{.}\) Both applications include uniqueness.
    The extra gluing condition in this statement is essential. A presheaf can have sheaf restrictions on all members of a cover and still impose an incorrect global condition on larger opens. For example, the normalized constant presheaf on a discrete two-point space has sheaf restrictions to each point but is not a sheaf on their union. Sheafhood is local for data that already satisfy descent across the chosen cover, not for arbitrary assignments of global sections.
    For a separated presheaf with terminal value on the empty open, the sheaf condition on all finite covers is equivalent to gluing on every two-open cover. Induction proves the assertion, using the intersection of the last member with the union of its predecessors. Arbitrary covers require another condition: gluing over an increasing union of opens. This is why a verification on finite covers alone does not exclude the bounded-function example.
    The definitions and basic examples can be compared with [Bre97, Chapter I], [Ive86, Chapter II], and Stacks, Sheaves. The basis construction is treated in Stacks, Bases and sheaves. Our proofs isolate the two uses of the sheaf axiom: uniqueness detects equality locally, and existence reconstructs sections from matching local data.
