    Chapter 13 Geometric sites and the étale topology

    The étale site replaces open subsets of a scheme by schemes mapping étale to it. The objects are generalized neighborhoods, the arrows are morphisms over the base, and a covering is a jointly surjective family of étale maps. This chapter explains that topology, its sheaves, its relation to the Zariski and big sites, and its geometric points.
    Scheme theory is external background here. We use affine schemes, fiber products, residue fields, and the stated elementary properties of étale morphisms. Their local algebra is cited rather than developed inside the sheaf theory. The constructions in this chapter are about sites, sheaves, ringed structures, and stalks; no étale cohomology theory or arithmetic formalism is needed.

    Section 13.1 Étale morphisms as generalized neighborhoods

    Definition 13.1.

    A morphism of schemes is étale if it is flat, locally of finite presentation, and unramified. Equivalently, it is smooth of relative dimension zero. These scheme-theoretic conditions are understood in their usual local sense. A basic algebraic model is a standard étale algebra
    \begin{equation*} B=(A[t]/(P))_g, \end{equation*}
    where \(P\) is monic and its derivative \(P'\) is invertible in \(B\text{.}\)
    The equivalence of the conditions, and the fact that étale morphisms are locally standard étale, are external algebraic results. See Stacks, Proposition 59.26.2 and Proposition 10.144.4. We use their following consequences: open immersions are étale; étale maps are stable under base change and composition; their images are open; and every morphism over \(X\) between schemes étale over \(X\) is itself étale. No quasi-compactness or separatedness is included in the definition of an étale morphism.
    The derivative condition has a direct infinitesimal meaning. If a root \(b\) of \(P\) is perturbed modulo a square-zero ideal by \(\epsilon\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} P(b+\epsilon)=P(b)+P'(b)\epsilon. \end{equation*}
    Invertibility of \(P'(b)\) permits one unique correction of an approximate root. This explains the unique infinitesimal lifting property. Extending that calculation to the equivalence of the scheme-theoretic definitions is part of the cited local algebra.1

    Example 13.2.

    Let \(n\geq1\) be an integer invertible in the field \(k\text{.}\) The map
    \begin{equation*} \Spec k[t,t^{-1}]\longrightarrow\Spec k[s,s^{-1}],\qquad s\longmapsto t^n \end{equation*}
    is finite étale and surjective. In characteristic \(p>0\text{,}\) the analogous map \(s\mapsto t^p\) is not étale.
    Solution.
    The target algebra adjoins a root of \(T^n-s\text{.}\) It is free of rank \(n\) over the base, and the derivative \(nT^{n-1}\) is invertible because \(n\) and \(T\) are invertible. This gives étaleness, and faithful freeness of positive rank gives surjectivity on spectra. For the characteristic-\(p\) map, the relative module of differentials contains the nonzero generator \(dt\text{,}\) since the derivative is zero. It is therefore not unramified.
    The omitted point \(t=0\) matters. On the affine line the map \(s\mapsto t^n\) is ramified there for \(n>1\text{,}\) so one cannot simply call every finite covering map étale. A nontrivial finite separable field extension \(L/k\) gives another example: \(\Spec L\to\Spec k\) is étale and surjective although it is not an isomorphism and cannot become one by shrinking the one-point Zariski space \(\Spec k\text{.}\)

    Section 13.2 The small site and its sheaves

    Definition 13.3.

    The small étale site \(X_{\mathrm{\acute et}}\) has as objects étale morphisms \(U\to X\text{,}\) and as arrows all \(X\)-morphisms. A family \((U_i\to U)\) is a covering if each map is étale and
    \begin{equation*} |U|=\bigcup_i\im(|U_i|\to|U|). \end{equation*}
    The topology is the one generated by these covering families. The small Zariski site \(X_{\mathrm{Zar}}\) has open immersions into \(X\) as objects, with ordinary open covers; it is equivalent to the site of open subsets of \(|X|\text{.}\)
    The identity is a covering, coverings compose, and they are preserved by base change. These assertions follow from the stated étale properties and the corresponding joint-surjectivity assertions. Fiber products exist in the small site and the terminal object is \(X\to X\text{.}\) Every arrow in this site is étale, so a family of arrows is a cover precisely when it is jointly surjective. This last simplification depends on the objects already being étale over \(X\text{;}\) it is false in the big site below. See Stacks, §59.27.

    Proposition 13.4.

    A presheaf \(F\) on the small étale site is a sheaf if and only if for every covering family the diagram
    \begin{equation*} F(U)\longrightarrow\prod_iF(U_i)\rightrightarrows\prod_{i,j}F(U_i\times_U U_j) \end{equation*}
    is an equalizer. An epimorphism of such sheaves permits lifts after an étale covering; sectionwise surjectivity is not required. The étale topology is subcanonical.

    Proof.
    The equalizer criterion is the sheaf criterion for a topology generated by this pretopology. The local lifting criterion is Definition 2.7. Subcanonicity is a geometric input: morphisms of schemes satisfy descent for étale covers. One can use the more general subcanonicity of the fpqc topology, since every étale covering is fpqc in the covering-family sense. The descent statement is recalled in Stacks, §59.27. It is not inferred merely from the fact that fiber products exist.
    The products include \(i=j\text{.}\) Even for one covering \(V\to U\text{,}\) its self-overlap \(V\times_U V\) can be much larger than \(V\text{.}\) Omitting that term would erase the descent condition. Likewise one cannot replace fiber products by intersections of their images in \(U\text{;}\) different lifts above the same base point carry information.2

    Example 13.5.

    Let \(L/k\) be a finite Galois field extension with group \(G\text{.}\) For every étale sheaf \(F\) on \(\Spec k\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} F(\Spec k)\simeq F(\Spec L)^G, \end{equation*}
    where the action is induced by the automorphisms of the covering.
    Solution.
    The standard field-algebra isomorphism \(L\otimes_kL\simeq\prod_{\sigma\in G}L\) decomposes the self-overlap as a disjoint union indexed by \(G\text{.}\) The sheaf condition identifies \(F(k)\) with the equalizer of the identity and the automorphism pullbacks on \(F(L)\text{,}\) namely the fixed elements. With right-action conventions one obtains inverse automorphisms, which have the same fixed elements. For the structure sheaf and \(L/k=\C/\R\text{,}\) this is the equality \(\R=\C^{\mathrm{conjugation}}\text{.}\)
    This example uses only degree-zero descent. It illustrates why a surjective étale map does not identify the sections upstairs with the sections downstairs. The extra automorphisms and their self-overlaps replace the equality of ordinary local functions on intersections.

    Section 13.3 Big sites and the direction of restriction

    Definition 13.6.

    The big étale site \((\operatorname{Sch}/X)_{\mathrm{\acute et}}\) has schemes \(T\to X\) as objects and arbitrary \(X\)-morphisms as arrows. Its coverings are jointly surjective étale families \((T_i\to T)\text{.}\) The structure map \(T\to X\) is not required to be étale.
    A size convention is part of this definition. We take schemes in a fixed universe containing \(X\text{,}\) then form the site and its sheaves in a sufficiently larger universe, or use a small admissible category with the required finite limits and covering refinements. The word big concerns the testing schemes; it does not remove the size convention of Chapter 0. See Stacks, §59.20.

    Proposition 13.7.

    Let \(r\) be restriction from sheaves on the big étale site to the small étale site:
    \begin{equation*} r:\Sh((\operatorname{Sch}/X)_{\mathrm{\acute et}})\longrightarrow\Sh(X_{\mathrm{\acute et}}). \end{equation*}
    There are geometric morphisms
    \begin{equation*} \pi:\Sh((\operatorname{Sch}/X)_{\mathrm{\acute et}})\longrightarrow\Sh(X_{\mathrm{\acute et}}),\qquad i:\Sh(X_{\mathrm{\acute et}})\longrightarrow\Sh((\operatorname{Sch}/X)_{\mathrm{\acute et}}) \end{equation*}
    with \(\pi_*=r=i^{-1}\) and \(\pi i\simeq\id\text{.}\) In particular \(r\) occurs as a right adjoint in one pair and as an inverse-image left adjoint in another.

    Proof.
    The inclusion of the small site in the big site is fully faithful, preserves finite limits and coverings, and has the same terminal object. A covering of an object already étale over \(X\) consists of objects again étale over \(X\text{.}\) The resulting two adjunctions and the retraction are Stacks, Lemma 34.4.14. In the convention of Proposition 4.3, restriction is the functor \(u_s\) for this inclusion \(u\text{,}\) and its sheafified left Kan extension \(u^s\) is \(\pi^{-1}\text{.}\) Its other adjoint is \(i_*\text{.}\) The extra adjunction uses these particular site properties; it is not part of the definition of every continuous functor.
    The restriction comparison is not an equivalence in general. Over a field \(k\text{,}\) let \(D=\Spec(k[\epsilon]/(\epsilon^2))\text{.}\) The representable big sheaf \(h_D\) restricts to the terminal sheaf on the small étale site: every scheme étale over \(k\) is reduced, so a map to \(D\) sends \(\epsilon\) to zero. But on the big test scheme \(D\text{,}\) the identity map and the map killing \(\epsilon\) are different sections. Restriction forgets that distinction. Big sheaves retain information on non-étale test schemes, including nilpotent thickenings.
    Similarly, inclusion of the open subsets into the small étale site gives a geometric morphism
    \begin{equation*} \varepsilon:\Sh(X_{\mathrm{\acute et}})\longrightarrow\Sh(X_{\mathrm{Zar}}). \end{equation*}
    Its direct image is restriction to opens; its inverse image is the sheafified left Kan extension in the convention of Proposition 4.3. Saying that the étale topology is finer is a statement about this comparison and its coverings. It is not the construction of a new ordinary topology on the set \(|X|\) with all the same information.

    Section 13.4 Geometric points and étale stalks

    Definition 13.8.

    A geometric point of \(X\) is a morphism \(\bar x:\Spec\Omega\to X\) with \(\Omega\) algebraically closed. An étale neighborhood of \(\bar x\) is an étale map \(U\to X\) with a lift \(\bar u:\Spec\Omega\to U\text{.}\) A morphism of neighborhoods is an \(X\)-morphism carrying the chosen lift to the chosen lift.
    The field \(\Omega\) supplies a point in a geometric fiber, not just a point of the underlying Zariski space. A finite separable extension of \(\kappa(x)\) may have several embeddings into \(\Omega\text{;}\) they give different lifts to one étale neighborhood. A separably closed field can also be used. We use algebraically closed fields to match Stacks, Definition 59.29.1.3

    Proposition 13.9.

    The category of étale neighborhoods of a fixed geometric point is cofiltered. A stalk is therefore a filtered colimit in the restriction direction.

    Proof.
    The identity neighborhood exists. The fiber product of two neighborhoods, with its induced geometric lift, maps to both. For two parallel arrows of neighborhoods, their equalizer contains the geometric lift and is an open subscheme of the source: the diagonal of the étale target over \(X\) is an open immersion. Restricting to that equalizer makes the arrows equal. This proves all the cofilteredness conditions. See Stacks, Lemma 59.29.4.

    Definition 13.10.

    For a sheaf \(F\) on the small étale site, define
    \begin{equation*} F_{\bar x}=\colim_{(U,\bar u)}F(U), \end{equation*}
    with transition maps given by restriction to smaller neighborhoods. A germ is represented by a triple \((U,\bar u,s)\text{.}\) Two representatives agree if they agree after pullback to a common neighborhood. This functor is the inverse image of a point of the étale topos.

    Theorem 13.11.

    The small étale topos has enough geometric points. A map of sheaves is monic, epic, or an isomorphism if and only if all its geometric stalk maps have that property in sets. For abelian and module sheaves, exactness is equivalent to exactness at all geometric stalks.

    Proof.
    Filtered colimits preserve finite limits, and a locally surjective map is surjective on geometric stalks: a geometric point lifts through an étale covering after selecting a point in a geometric fiber. Thus the stalk functors preserve finite limits and epimorphisms. They also preserve colimits and give the point functors, as proved in Stacks, Lemma 59.29.7.
    For conservativity, suppose \(F\to G\) is surjective at all geometric stalks. Fix \(s\in G(U)\text{.}\) For each geometric point of \(U\text{,}\) the stalk surjection gives an étale neighborhood with a section of \(F\) mapping to the germ of \(s\text{.}\) Equality of germs permits a further shrinking where the image equals \(s\) itself. These neighborhoods cover \(U\text{:}\) every ordinary point has an algebraically closed residue-field extension, hence a geometric point. Therefore \(s\) has lifts on an étale covering. For injectivity, equality of image sections is tested in the same way and then by the sheaf separation axiom. The isomorphism criterion follows by gluing the unique local inverses. Exactness for abelian or module sheaves follows from the kernel and image criteria. This is Stacks, Theorem 59.29.10.
    This is a theorem about this geometric site, not a convention for all topoi. For the big site, geometric points of \(X\) alone test only restrictions to the small site. One must instead use geometric points over all testing schemes \(T\to X\) and their slice sites. The dual-number example shows why the smaller family would miss information.

    Example 13.12.

    If \(k\) is separably closed, the small étale topos of \(\Spec k\) is equivalent to \(\Set\text{.}\) For a general field, its small Zariski topos is still \(\Set\text{,}\) but the small étale topos can contain nontrivial finite covers.
    Solution.
    An étale scheme over a field is a disjoint union of spectra of finite separable extensions, by Stacks, Proposition 59.26.2. For separably closed \(k\text{,}\) these are copies of \(\Spec k\text{.}\) The sheaf condition on this disjoint cover makes its sections a product of copies of one set, so a sheaf is determined by its value at \(\Spec k\text{.}\) For a nontrivial finite separable \(L/k\text{,}\) the representable sheaf of \(\Spec L\) has geometric stalk the set of \(k\)-embeddings of \(L\) into a separable closure. The field extension is thereby visible as a generalized neighborhood, even though the underlying Zariski space of the base is a point.

    Section 13.5 Structure sheaves and changes of base

    The structure sheaf on the small étale site is
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal O_{X_{\mathrm{\acute et}}}(U)=\Gamma(U,\mathcal O_U). \end{equation*}
    It is a sheaf because functions descend along étale covers; alternatively it is the representable functor of the affine line over \(X\text{.}\) Its restriction to the Zariski site is the usual structure sheaf. The resulting map \(\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal O_X\to\mathcal O_{X_{\mathrm{\acute et}}}\) makes \(\varepsilon\) a ringed morphism, with the extension-of-scalars inverse image of Definition 8.10.
    At a geometric point above \(x\text{,}\) its stalk is the strict henselization \(\mathcal O_{X,x}^{\mathrm{sh}}\text{,}\) with the separable residue-field closure selected by the geometric point. This is Stacks, Lemma 59.33.1. The construction and algebra of strict henselization are external local algebra here. This identification explains why this stalk usually differs from the Zariski local ring; even when \(X=\Spec k\text{,}\) the étale stalk of the structure sheaf is a separable closure of \(k\text{.}\)
    For a scheme map \(f:X\to Y\text{,}\) base change sends \(V\to Y\) to \(V\times_YX\to X\text{.}\) This functor
    \begin{equation*} Y_{\mathrm{\acute et}}\longrightarrow X_{\mathrm{\acute et}} \end{equation*}
    preserves finite limits and coverings, and induces a geometric morphism
    \begin{equation*} f_{\mathrm{\acute et}}:\Sh(X_{\mathrm{\acute et}})\longrightarrow\Sh(Y_{\mathrm{\acute et}}). \end{equation*}
    Its direct image has the formula \((f_{\mathrm{\acute et},*}F)(V)=F(V\times_YX)\text{.}\) Its inverse image is the corresponding sheafified left Kan extension, not restriction in that same direction. Pullback of functions gives the ringed structure map. At geometric points there is a natural identification \((f_{\mathrm{\acute et}}^{-1}G)_{\bar x}=G_{f\bar x}\text{,}\) expressing composition of the topos point with the geometric morphism.
    The words coherent and quasi-coherent still require the selected ringed site and the selected module category. Familiar scheme-theoretic comparison results are additional geometric facts, as discussed in Proposition 10.19. Changing to a big site and applying the intrinsic coherence definition there without checking the comparison can alter the category.

    Section 13.6 Related covering notions

    On the same admissible category of schemes over \(X\text{,}\) Zariski coverings are open covers, étale coverings are jointly surjective étale families, and fppf coverings are jointly surjective families flat and locally of finite presentation. Each Zariski cover is étale, and each étale cover is fppf. Fpqc coverings impose faithful flatness together with the appropriate quasi-compact covering condition; for a family this is expressed locally by finite quasi-compact refinements, rather than by requiring the entire disjoint union of an infinite family to be quasi-compact. Every étale covering is fpqc; see Stacks, Lemma 59.27.2.
    The characteristic-\(p\) map \(k[s,s^{-1}]\to k[t,t^{-1}]\text{,}\) \(s\mapsto t^p\text{,}\) is finite faithfully flat and finitely presented, hence an fppf cover, while it is not étale. Thus the added coverings genuinely change local lifting questions. A presheaf that is already an fppf sheaf is an étale sheaf on the same big category. Comparison between different underlying small and big categories requires the functors described earlier, rather than only an inclusion of sets of covers.
    These examples complete the use of the étale topology here: it supplies a subcanonical geometric site, nontrivial one-member covers, conservative geometric points, and a ringed structure with a different local meaning from the Zariski one. Cohomological and arithmetic results specific to that site require further theorems in their own geometric setting.
