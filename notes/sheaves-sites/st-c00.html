    Chapter 0 From open covers to sites

    A sheaf on a space is tested on open covers. In many questions, the useful local maps are not open inclusions: a covering space, an étale map of schemes, or a family of objects mapping onto another object can provide the local data. A Grothendieck topology specifies which such families suffice for descent. It changes the meaning of local without requiring a new definition of a compatible family each time.
    These notes develop that language, the resulting categories of sheaves, and their ringed structures. The first part treats sheaves of sets without assuming that points detect them. The second establishes module operations, finiteness, coherence, and derived constructions. A final example explains the étale topology. Its arithmetic cohomology is a separate subject.

    Section 0.1 What changes from topological sheaves

    Let \(\operatorname{Open}(X)\) be the category of open subsets of a space, with inclusions as arrows. A presheaf is a contravariant functor on this category. A covering of \(U\) consists of open subsets whose union is \(U\text{.}\) Intersections provide the overlaps on which compatibility is tested. This is the model developed in Sheaves on topological spaces and cohomology.
    In a general category, arrows into \(U\) need not be inclusions. Two arrows with the same target may have no fiber product. A sieve avoids requiring such a product: it is a collection of arrows into \(U\) closed under further precomposition. A compatible family on the sieve assigns data to all those arrows and respects every refinement. Covering sieves are the primitive covering objects in Chapter 1.1
    Three choices must remain distinct: the category of test objects, its topology, and its category of sheaves. Different covering families can generate the same topology. Different sites can present equivalent sheaf categories. On a fixed category, however, the full subcategory of sheaves determines the topology. The last assertion is proved in Chapter 2; it concerns the inclusion among presheaves, not an arbitrary abstract equivalence between categories.
    Points are another distinction. A topological stalk uses neighborhoods of a specified point. A point of a topos is a suitable functor to sets, expressed as an inverse-image functor. Not every topos has a conservative family of points. Local lifting on covering sieves replaces stalkwise arguments in the general constructions; Chapter 6 explains when points are available.

    Section 0.2 Size and categorical conventions

    Fix a universe. Unless stated otherwise, a site \((\mathcal C,J)\) has a small underlying category at that scale. Presheaves take values in sets of that universe. We pass to a larger universe to regard a category of presheaves or sheaves as an object of a category. The word small always refers to the fixed scale. A geometric example usually admits a small presentation; choosing one is part of its setup.2
    Write \(\PSh(\mathcal C)=\operatorname{Fun}(\mathcal C^{\op},\Set)\) and \(\Sh(\mathcal C,J)\) for set-valued presheaves and sheaves. For \(U\in\mathcal C\text{,}\) let \(h_U=\Hom_{\mathcal C}(-,U)\text{.}\) The sheafification functor is \(a\text{;}\) it depends on \(J\text{.}\) Thus \(ah_U\) is a sheaf even when \(h_U\) is not. A map of presheaves is a natural transformation.
    A topos means a Grothendieck topos, namely a category equivalent to \(\Sh(\mathcal C,J)\) for such a site. It is denoted \(\mathcal E\) or \(\mathcal F\text{.}\) An elementary topos is a different, more general categorical notion. Chapter 3 states the relation needed here. We study ordinary categories of sheaves; no higher categorical enhancement is implicit in the word topos.
    The underlying category \(\mathcal C\) is not assumed to have a terminal object, all fiber products, or a subcanonical topology. If it has a terminal object \(e\text{,}\) then \(h_e\) is the terminal presheaf and is a sheaf. A sheaf’s global sections can then be written \(F(e)\text{.}\) In general the definition is \(\Gamma(\mathcal E,F)=\Hom_{\mathcal E}(1,F)\text{.}\) The object \(1\) is the terminal sheaf, whether or not represented in the chosen site.

    Section 0.3 Ringed structures and directions

    A ring object \(\mathcal O\) is commutative with identity; modules are unital, and the zero ring is allowed. The category of modules in \(\mathcal E\) is \(\Mod(\mathcal O)\text{.}\) A fixed coefficient ring is denoted \(\Lambda\text{,}\) a coefficient field \(\Bbbk\text{,}\) and a geometric base field \(k\text{.}\) None of the general results assumes that a space or scheme is over \(\C\text{.}\)
    For a geometric morphism \(f:\mathcal E\to\mathcal F\text{,}\) the inverse image and direct image have types
    \begin{equation*} f^{-1}:\mathcal F\longrightarrow\mathcal E,\qquad f_*:\mathcal E\longrightarrow\mathcal F,\qquad f^{-1}\dashv f_*. \end{equation*}
    The inverse image preserves finite limits and all colimits. A ringed morphism additionally has a map \(f^{-1}\mathcal O_{\mathcal F}\to\mathcal O_{\mathcal E}\text{.}\) Its module inverse image is
    \begin{equation*} f^*M=\mathcal O_{\mathcal E}\otimes_{f^{-1}\mathcal O_{\mathcal F}}f^{-1}M. \end{equation*}
    Here \(f^{-1}M\) is first a module over \(f^{-1}\mathcal O_{\mathcal F}\text{.}\) Changing that ring need not be exact. This is the distinction between \(f^{-1}\) and \(f^*\text{,}\) not just two names for one operation.
    Authors use different superscripts for the functors induced by a functor between sites. We define every such symbol with its source and target in Chapter 4. The geometric notation just displayed is used throughout the notes. It remains valid when a geometric morphism cannot be described by a morphism of the initially chosen sites.
    For modules, \(\sheafHom_{\mathcal O}(M,N)\) is internal Hom and \(\Hom_{\mathcal O}(M,N)\) is the abelian group of global module morphisms. The corresponding derived objects are \(\RsheafHom_{\mathcal O}\) and \(\RHom_{\mathcal O}\text{.}\) Cohomological indices and shifts agree with the homological algebra note: \(K[r]^n=K^{n+r}\text{,}\) with differential \((-1)^r d_K\text{.}\) Cohomology objects are \(\mathcal H^n(K)\text{;}\) hypercohomology is \(H^n(\mathcal E,K)=H^n(R\Gamma(\mathcal E,K))\text{.}\)

    Section 0.4 Sources and dependencies

    Chapter 1 through Chapter 7 develop sites and topoi. The point-free sheafification construction is included in full because it supplies the local tests used later. Module theory begins in Chapter 8. Finite presentation and coherence have a separate chapter; derived operations and hypercovers follow them. The geometric sites chapter can be read after the first part, with the stated scheme-theoretic prerequisites.
    The main categorical references are [KS06, Chapters 16–18], [MM92, Chapters III–VII], and [SGA4-I, Exposés II–IV]. [Stacks] supplies stable links to technical statements and their exact hypotheses. Its opening definition of a site uses specified covering families with representable pullbacks; its later sieve formulation is the starting convention here. The distinction is explained at the relevant definitions.
    [Sch26] provides a concise route through sites and derived sheaves. We use the author version dated October 15, 2026, marked work in progress, and check technical statements against the other sources. General homological algebra is called from the existing notes or precisely cited external theorems. It is not redeveloped between the construction of two sheaf operations.
    The finiteness chapter distinguishes local finite presentation, coherence, and dualizability, including the role of local rings. Its scheme and analytic examples state the additional geometric inputs. Hypercover descent is a theorem about cohomology and bounded below complexes under its specified conventions. It does not assert that ordinary triangulated derived categories form a stack. Proper-support images, extraordinary inverse images, and Verdier duality belong to the third note, rather than to the definition of an arbitrary ringed topos.
