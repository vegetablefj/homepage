    Appendix A. Terminology, hypotheses, and source conventions

    This appendix collects distinctions used throughout the note. The linked statements contain their precise hypotheses and proofs. The descriptions here do not impose a further global assumption on the sites or rings.

    Section A.1 Sites, presentations, and local tests

    Topology and pretopology. Here a site has covering sieves as primitive data; Definition 1.5 does not assume fiber products. A pretopology provides selected covering families whose generated sieves define a topology. The opening Stacks convention for a site includes the representable base changes of covering arrows. Its later topology formulation agrees with our starting convention. When an equalizer or a Čech nerve is written using objects \(U_i\times_U U_j\text{,}\) their existence is part of that calculation, rather than part of our general definition.
    Canonical and subcanonical. Subcanonical says that all representables are sheaves. Canonical means the finest topology with that property. The defining effective gluing property must hold after every base change; an epimorphic family alone need not qualify. See Proposition 1.14. When subcanonicity is absent, use \(ah_U\text{,}\) not an unproved representable sheaf. These objects still generate the topos by Proposition 3.3.
    Local equality and local surjectivity. A sectionwise equality is an equality on the specified test object. Local equality allows restriction to a covering sieve. A sheaf identifies locally equal sections, but a presheaf need not. Epimorphisms of sheaves have local preimages, not necessarily preimages on the original test object. Proposition 2.8 and Proposition 3.4 give the point-free tests.
    Site and topos. A site is a category equipped with specified local tests. Its topos is the category of sheaves, considered up to equivalence. Equivalent topoi need not have equivalent underlying site categories. On one fixed category, however, the full subcategory of sheaves among its presheaves determines the topology; see Proposition 2.10. An abstract equivalence of topoi forgets that particular inclusion.
    Localization. The slice topos \(\mathcal E/A\) organizes objects over \(A\text{.}\) This use of localization differs from localizing a ring, inverting quasi-isomorphisms, or applying a reflective localization of a category. A slice over a site object is described using its sheafified representable unless subcanonicity has been supplied; see Chapter 5.

    Section A.2 Morphisms, points, and descent

    Geometric directions. A geometric morphism \(f:\mathcal E\to\mathcal F\) has \(f^{-1}:\mathcal F\to\mathcal E\) left adjoint to \(f_*:\mathcal E\to\mathcal F\text{.}\) The inverse image is left exact. A continuous functor of sites initially gives a restriction functor preserving sheaves; to obtain a geometric morphism from its adjoint pair one must also verify the appropriate left exactness. The explicit types and the Stacks translation of \(u_s,u^s\) are in Chapter 4. A symbol’s subscript alone does not determine which of these functors an author means.
    Point and global section. A point is a geometric morphism \(\Set\to\mathcal E\text{;}\) its inverse image is a fiber functor to sets. Global sections are \(\Hom_{\mathcal E}(1,-)\text{.}\) They are different functors. For a group-action topos, the underlying-set functor is the inverse image of a point, whereas global sections take fixed elements. Enough points means that a family of such inverse images is conservative, and is an additional property, discussed in Chapter 6.
    Descent and its effectivity. Descent data include specified isomorphisms on overlaps and a cocycle equation. Effective descent says that these data come from an object on the base, with the corresponding morphism statement. It is stronger than simply knowing that a particular collection of sections glues. The ordinary object-level theorem in Chapter 7 applies to sheaves and subsequently to their algebraic structures. Descent of derived objects requires a separate model for the needed compatibilities.

    Section A.3 Ringed objects and finiteness

    Underlying inverse image and module pullback. The functor \(f^{-1}\) transports an \(\mathcal O_{\mathcal F}\)-module to an \(f^{-1}\mathcal O_{\mathcal F}\)-module. The functor \(f^*\) then extends scalars to \(\mathcal O_{\mathcal E}\text{.}\) The first step is exact; the second can introduce Tor. Fixed constant coefficients in Note I make the scalar change an isomorphism, which is why that case has an exact inverse image. See Chapter 8 and Theorem 11.4.
    Finite type, local finite presentation, coherence. Finite type provides finitely many local generators. Local finite presentation provides finitely many generators and relations in a local presentation. Coherence additionally controls the kernels of all maps from finite free modules, and is defined in Definition 10.4. Coherent modules form an abelian subcategory even without coherence of \(\mathcal O\text{.}\) If the ring object is coherent, coherence and local finite presentation agree. None of these local definitions automatically says that the module is a finitely presented object of the entire category \(\Mod(\mathcal O)\text{.}\)
    Coherent ring, topos, and category. Coherence of a ring object concerns relations of its modules. A coherent topos concerns a finite-cover presentation and its finite-limit properties. A locally coherent Grothendieck category concerns its finitely presented objects and filtered-colimit generation. These are different conditions on different objects. One must not infer the second or third simply from the first; the finiteness chapter explains the distinctions at the relevant uses.
    Dualizable and finite locally free. Over a general ring object, a dualizable module is locally a direct summand of a finite free module, equivalently flat and locally finitely presented. Over a local ring a finite projective module is free, giving the familiar finite locally free description on locally ringed spaces. A one-point ringed topos with a nonlocal ring already shows why the general formulation uses projective summands. The module and complex versions appear in Chapter 10 and Theorem 11.11.
    Geometric coherent sheaves. The affine correspondence concerns quasi-coherent sheaves. It does not identify all module sheaves with modules over global sections. On stacks, the chosen site matters: an intrinsic coherent module on a large ringed site need not be the object called coherent in geometric usage. The geometry-specific comparison and external analytic coherence theorem are recorded in Chapter 10; no global proper-image or analytic vanishing theorem is included as part of the general definition.

    Section A.4 Derived operations and covering complexes

    Three finiteness conditions for complexes. Bounded coherent cohomology, pseudo-coherence, and perfectness are distinct. Finite approximation defines pseudo-coherence; a local finite projective model defines perfectness. Locally finite Tor amplitude distinguishes perfect objects among pseudo-coherent ones. Global cohomological bounds require a separate check when local bounds vary. Also, \(\D^b(\Coh(\mathcal O))\) is not identified by definition with the full subcategory \(\D^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(\mathcal O)\text{;}\) see Chapter 11.
    Existence and comparison. The derived functors exist on the categories specified in Chapter 11. A projection or base change morphism can be constructed without being invertible. Tensor duality gives the projection formula for a perfect factor; general base change needs more assumptions. Neither construction supplies proper-support images or extraordinary inverse images on every ringed topos.
    Čech nerve, hypercover, and totalization. A Čech nerve is forced by an epimorphism in degree zero. A hypercover allows further covering choices for each compatible boundary. For either, derived sections with a compatible total-complex model compute bounded below cohomology. Computing with ordinary sections instead requires acyclicity, or refinement through the classical hypercover theorem. Chapter 12 distinguishes these assertions and gives the signs, degrees, and convergence range.
    Étale terminology. An étalé space is a local homeomorphism in topology. An étale morphism of schemes defines a different geometric local test. Small and big étale sites have different test objects and must not be identified merely because both use étale covering families. The final chapter develops those sites, geometric points, and strict-henselian stalk descriptions. It does not develop étale cohomology, adic coefficients, weights, or an arithmetic six-functor formalism.
