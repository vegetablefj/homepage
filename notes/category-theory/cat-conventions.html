    Chapter 0 Conventions and foundations

    Section 0.1 Language and notation

    These notes develop ordinary category theory, with sets, ordered sets, monoids, groups, and rings as recurring examples. This chapter fixes the language in which the definitions and results will be stated. It supplies the size conventions needed to form categories of mathematical objects and functors, without making set theory a separate course. The category axioms and the definitions of functors, natural transformations, and equivalences are developed in the next chapter.
    Categories are denoted by \(\mathcal C,\mathcal D,\mathcal E\text{.}\) We write \(\Ob(\mathcal C)\) for the set of objects and \(\Hom_{\mathcal C}(X,Y)\) for the set of morphisms from \(X\) to \(Y\text{.}\) The notation \(X\in\mathcal C\) abbreviates \(X\in\Ob(\mathcal C)\text{;}\) it does not assert membership in a set identified with the category itself. A morphism is also called an arrow, and \(f:X\to Y\) specifies its source and target. For \(f:X\to Y\) and \(g:Y\to Z\text{,}\) the composite is \(g\circ f:X\to Z\text{:}\) first apply \(f\text{,}\) then \(g\text{.}\) The identity of \(X\) is \(\id_X\text{.}\)
    We reserve \(=\) for equality, write \(X\cong Y\) for isomorphism of objects, and use \(\mathcal C\simeq\mathcal D\) for equivalence of categories. An unspecified isomorphism is not thereby canonical. Whenever a construction is said to be unique up to unique isomorphism, the required compatibility with its structure maps is part of that assertion.1
    We use \(\N=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\text{.}\) A finite set may be empty; a finite indexing category may also be empty. Consequently, a claim about all finite limits or colimits includes the empty diagram. We use \(A\subseteq B\) for inclusion and specify proper inclusion when it matters. Conventions of this kind determine the meaning of later statements; they are not claims that one definition is the broadest possible in every mathematical context.

    Section 0.2 The ambient sets

    We work in Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory with the axiom of choice, and additionally assume two Grothendieck universes \(\U\in\V\text{,}\) with \(\N\in\U\text{.}\) A Grothendieck universe is a transitive set closed under forming pairs, power sets, and unions of families of its elements indexed by an element of the universe. The existence of the chosen universes is an additional foundational assumption. Any further universe enlargement required later is likewise an explicit assumption, not a theorem being deduced from ZFC.2
    We call a set \(A\) \(\U\)-small when \(A\in\U\text{.}\) Transitivity means that every element of a \(\U\)-small set also belongs to \(\U\text{.}\) Subsets of a \(\U\)-small set, finite products, function sets, and disjoint unions and products indexed by a \(\U\)-small set remain in \(\U\text{.}\) These closure properties justify the size estimates below.
    Membership \(A\in\U\) is stronger than inclusion \(A\subseteq\U\text{.}\) In particular, \(\U\subseteq\U\text{,}\) but \(\U\notin\U\text{.}\) If \(A\subseteq B\) and \(B\in\U\text{,}\) then \(A\in\U\text{,}\) because \(A\in\mathcal P(B)\in\U\text{.}\) No analogous conclusion follows from \(A\subseteq\U\) alone.3
    Every category in these notes has a set of objects and a set of all arrows. Classes are not used as primitive data. A category described informally as large is therefore large relative to the currently specified universe, while its complete encoding is still an ordinary set. Since \(\U\in\V\text{,}\) the entire set \(\U\) and constructions indexed by it can be handled at the \(\V\) scale. A category need not have its complete encoding in this particular \(\V\) unless that is assumed.

    Section 0.3 Small, locally small, and essentially small

    A category is presented by its object set, its morphism sets, and the identity and composition data. Its total arrow set \(\Ar(\mathcal C)\) is the disjoint union of the morphism sets, with source and target recorded. This distinguishes a morphism in one hom-set from an identical underlying label used in another.4

    Definition 0.1 Relative size conventions.

    For a set-encoded category \(\mathcal C\text{,}\) we use the following terms.
    1. It is \(\U\)-small if \(\Ob(\mathcal C)\in\U\) and \(\Ar(\mathcal C)\in\U\text{.}\)
    2. It is locally \(\U\)-small if \(\Hom_{\mathcal C}(X,Y)\in\U\) for every pair of objects \(X,Y\text{.}\)
    3. It is essentially \(\U\)-small if it is equivalent to a \(\U\)-small category.
    Unless otherwise specified, our categories are locally \(\U\)-small. When we use the abbreviation \(\U\)-category, it means a set-encoded, locally \(\U\)-small category; it does not mean a \(\U\)-small category. Its object set can have size beyond \(\U\text{.}\) With our hom-set presentation, a locally \(\U\)-small category whose object set belongs to \(\U\) is \(\U\)-small: its total arrows form a \(\U\)-small indexed disjoint union.
    Essential smallness permits a change of presentation. A category can contain many isomorphic copies of the same object while being equivalent to a small category. Choosing representatives uses the axiom of choice on a set of isomorphism classes. A full subcategory of representatives need not itself satisfy our strict membership convention if the chosen object names lie outside \(\U\text{;}\) the definition allows an equivalent category whose data have been encoded inside \(\U\text{.}\) The words small and essentially small should therefore not be interchanged in constructions that explicitly enumerate the given objects.
    A small diagram means a functor whose indexing category is \(\U\)-small, unless another universe is specified. Later, complete or cocomplete will mean that all such small limits or colimits exist. This does not require limits or colimits indexed by every category at the \(\V\) scale. Changing the universe changes the range of these assertions.

    Section 0.4 Categories of algebraic objects

    The category \(\Set_{\U}\) has the sets in \(\U\) as objects and all functions between them as morphisms. Thus \(\Ob(\Set_{\U})=\U\text{.}\) For objects \(A,B\text{,}\) its hom-set is the function set \(B^A\in\U\text{.}\) Consequently \(\Set_{\U}\) is locally \(\U\)-small but not \(\U\)-small. Its complete category encoding belongs to \(\V\text{.}\) The universe has made the intended collection of sets into an object set at a larger scale, without making it small at the original scale.
    Similarly, \(\Mon_{\U}\text{,}\) \(\Grp_{\U}\text{,}\) and \(\Ring_{\U}\) consist of structures whose underlying sets belong to \(\U\text{.}\) Their morphisms are the usual structure-preserving functions. Monoids have an identity and their homomorphisms preserve it. Rings are associative with identity, are not assumed commutative, and their homomorphisms preserve the identity. The zero ring is included; the commutative subcategory is denoted by \(\CRing_{\U}\text{.}\)5
    We usually omit the subscript and write \(\Set,\Mon,\Grp,\Ring\text{.}\) This always retains the current \(\U\) convention. It never means the collection of all sets or all algebraic structures in every universe. When both scales appear in one argument, the subscripts are restored.

    Section 0.5 Size of functors and natural transformations

    The notation \(\Fun(\mathcal C,\mathcal D)\) denotes the category of functors and natural transformations; its morphism sets are written \(\Nat(F,G)\text{.}\) Local smallness of \(\mathcal C\) and \(\mathcal D\) alone does not guarantee local smallness of this category at the same scale. The number of components in a natural transformation matters.

    Proposition 0.2 A size bound for functor categories.

    If \(\mathcal C\) is \(\U\)-small and \(\mathcal D\) is locally \(\U\)-small, then \(\Nat(F,G)\in\U\) for all functors \(F,G:\mathcal C\to\mathcal D\text{.}\) If, in addition, \(\mathcal D\) is \(\V\)-small, the full functor category is \(\V\)-small.

    Proof.
    A natural transformation is a component family satisfying equations. Hence its actual set of component families is a subset of
    \begin{gather*} \prod_{X\in\Ob(\mathcal C)}\Hom_{\mathcal D}(F(X),G(X)). \end{gather*}
    The indexing set and every factor belong to \(\U\text{,}\) so the product and its subset of natural families belong to \(\U\text{.}\) For the second assertion, functors are specified by object and morphism assignments satisfying the functor equations. Under the additional assumption, all these assignments range over function sets formed inside \(\V\text{.}\) The functors form a subset of such a set. Their total natural-transformation arrows are a \(\V\)-small indexed disjoint union.
    For comparison with the component-family argument, see [Rie, Exercise 1.7.i]. The extra condition on \(\mathcal D\) controls the complete collection of functors, not only the morphisms between two already specified functors. Local smallness does not put an entire object set inside \(\U\text{.}\)
    For a concrete failure without a small source, let \(\mathcal I\) be the discrete category with object set \(\U\text{,}\) using a singleton hom-set at each object and the empty set otherwise. It is locally \(\U\)-small and \(\V\)-small. Let \(F=G:\mathcal I\to\Set_{\U}\) be constant at a two-element set \(A\text{.}\) There are no nonidentity naturality equations, so
    \begin{gather*} \Nat(F,G)=\prod_{X\in\U}\Hom_{\Set_{\U}}(A,A). \end{gather*}
    This set belongs to \(\V\) but not to \(\U\text{.}\)6

    Section 0.6 Choices and comparisons with sources

    The axiom of choice is available for set-indexed families. Selecting representatives, choosing inverses to an equivalence, or choosing one realization of a universal construction must therefore take place over a specified set. No separate principle of global choice is assumed. A chosen construction and a construction independent of choices are different assertions: independence is normally expressed by a compatible isomorphism, and functoriality or naturality still needs to be proved.
    Our set-encoded presentation and use of universes are close to [KS06, Sections 1.1–1.2], subject to the strict smallness convention explained above. Li likewise distinguishes membership from smallness up to bijection, while using actual \(\U\)-sets as the underlying sets of standard algebraic objects [LiI, Sections 1.5 and 2.1]. Our standard object convention agrees with his; our strict use of small still requires translation. The Stacks Project instead explicitly works in ZFC without universes, and its default category has a set of objects and set-valued homs [Stacks, Tags 0004 and 0005]. It also admits separately specified big categories. We therefore translate its hypotheses into the present relative-size language when using a result; a statement about every category or every diagram is read together with that source’s conventions.
    In practice, each later construction will indicate the size conditions it uses. Smallness, existence, and compatibility conditions belong to the statement of a result. Terminological differences and alternative encodings can be clarified in notes, while a change that affects the validity of an argument must remain visible in the main text.
