Theorem 14.5.

For presheaves \(P,Q\text{,}\) define
\begin{equation*} (Q^P)(c)=\Nat(h_c\times P,Q),\qquad h_c=\Hom_{\mathcal C}(-,c). \end{equation*}
For \(a:c'\to c\text{,}\) its restriction sends \(\alpha\) to \(\alpha(h_a\times\id_P)\text{.}\) This defines a presheaf and an exponential, with evaluation
\begin{equation*} \operatorname{ev}_c(\alpha,p)=\alpha_c(\id_c,p). \end{equation*}
Consequently \(\widehat{\mathcal C}\) is cartesian closed.
🔗
🔗

Proof.
The natural-transformation sets in the formula belong to \(\U\) by Proposition 0.2. Precomposition with \(h_a\) has the stated contravariant direction and respects identities and composition. Thus \(Q^P\) is a presheaf with the required size.
🔗
Evaluation is natural. For \(a:d\to c\text{,}\) naturality of \(\alpha:h_c\times P\Rightarrow Q\) gives
\begin{equation*} Q(a)\bigl(\alpha_c(\id_c,p)\bigr) =\alpha_d(a,P(a)(p)). \end{equation*}
The right side is evaluation at \(d\) of the restricted transformation and the restricted element \(P(a)(p)\text{.}\)
🔗
Given \(\theta:R\times P\Rightarrow Q\text{,}\) define \(\beta:R\Rightarrow Q^P\) by sending \(r\in R(c)\) to the transformation whose component at \(d\) is
\begin{equation*} \beta_c(r)_d(a,p)=\theta_d(R(a)(r),p), \qquad a:d\to c,\quad p\in P(d). \end{equation*}
Naturality of \(\theta\) and the presheaf rules prove naturality of this transformation in \(d\text{:}\) restricting by \(b:e\to d\) gives \(\theta_e(R(ab)(r),P(b)(p))\) on either side. Likewise, restricting \(r\) along \(u:c'\to c\) replaces \(a:d\to c'\) by \(ua\text{,}\) exactly the arrow rule for \(Q^P\text{.}\) Hence \(\beta\) is natural in \(c\text{.}\)
🔗
Conversely, a transformation \(\beta:R\Rightarrow Q^P\) gives
\begin{equation*} \theta_c(r,p)=\beta_c(r)_c(\id_c,p). \end{equation*}
This is natural by evaluation. Starting with \(\theta\text{,}\) transposing and evaluating returns it because \(R(\id_c)=\id_{R(c)}\text{.}\) Starting with \(\beta\text{,}\) naturality along \(a:d\to c\) gives
\begin{equation*} \beta_d(R(a)(r))_d(\id_d,p)=\beta_c(r)_d(a,p), \end{equation*}
so the transpose of the evaluated transformation returns \(\beta\) at every component. These constructions are inverse, and evaluation gives precisely the defining exponential bijection.
