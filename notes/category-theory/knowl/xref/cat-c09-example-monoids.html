Example 9.21 The finite-word monad recovers monoids.

On \(\Set_{\U}\text{,}\) let \(T(X)=\coprod_{n\in\N}X^n\text{,}\) let \(T(f)\) apply \(f\) to each letter, and use singleton words and flattening for \(\eta,\mu\text{.}\) For an algebra \(a:T(A)\to A\text{,}\) define \(e=a(())\) and \(x\cdot y=a((x,y))\text{.}\) The algebra law says that evaluating a concatenation of words is the same as evaluating each word first and then evaluating the word of resulting values.
🔗
Apply this law to the word of words \(((),(x))\text{,}\) and then to \(((x),())\text{;}\) the singleton unit law gives \(e\cdot x=x=x\cdot e\text{.}\) Apply it to \(((x,y),(z))\) and \(((x),(y,z))\text{,}\) whose flattenings agree, to prove associativity. Splitting a nonempty word into a shorter word and its last singleton proves inductively that \(a\) is its product evaluation. Conversely, evaluating words in a monoid satisfies both algebra laws by associativity and the empty-product convention. A function is an algebra map exactly when it preserves the identity and multiplication, since these are evaluations of empty and two-letter words; those conditions conversely preserve all words by induction. Thus the comparison from \(\Mon\) is an equivalence, indeed an isomorphism for these explicit encodings.
 10 
This elementary verification is [LiII, Example 7.6.4]. The broader list of familiar monadic categories in [Rie, Corollary 5.5.3] requires its own identifications of algebra structures; it is not an automatic consequence of possessing a free functor.
