    Chapter 10 Localizations

    Localization makes a chosen collection of arrows invertible, imposing the relations necessary for this change and no further relations beyond their consequences. The objects can be kept fixed, but their morphism sets usually change: new composites become available, and existing arrows can become equal. The universal property describes precisely which information the construction retains.
    We first construct localization by finite paths, including its effect on natural transformations and its size. We then give an explicitly oriented calculus of fractions, and relate reflective subcategories to localization. The main sources are [LiII, Section 1.9], [KS06, Section 7.1], and [Stacks, Section 4.27]. In this chapter, categories are set-encoded, but a localized category and an arbitrary target of its universal property are not assumed locally \(\U\)-small. Such smallness will be stated when it is available.

    Section 10.1 The universal problem and the path construction

    Fix a category \(\mathcal C\) and a subset \(W\subseteq\Ar(\mathcal C)\) of its typed arrows. We impose no closure condition on \(W\text{.}\) In particular, it need not contain the identities or be closed under composition, and it need not belong to \(\U\text{.}\) It is an ordinary set because the entire arrow collection is a set.

    Definition 10.1 Localization at a family of arrows.

    A functor \(F:\mathcal C\to\mathcal D\) is \(W\)-inverting if \(F(w)\) is an isomorphism for every \(w\in W\text{.}\) Write \(\Fun_W(\mathcal C,\mathcal D)\) for the full subcategory of such functors. A localization of \(\mathcal C\) at \(W\) is a \(W\)-inverting functor \(Q:\mathcal C\to\mathcal L\) such that, for every set-encoded category \(\mathcal D\text{,}\) precomposition is an equivalence
    \begin{equation*} Q^*:\Fun(\mathcal L,\mathcal D)\longrightarrow \Fun_W(\mathcal C,\mathcal D). \end{equation*}
    We denote a chosen localization by \(\mathcal C[W^{-1}]\text{,}\) retaining its structure functor \(Q\text{.}\)
    This formulation includes natural transformations. It asserts that every inverting functor factors up to natural isomorphism and that transformations between factorizations are uniquely determined on \(\mathcal C\text{.}\) There is also a useful stricter model: its objects are exactly those of \(\mathcal C\text{,}\) and every inverting functor factors by equality through a unique functor. Li uses this strict formulation in [LiII, Definition 1.9.1]; the accompanying footnote compares the functor-category version.

    Theorem 10.2.

    For every set-encoded \(\mathcal C\) and every \(W\subseteq\Ar(\mathcal C)\text{,}\) there is an identity-on-objects localization \(Q:\mathcal C\to\mathcal C[W^{-1}]\text{.}\) In this model every \(W\)-inverting \(F:\mathcal C\to\mathcal D\) has a unique extension \(\overline F\) with \(\overline FQ=F\text{.}\) Precomposition is an isomorphism onto \(\Fun_W(\mathcal C,\mathcal D)\text{,}\) rather than just an equivalence.

    Proof.
    Form a quiver with the objects of \(\mathcal C\text{.}\) For each original arrow \(f:X\to Y\text{,}\) include a forward edge \([f]:X\to Y\text{.}\) For each \(w:X\to Y\) in \(W\text{,}\) include a separate reverse edge \([w]^{-1}:Y\to X\text{.}\) Take finite composable paths, including an empty path at every object, and concatenate them. Impose the smallest congruence on parallel paths generated by
    \begin{equation*} [g][f]=[gf],\qquad [\id_X]=\id_X,\qquad [w]^{-1}[w]=\id_X,\qquad [w][w]^{-1}=\id_Y. \end{equation*}
    A congruence here is an equivalence relation on each parallel-path set preserved by concatenation on either side. The smallest one exists as the intersection of all such congruences containing the displayed relations. The quotient therefore has well-defined associative composition and identity arrows. Let \(Q(f)\) be the class of \([f]\text{;}\) the relations make \(Q\) a functor and make each \(Q(w)\) invertible.
    For an inverting functor \(F\text{,}\) send each forward edge to \(F(f)\text{,}\) each reverse edge to \(F(w)^{-1}\text{,}\) and a path to the corresponding composite. The defining relations hold in \(\mathcal D\text{,}\) so this gives \(\overline F\) on the quotient. It is the unique possible extension because the forward edges and the stipulated inverses generate every path class. The natural-transformation part, proved immediately below, completes the asserted functor-category universal property.
    One may visualize a path as a finite zigzag in \(\mathcal C\text{,}\) with only arrows of \(W\) traversed backward. The symbols for reverse edges are formal data until the quotient has been taken. They do not presume that these arrows already possess inverses in \(\mathcal C\text{.}\) No arbitrary reversal of the other arrows is permitted.
    The construction handles the empty family as well. With no reverse edges, composition and identity relations reduce every path to its original composite, so the resulting category is canonically isomorphic to \(\mathcal C\text{.}\) The same holds when all chosen arrows were already invertible. In general, one may first adjoin identities to \(W\) and close it under finite composition without changing the inverting functors. This elementary normalization is different from the Ore conditions introduced later: it supplies no rule for rearranging a forward arrow and a reversed denominator.

    Section 10.2 Natural transformations and comparison of models

    A factorization statement only about functors leaves out the comparison maps used in applications. The path model supplies these maps without any additional choices on the new arrows.

    Proposition 10.3.

    For the path localization and functors \(A,B:\mathcal C[W^{-1}]\to\mathcal D\text{,}\) restriction is a bijection
    \begin{equation*} \Nat(A,B)\longrightarrow\Nat(AQ,BQ). \end{equation*}
    A restricted transformation is invertible exactly when the original one is invertible.

    Proof.
    Because \(Q\) is the identity on objects, a transformation \(\alpha:AQ\Rightarrow BQ\) determines the only possible component family on the localization. It is natural for every forward edge by assumption. For \(w:X\to Y\) in \(W\text{,}\) the naturality equation
    \begin{equation*} \alpha_YA(Qw)=B(Qw)\alpha_X \end{equation*}
    implies, by multiplication with the two inverse arrows,
    \begin{equation*} \alpha_XA(Qw)^{-1}=B(Qw)^{-1}\alpha_Y. \end{equation*}
    Hence it is also natural for reverse edges. Naturality is preserved under composition, so it holds for every path class. This proves existence and uniqueness of the extension. Its components have not changed, so the componentwise criterion for a natural isomorphism proves the final assertion.
    The components of \(\alpha\) need not be isomorphisms in this argument. Only the images of the chosen denominators are inverted. This distinction is what permits arbitrary natural transformations, rather than only natural isomorphisms, in the universal property. Compare [LiII, Proposition 1.9.4] and [Rie, Remark 6.4.6].

    Proposition 10.4.

    Any localization in the functor-category sense is equivalent to the path localization, compatibly with the structure functors up to natural isomorphism. Two models with the strict extension property are uniquely isomorphic by an isomorphism commuting strictly with the structure functors. Moreover,
    \begin{equation*} \bigl(\mathcal C[W^{-1}]\bigr)^\op \cong\mathcal C^\op[(W^\op)^{-1}] \end{equation*}
    for the path models, compatibly with \(Q^\op\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Write \(P:\mathcal C\to\mathcal F\) for the path model and \(Q:\mathcal C\to\mathcal L\) for another localization. Strict extension gives \(H:\mathcal F\to\mathcal L\) with \(HP=Q\text{;}\) essential surjectivity of \(Q^*\) gives \(K:\mathcal L\to\mathcal F\) with \(KQ\cong P\text{.}\) Full faithfulness of \(P^*\) and \(Q^*\) lifts these comparisons uniquely to \(KH\cong\id_{\mathcal F}\) and \(HK\cong\id_{\mathcal L}\text{.}\) Thus \(H\) is an equivalence. With strict universal properties, the same argument uses equalities and uniqueness of extensions, giving strict inverse functors. Finally, reversing paths and all their generating relations gives the asserted isomorphism of opposite path models.
    The compatibility condition is part of these uniqueness statements. There is no claim that the underlying category has a unique equivalence to another presentation after its structure functor is forgotten. In particular, keeping the objects fixed is a useful construction choice, not a requirement on every equivalent model of a localization.
    In the strict path model, a factorization is determined on objects as well as arrows. In an equivalent model, its object assignment may instead be specified only through an isomorphism with the original assignment. The functor-category definition retains these comparison isomorphisms and all the transformations between factorizations. It therefore allows one to replace a concrete construction by a more convenient category of representatives without altering the universal problem. The distinction between isomorphism and equivalence of models is useful here, rather than merely a difference in phrasing.

    Section 10.3 Existence and local size are different questions

    The path construction is always available in the present set-encoded framework. This does not imply that the resulting local morphism sets belong to the fixed universe.

    Proposition 10.5.

    The formal localization of a set-encoded category is set-encoded. If \(\mathcal C\) is \(\U\)-small, its path localization is \(\U\)-small. If \(\mathcal C\) is only locally \(\U\)-small, its localization need not be locally \(\U\)-small.

    Proof.
    The edge collection is a set, and the finite paths form a subset of a union, indexed by \(\N\text{,}\) of finite powers of the edge collection, together with the empty paths labeled by objects. Taking a quotient and recording endpoints still gives a set. If all the original category data lie in \(\U\text{,}\) then \(W\text{,}\) the edges, and the finite-word set lie in \(\U\text{,}\) using \(\N\in\U\text{.}\) Subsets, quotient sets, and the endpoint data also lie in \(\U\text{.}\) The counterexample below proves the last assertion.

    Example 10.6 Many intermediate objects create a large Hom set.

    Take objects \(A,B\) and \(T_s\) for \(s\in\U\text{,}\) all distinct, and nonidentity arrows
    \begin{equation*} a_s:A\to T_s,\qquad b_s:B\to T_s. \end{equation*}
    There are no other nonidentity arrows. Use the same small singleton of local arrow labels for each indicated pair of endpoints. This category is locally \(\U\)-small, although its object set is not \(\U\)-small. Invert the family \(W=\{b_s:s\in\U\}\text{.}\) For every \(s\) we obtain a morphism
    \begin{equation*} Q(b_s)^{-1}Q(a_s):A\longrightarrow B. \end{equation*}
    These morphisms are distinct. Indeed, form an ordinary set-encoded category with two objects \(P,R\text{,}\) with \(\Hom(P,R)=\U\text{,}\) identities at its objects, and no arrows from \(R\) to \(P\text{.}\) Send \(A\) to \(P\text{,}\) all other objects to \(R\text{,}\) each \(b_s\) to \(\id_R\text{,}\) and \(a_s\) to the label \(s\text{.}\) This is an inverting functor, so its extension sends the displayed morphisms to their distinct labels. Thus the localized Hom set contains at least \(|\U|\) elements and cannot belong to \(\U\text{.}\)
    The target in this example is deliberately not locally \(\U\)-small, which is permitted in the universal property. Existence of the localization as an ordinary category remains intact. Passing to a larger scale can sometimes recover a relative smallness statement, but it should not be silently built into a claim at the original scale.
    Li emphasizes the same separation between general existence and control of morphism sets in [LiII, the discussion after Proposition 1.9.3 and Remark 1.9.15]. The finite-zigzag construction supplies existence, while a calculus of fractions supplies a more usable description. Neither the word fraction nor local smallness of the starting category alone is a bound on the collection of all possible intermediate objects.

    Section 10.4 Saturation and weaker closure conditions

    Inverting the prescribed arrows necessarily inverts further arrows. It is useful to distinguish the original input from the full collection detected by the resulting universal problem.

    Definition 10.7 Localization saturation.

    The saturation of \(W\) is
    \begin{equation*} W^{\mathrm{sat}}= \{f\in\Ar(\mathcal C):Q(f)\text{ is an isomorphism in }\mathcal C[W^{-1}]\}. \end{equation*}
    We call \(W\) saturated when \(W=W^{\mathrm{sat}}\text{.}\) A compatible equivalence between localization models gives the same subset \(W^{\mathrm{sat}}\) of \(\Ar(\mathcal C)\text{.}\)

    Proposition 10.8.

    1. An arrow belongs to \(W^{\mathrm{sat}}\) if and only if every \(W\)-inverting functor to every set-encoded target sends it to an isomorphism. Localizing at \(W^{\mathrm{sat}}\) gives the same universal problem as localizing at \(W\text{,}\) and \((W^{\mathrm{sat}})^{\mathrm{sat}}=W^{\mathrm{sat}}\text{.}\)
    2. The class \(W^{\mathrm{sat}}\) contains all isomorphisms and is closed under composition and retracts in the arrow category. It has the two-out-of-three property: for composable \(f,g\text{,}\) membership of any two of \(f,g,gf\) implies membership of the third. It also has the two-out-of-six property: if \(f,g,h\) are consecutively composable and \(gf,hg\) belong to it, then \(f,g,h,hgf\) all belong to it.

    Proof.
    An inverting functor factors through \(Q\) up to natural isomorphism and hence sends every member of \(W^{\mathrm{sat}}\) to an isomorphism. The converse takes \(Q\) itself as the target functor. Thus the \(W\)-inverting functors and the \(W^{\mathrm{sat}}\)-inverting functors coincide, proving the localization and idempotence assertions.
    It remains to recall the relevant facts about isomorphisms and apply them after \(Q\text{.}\) Composition and two-out-of-three follow by composing inverses. If \(gf\) and \(hg\) are invertible, then \(f(gf)^{-1}\) is a right inverse to \(g\text{,}\) while \((hg)^{-1}h\) is a left inverse. They coincide, so \(g\text{,}\) and then \(f,h,hgf\text{,}\) are invertible.
    For retracts, suppose an arrow \(f:A\to B\) is a retract of an isomorphism \(g:C\to D\text{.}\) Write the two commuting squares using maps \(a:A\to C\text{,}\) \(b:B\to D\text{,}\) \(r:C\to A\text{,}\) \(s:D\to B\text{,}\) with \(ra=\id_A\) and \(sb=\id_B\text{.}\) The commutation equations are \(ga=bf\) and \(fr=sg\text{.}\) Then \(rg^{-1}b\) is an inverse to \(f\text{.}\) Applying this calculation to the images of a retract diagram under \(Q\) proves the claim.
    These closure properties are consequences of saturation, not hypotheses on the original \(W\text{.}\) Adjoining identities and finite composites to \(W\) gives exactly the same inverting functors, but need not produce a saturated set of arrows. Nor do we use weak saturation as an unspecified synonym for localization saturation: an assertion of two-out-of-three or retract closure must state those rules explicitly. For the stronger two-out-of-six rule and inverse images of isomorphisms, compare [Rie, Lemma 6.4.2].
    There is also a source-specific meaning of saturated multiplicative system. Stacks assumes both fraction calculi and adds an algebraic cancellation condition in [Stacks, Tag 05Q8]; [Stacks, Tag 05Q9] proves that its condition is equivalent to equality with the inverse image of isomorphisms. Those extra Ore hypotheses are part of that criterion. We will not apply it to an arbitrary collection \(W\text{.}\)

    Section 10.5 An oriented calculus of fractions

    Arbitrary zigzags are difficult to compare. A fraction calculus gives conditions under which every zigzag reduces to a single roof. The direction of the roof is more reliable than the words left and right, which differ between our sources.

    Definition 10.9 A right multiplicative system, in the Stacks convention.

    A subset \(S\subseteq\Ar(\mathcal C)\) is a right multiplicative system for the conventions of this chapter if it satisfies the following conditions.
    1. Every identity belongs to \(S\text{,}\) and composites of composable members of \(S\) belong to \(S\text{.}\)
    2. For \(f:A\to B\) and \(s:B'\to B\) with \(s\in S\text{,}\) there exist \(t:A'\to A\) in \(S\) and \(g:A'\to B'\) such that \(ft=sg\text{.}\) This is the Ore square condition.
    3. If \(f,g:A\to B\) and \(s:B\to B'\) in \(S\) satisfy \(sf=sg\text{,}\) there is \(t:A'\to A\) in \(S\) with \(ft=gt\text{.}\) This is the cancellation condition.
    A left multiplicative system means a right multiplicative system in \(\mathcal C^\op\text{,}\) with all arrows reversed. A system satisfying both sets of axioms is called a two-sided multiplicative system.
    These are the right-system axioms in [Stacks, Section 4.27, Definition 4.27.1]. They lead to roofs
    \begin{equation*} X\xleftarrow{\ s\ }A\xrightarrow{\ f\ }Y,\qquad s\in S,\qquad Q(f)Q(s)^{-1}:X\to Y. \end{equation*}
    Li calls this a left multiplicative system in [LiII, Definition 1.9.5]. Kashiwara–Schapira use the same handedness as Li and discuss the reversal of names in [KS06, Remark 7.1.8]. Our opposite system gives the other form, \(X\to A\leftarrow Y\text{,}\) interpreted as \(Q(s)^{-1}Q(f)\text{.}\)
    The Ore condition allows an arrow and a reverse denominator to be interchanged inside a path, producing a new denominator. The cancellation condition ensures that equations already forced by an inverted denominator can be witnessed after refining a roof. These serve different purposes: a proposed rule for multiplying roofs is not enough unless their equivalence relation and the choices in that rule are also controlled.

    Section 10.6 Roofs, refinements, and composition

    Fix a right multiplicative system in the preceding orientation. For a fixed source \(X\text{,}\) let \(S/X\) be the full subcategory of \(\mathcal C/X\) whose objects are denominators \(s:A\to X\) in \(S\text{.}\) An arrow from \(s:A\to X\) to \(t:B\to X\) is any \(u:A\to B\) with \(tu=s\text{.}\) We do not require \(u\in S\text{.}\)

    Proposition 10.10.

    The category \(S/X\) is cofiltered. For each \(Y\text{,}\) roofs \((s:A\to X,f:A\to Y)\) are equivalent precisely when they have a common refinement: two roofs \((s_1,f_1)\text{,}\) \((s_2,f_2)\) are equivalent if there exist \(u:B\to A_1\text{,}\) \(v:B\to A_2\) with
    \begin{equation*} s_1u=s_2v\in S,\qquad f_1u=f_2v. \end{equation*}
    This is an equivalence relation, and the roof classes are the ordinary set colimit
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{colim}_{(s:A\to X)\in(S/X)^\op} \Hom_{\mathcal C}(A,Y). \end{equation*}

    Proof.
    The identity of \(X\) is a denominator. For two denominators \(s:A\to X\text{,}\) \(t:B\to X\text{,}\) the Ore condition gives \(r:C\to A\) in \(S\) and \(v:C\to B\) with \(sr=tv\text{.}\) This common composite belongs to \(S\text{,}\) providing a common source in \(S/X\text{.}\) For parallel arrows \(u,v:s\to t\text{,}\) the equation \(tu=tv\) and cancellation give \(r:C\to A\) in \(S\) with \(ur=vr\text{.}\) The denominator \(sr\) and its map to \(s\) equalize the pair. Thus \((S/X)^\op\) is filtered.
    On that opposite category the displayed Hom functor acts by precomposition. Its common-stage relation from Theorem 6.10 is exactly the common-refinement condition. The proof of that relation is an equivalence relation uses only finite witnesses, so it applies at the ordinary-set level even if the denominator category is not \(\U\)-small.
    The refinement maps \(u,v\) need not belong to \(S\text{;}\) their common composite denominator must. Requiring both maps to lie in \(S\) without an additional hypothesis would change the asserted equivalence relation. This filtered-colimit description is also [LiII, Lemma 1.9.8] after translating handedness, and [Stacks, Tag 05Q4].

    Theorem 10.11.

    The roof classes define an identity-on-objects localization of \(\mathcal C\) at \(S\text{.}\) Write a roof class as \([s,f]\text{.}\) For composable roofs \([s,f]:X\to Y\) and \([t,g]:Y\to Z\text{,}\) choose an Ore square
    \begin{equation*} h:P\to A,\quad h\in S,\qquad k:P\to B,\qquad fh=tk, \end{equation*}
    where \(s:A\to X\text{,}\) \(f:A\to Y\text{,}\) \(t:B\to Y\text{,}\) and \(g:B\to Z\text{.}\) Composition and identities are
    \begin{equation*} [t,g]\,[s,f]=[sh,gk],\qquad \id_X=[\id_X,\id_X]. \end{equation*}
    The localization functor sends \(f:X\to Y\) to \([\id_X,f]\text{.}\) For a functor \(F\) inverting \(S\text{,}\) its extension is
    \begin{equation*} \overline F([s,f])=F(f)F(s)^{-1}. \end{equation*}

    Proof.
    The standard verification that this composition is independent of the Ore square and of the roof representatives, and is associative, is [Stacks, Tag 04VH, Lemma 4.27.11], by its dual reference to Lemma 4.27.2. A proof in the present roof orientation is [LiII, Proposition 1.9.9 and Theorem 1.9.10], with that author’s left-system terminology. The denominator and refinement conventions in those results are exactly the ones stated here.
    To verify the universal property, observe first that the inverse of \(Q(s)\text{,}\) for \(s:A\to X\) in \(S\text{,}\) is the roof \([s,\id_A]:X\to A\text{.}\) Every roof is consequently \(Q(f)Q(s)^{-1}\text{,}\) so the asserted extension is forced. It respects common refinements: if \(d=s_1u=s_2v\in S\) and \(f_1u=f_2v\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} F(f_1)F(s_1)^{-1} =F(f_1u)F(d)^{-1} =F(f_2v)F(d)^{-1} =F(f_2)F(s_2)^{-1}. \end{equation*}
    For composition, applying \(F\) to \(fh=tk\) and inverting \(F(h),F(t)\) gives the required equality between the extension of \([sh,gk]\) and the composite of the two extended roofs. Identities are preserved. This proves strict existence and uniqueness of the extension; natural transformations extend by the same inverse-arrow argument as before.
    In particular, for parallel original arrows \(f,g:X\to Y\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} Q(f)=Q(g)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad ft=gt\text{ for some }t:X'\to X\text{ in }S. \end{equation*}
    More generally, roofs with the same denominator \(s:A\to X\) are equal exactly when their numerators become equal after precomposition by one member of \(S\) into \(A\text{;}\) see [Stacks, Tag 04VJ]. The former criterion is [LiII, Corollary 1.9.13] in the translated orientation.
    To see the extra step in the same-denominator assertion, the filtered-colimit criterion first gives \(q:B\to A\) with \(sq\in S\) and \(fq=gq\text{.}\) This does not say \(q\in S\text{.}\) Apply Ore to \(s:A\to X\) and \(sq:B\to X\text{,}\) obtaining \(t:C\to A\) in \(S\) and \(r:C\to B\) with \(st=sqr\text{.}\) Cancellation against \(s\) gives \(e:D\to C\) in \(S\) with \(te=qre\text{.}\) Hence \(fte=gte\text{,}\) and \(te\in S\) is the required single denominator refinement. The converse is immediate from common refinement.
    The opposite construction handles left fractions. If both calculi hold, their two roof models are canonically isomorphic over \(\mathcal C\) by the strict universal property, as in [Stacks, Tag 04VL]. The arbitrary path model remains available when no Ore calculus holds; a two-arrow roof representation is an additional conclusion under these axioms.

    Proposition 10.12.

    For a right multiplicative system in our orientation, the localization functor preserves every finite limit that exists in \(\mathcal C\text{.}\) Dually, a left-fraction localization preserves existing finite colimits.

    Proof.
    Let \(D:K\to\mathcal C\) have finite index and a limit \(L\text{.}\) For each object \(X\text{,}\) the roof formula gives bijections
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \Hom_{\mathcal C[S^{-1}]}(QX,QL) &\cong\operatorname{colim}_{(s:A\to X)\in(S/X)^\op} \Hom_{\mathcal C}(A,L)\\ &\cong\operatorname{colim}_{s}\lim_{k\in K}\Hom_{\mathcal C}(A,Dk)\\ &\cong\lim_{k\in K}\operatorname{colim}_{s}\Hom_{\mathcal C}(A,Dk)\\ &\cong\lim_{k\in K}\Hom_{\mathcal C[S^{-1}]}(QX,QDk). \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    The middle interchange is the filtered-colimit theorem of Theorem 6.13, since \((S/X)^\op\) is filtered. If the indexing category is larger than \(\U\text{,}\) use the same finite-representative proof for these ordinary sets; no assertion of membership in \(\U\) is required. The bijections are induced by the images of the original projections. Every object of the localized category is \(QX\) for an object \(X\text{,}\) so the Hom criterion proves that those images form a limit cone. Opposites give the dual assertion.
    This is [LiII, Lemma 1.9.16] in the translated handedness, and [Stacks, Lemma 4.27.17]. Preservation concerns cones on diagrams already given in \(\mathcal C\text{,}\) with a limit there. It does not say that every diagram in the localized category comes from such a diagram, or that missing limits automatically appear. The proof explains the role of filteredness: morphisms in the localization are filtered colimits of Hom sets, so the finite equations specifying a cone can be imposed at one denominator stage.
    The same formula gives a useful local size criterion. Suppose \(\mathcal C\) is locally \(\U\)-small and, for each \(X\text{,}\) the filtered category \((S/X)^\op\) admits a final functor from a \(\U\)-small category. Reindexing computes each displayed Hom colimit using a small diagram of sets in \(\U\text{.}\) It therefore gives a bijection with a set belonging to \(\U\text{.}\) This is the size criterion discussed in [LiII, Remark 1.9.15].
    Our strict membership convention needs a qualification: the original set of roof classes may contain external object labels, so the bijection does not put that particular encoding inside \(\U\text{.}\) One can use the small colimit sets as new local morphism sets and transport identities and composition across the bijections. This yields a locally \(\U\)-small presentation with the same objects. Reindexings chosen over the set of source objects require only set-indexed choice. If \(\mathcal C\) is already \(\U\)-small, this relabeling issue is absent for the formal path construction, by Proposition 10.5.

    Section 10.7 Monoids and the effect on arrows

    One-object categories make the formulas concrete without changing the categorical problem. The object stays fixed, and all the information lies in its endomorphism monoid.

    Example 10.13 Commutative fractions and group completion.

    Let \(M\in\U\) be a commutative monoid and \(S\subseteq M\) a submonoid containing the identity. The right Ore square exists by commutativity: for elements \(f,s\text{,}\) take \(t=s\) and \(g=f\text{.}\) If \(sf=sg\text{,}\) then \(fs=gs\text{,}\) giving cancellation with \(t=s\text{.}\) Thus the fraction calculus applies, without assuming that \(M\) is cancellative.
    Its endomorphisms are fractions \(a/s\text{,}\) with the relation
    \begin{equation*} a/s=b/t\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u t a=u s b\text{ for some }u\in S. \end{equation*}
    Multiplication is \((a/s)(b/t)=ab/(st)\text{.}\) For example, the displayed witness gives a common refinement by multiplying the first roof by \(ut\) and the second by \(us\text{.}\) Conversely, from a common refinement \(sv=tw=d\in S\) and \(av=bw\text{,}\) multiplication shows \(dta=dsb\text{.}\) This proves the stated relation directly from the roof criterion.
    For \(S=M\text{,}\) all endomorphisms become invertible and the result is the group completion of the commutative monoid. For the additive monoid \((\N,+)\text{,}\) the fraction represented by \((n,m)\) is the integer \(n-m\text{,}\) giving \((\Z,+)\text{.}\) The arrows outside the image of the localization functor correspond to the negative integers.
    Even a composition-closed denominator set can be smaller than its saturation. In \((\N,+)\text{,}\) take \(S=2\N\text{.}\) Inverting the element \(2\) also inverts \(1\text{,}\) whose inverse is represented by \(1-2\text{.}\) Thus every original arrow belongs to \(S^{\mathrm{sat}}\text{,}\) although \(1\notin S\text{.}\)
    Localization can also identify distinct arrows. In the monoid \(\{1,0\}\) with absorbing zero, inverting \(0\) forces \(1=0\text{,}\) since \(0\cdot1=0\cdot0\) can then be canceled. The localized monoid is trivial. More generally, whenever \(sf=sg\) and \(s\) is inverted, \(Q(f)=Q(g)\) follows. A localization functor is therefore neither automatically faithful nor automatically full.
    Inverting the only nonidentity arrow of \([1]\) gives the category with two distinct objects joined by a unique isomorphism in each direction. It is equivalent to the terminal category, but the path model has not identified the objects. This separates adjoining inverses from a literal quotient of the object set. An inverting functor may send these two objects to different isomorphic objects, so strict factorization through a one-object quotient would be too restrictive.
    If every arrow of a category is inverted, the result is its universal groupoid: every path class is invertible, and every functor to a groupoid factors through it. This operation retains more information than connected components. For a one-object category, there is only one component, but its universal group can be nontrivial, as the example of \(\N\) shows. If \(\mathcal C\) is already a groupoid, its path localization functor is an isomorphism. Thus adjoining inverses and replacing each connected component by a single point are different constructions.

    Section 10.8 Reflective localizations

    A reflective subcategory provides a different presentation of localization: each object has a universal map to an object satisfying a chosen condition. Unlike the path construction, this presentation may replace the original objects by representatives in a full subcategory.

    Proposition 10.14.

    Suppose \(L:\mathcal C\rightleftarrows\mathcal A:i\) is an adjunction \(L\dashv i\) with \(i\) fully faithful. Put
    \begin{equation*} W_L=\{f\in\Ar(\mathcal C):L(f)\text{ is invertible}\}. \end{equation*}
    Then \(L\) is a localization at \(W_L\) in the functor-category sense. The induced functor \(\mathcal C[W_L^{-1}]\to\mathcal A\) is an equivalence. The associated monad \(iL\) is idempotent, as developed in Chapter 9.

    Proof.
    Write \(\eta:\id_{\mathcal C}\Rightarrow iL\) and \(\epsilon:Li\Rightarrow\id_{\mathcal A}\) for unit and counit. Full faithfulness makes \(\epsilon\) invertible. The triangle identity implies that \(L(\eta_X)\) is inverse to \(\epsilon_{LX}\text{,}\) so each \(\eta_X\) belongs to \(W_L\text{.}\)
    For any \(F:\mathcal C\to\mathcal D\) inverting \(W_L\text{,}\) the transformation \(F\eta:F\Rightarrow FiL\) is a natural isomorphism. Thus \(F\) factors up to natural isomorphism through \(L\text{,}\) using \(Fi\text{.}\) More precisely, restriction along \(i\) is a quasi-inverse to \(L^*:\Fun(\mathcal A,\mathcal D)\to\Fun_{W_L}(\mathcal C,\mathcal D)\text{.}\) The two required natural isomorphisms are supplied by \(H\epsilon:HLi\cong H\) for a functor \(H\) on \(\mathcal A\text{,}\) and by \(F\eta:F\cong FiL\) for an inverting \(F\text{.}\) These comparisons are also natural in transformations, by naturality and whiskering. Hence \(L^*\) is an equivalence. Comparison with the path model now follows from Proposition 10.4.
    For this example of localization, the universal property naturally uses equivalence of functor categories, since the reflector need not be the identity on objects. The path model and the reflective model answer the same universal problem, but their object sets can be very different.
    A general localization need not have a fully faithful right adjoint. The localization of the one-object category \((\N,+)\) to \((\Z,+)\) is an elementary example. A fully faithful functor back would induce an injective monoid homomorphism \(\Z\to\N\text{.}\) Such a homomorphism must send each integer to an invertible element of \(\N\text{,}\) whose only invertible element is zero, so it cannot be injective. Consequently this localization has no reflective presentation inside the original one-object category. The reflector hypothesis adds substantive structure beyond universal inversion.
    One can recognize the objects in the reflective image by their maps from inverted arrows. Call \(Z\in\mathcal C\) \(W_L\)-local if
    \begin{equation*} \Hom_{\mathcal C}(Y,Z)\longrightarrow\Hom_{\mathcal C}(X,Z),\qquad a\longmapsto aw \end{equation*}
    is bijective for every \(w:X\to Y\) in \(W_L\text{.}\) These are exactly the objects isomorphic to some \(iA\text{.}\) Indeed, adjunction proves the condition for \(iA\text{.}\) Conversely, localness at \(\eta_Z\) supplies \(r:iLZ\to Z\) with \(r\eta_Z=\id_Z\text{.}\) The object \(iLZ\) is itself local, so injectivity of precomposition by \(\eta_Z\) gives \(\eta_Zr=\id_{iLZ}\text{.}\) Thus \(\eta_Z\) is an isomorphism, as required.

    Example 10.15 A reflection that does not preserve equalizers.

    The full subcategory of \(\Set_\U\) on the empty set and a chosen singleton is reflective. Its reflector sends an empty set to the empty set and every nonempty set to the singleton. To check the adjunction, maps into the singleton are always unique, while a map into the empty set exists exactly when the source is empty. These are precisely the two Hom comparisons required for the reflector. The empty set is retained as a separate object, so the reflection records whether an element exists without choosing one.
    Take two distinct maps from a singleton to a two-element set. Their equalizer is empty. After reflection both maps are the identity of the singleton, whose equalizer is the singleton. Thus this localization fails to preserve an existing finite limit.
    Its inverted class also fails the right cancellation axiom above. Postcompose the two distinct maps by the map from the two-element set to a singleton; this map is inverted and makes the composites equal. A proposed denominator into the original singleton would have to have nonempty source. Precomposing the two distinct constant maps by such a function cannot make them equal. This explains directly why the right-fraction preservation proposition does not apply. Reflective localization and a particular Ore calculus are distinct additional structures on the general localization problem.

    Example 10.16 A closure operator on a poset.

    Let \(P\) be a poset with a monotone map \(c:P\to P\) satisfying \(x\le c(x)\) and \(c(c(x))=c(x)\text{.}\) Its fixed points form a full subposet \(P_c\text{.}\) For a fixed point \(a\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} c(x)\le a\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\le a, \end{equation*}
    so \(c:P\to P_c\) is left adjoint to inclusion. It localizes the arrows \(x\le y\) for which \(c(x)=c(y)\text{.}\) The localized Hom set from \(x\) to \(y\) is a singleton if \(c(x)\le c(y)\text{,}\) and empty otherwise, by the equivalence with \(P_c\text{.}\)
    For a concrete choice, round each natural number up to the next even number. The fixed-point poset is the even numbers, and the arrows from each odd number to its next even number become invertible. In particular, there is a new arrow from \(2\) to \(1\) in the localization. The fixed-point presentation chooses one representative for each such pair; the path presentation retains both objects and makes them isomorphic.
    These examples illustrate two useful ways to control localization: explicit fractions compute the altered morphism sets, while a reflection identifies a category of representatives through an adjunction. General localization assumes neither description. Its common foundation remains the universal factorization of functors and natural transformations.
