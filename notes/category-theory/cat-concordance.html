    Appendix A. Notation, terminology, and construction guide

    This appendix collects conventions that are easy to confuse when moving between the chapters or their sources. The linked definitions give the full hypotheses and remain the reference for each term. The construction guide records the universal problem being solved, so that similar-looking enlargements of a category can be distinguished.

    Section A.1 Size and basic notation

    \(\U\in\V\text{;}\) small, locally small, essentially small
    Small sets are actual members of \(\U\text{.}\) A small category has its object set and total arrow set in \(\U\text{;}\) local smallness concerns its actual local Hom labels. Essentially small means equivalent to a small category. A bijection with a small set gives a cardinality bound, but does not put externally named elements inside \(\U\text{.}\) Relabeling must be stated when it is needed. All categories remain set-encoded; a larger universe controls an entire encoding only when the relevant assumption is made. See Definition 0.1 and Proposition 0.2.
    \(=\text{,}\) \(\cong\text{,}\) and \(\simeq\)
    These indicate equality, isomorphism of objects, and equivalence of categories, respectively. Uniqueness up to unique isomorphism includes compatibility with the specified universal structure. Forgetting that structure can leave many automorphisms; see Section 0.1.
    \(\N\text{,}\) \([n]\text{,}\) and \(\underline n\)
    We take \(\N=\{0,1,\ldots\}\text{.}\) The ordinal category \([n]\) has objects \(0,\ldots,n\text{,}\) with one arrow from a smaller object to a larger one, including identities. Thus \([1]\) is the walking arrow. The finite cardinal \(\underline n=\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\) has \(n\) elements, and \(\underline0=\varnothing\text{.}\) All functions between these finite sets form the category \(\mathsf{Fin}\) of Section 12.7.

    Section A.2 Variance and adjoint notation

    \(h_X\) and \(h^X\)
    The representables are \(h_X=\Hom_{\mathcal C}(-,X)\) and \(h^X=\Hom_{\mathcal C}(X,-)\text{.}\) Their test-object variances are opposite. In the representing object, \(X\mapsto h_X\) is covariant and \(X\mapsto h^X\) is contravariant. Presheaf means a functor \(\mathcal C^\op\to\Set_\U\text{;}\) \(\widehat{\mathcal C}\) denotes their category when \(\mathcal C\) is small. See Definition 3.1.
    \(\operatorname{el}(P)\)
    For a presheaf, an arrow \((c,x)\to(d,y)\) is an \(f:c\to d\) satisfying \(P(f)y=x\text{.}\) A universal element is terminal in this element category. For a covariant set-valued functor the condition becomes \(P(f)x=y\text{,}\) and a universal element is initial. Both conventions and their slice descriptions occur in Definition 3.6 and Proposition 3.7.
    \(F\dashv G\)
    The functor on the left is the left adjoint. For \(F:\mathcal C\to\mathcal D\text{,}\) its unit is \(\id_{\mathcal C}\Rightarrow GF\) and its counit is \(FG\Rightarrow\id_{\mathcal D}\text{;}\) see Definition 5.1. In particular, free algebras satisfy \(F^T\dashv U^T\text{,}\) while cofree coalgebras satisfy \(U_K\dashv R_K\text{.}\)
    Mates and conjugates
    With \(F\dashv G\) and \(F'\dashv G'\text{,}\) the specified connecting functors give \(\Nat(FH,KF')\cong\Nat(HG',GK)\text{.}\) We write \(\alpha^\sharp\) for the mate towards the right adjoints; its typed source and target determine the convention. Conjugation is the case where the connecting functors are identities and reverses composition. General mates need not preserve invertibility. See Definition 5.11 and Example 5.14.
    \(u_!\dashv u^*\dashv u_*\)
    For ordinary diagrams, \(u^*\) is precomposition with \(u\text{,}\) and its two adjoints are left and right Kan extension when they exist. For presheaves on \(\mathcal A,\mathcal B\text{,}\) where \(u:\mathcal A\to\mathcal B\text{,}\) restriction instead uses \(u^\op\text{.}\) Consequently the left-extension formula uses \((b\downarrow u)^\op\text{,}\) and the right-extension formula uses \((u\downarrow b)^\op\text{.}\) See Definition 7.1 and Section 7.9.
    \(\Sigma_f\dashv f^*\dashv\Pi_f\)
    These functors act between slices. For \(f:A\to B\text{,}\) composition \(\Sigma_f\) and dependent product \(\Pi_f\) go from \(\mathcal C/A\) to \(\mathcal C/B\text{;}\) pullback \(f^*\) goes back. Pullbacks supply the first adjunction; the second requires an additional existence condition. This use of a star has a different typed domain from restriction of diagrams. See Definition 14.6 and Definition 14.8.
    \(\int_c\) and \(\int^c\)
    For a bifunctor with opposite variances, a subscript denotes an end and a superscript a coend. They impose compatibility between those variances; the diagonal assignment need not itself be a functor. Ends describe compatible families, including natural transformations; coends describe identifications, including density presentations. See Definition 8.1. The notation \(\int_{\mathcal B}F\) for an indexed family of categories in Proposition 16.7 instead denotes its Grothendieck construction. Its input and universal problem are different.

    Section A.3 Terminology across sources

    Final, cofinal, and initial
    Here final and cofinal are synonyms: \((i\downarrow u)\) must be nonempty and connected. Final functors reindex colimits. Initial, or coinitial, uses \((u\downarrow i)\) and reindexes limits. Kashiwara–Schapira call the latter co-cofinal. Translate the comma condition and the domain of the diagram together; see Definition 6.3.
    Filtered, filtrant, and directed
    Filtrant is another name for filtered. Our definition includes nonemptiness, common receiving objects, and eventual equalization of parallel arrows. Directed posets are special cases; passing to a directed replacement is a theorem, not part of the definition. Cofiltered means that the opposite category is filtered. See Definition 6.7.
    Presentable and compact
    A \(\kappa\)-presentable object preserves small \(\kappa\)-filtered colimits through its Hom functor. Li calls this \(\kappa\)-compact, and calls the finite case compact. Our terminology keeps the particular preservation condition visible; see Definition 13.3. The ambient category is essential data.
    Sifted, filtered, and reflexive
    Siftedness requires commutation of colimits with finite products of sets. Filteredness gives the stronger commutation with all finite limits. The indexing category of a reflexive coequalizer is sifted but not filtered. Reflexive means that the two arrows have a common section; it does not mean that their coequalizer has the splitting data of Definition 9.16. See Definition 6.18 and Proposition 6.21.
    Small projective and projective
    Small projective means preservation of every small colimit through Hom, including the empty colimit. Unqualified projective means existence of lifts against every epimorphism. Regular projective restricts the test to regular epimorphisms, while relative projectivity specifies an arrow class. Finite presentability tests preservation of filtered colimits. See Definition 11.9, Definition 15.17, and the comparison below.
    Finitary and finitely presentable
    A finitary functor preserves small filtered colimits. An object is finitely presentable when its Hom functor has this property; finite presentability concerns that object inside a specified category. Finite accessibility of a category additionally requires a small family giving filtered presentations. On sets, a finitary endofunctor also preserves sifted colimits, by Proposition 9.13. The proof uses finite powers of sets and does not establish that equivalence for arbitrary ambient categories.
    Lawvere theories and algebraic theories
    Our Lawvere theories are single-sorted and have operations with finitely many arguments, including constants. Neither the set of operations nor a presentation by symbols and equations is required to be finite. Models preserve finite products by comparison isomorphisms; their values need not literally be chosen powers of one set. Many-sorted theories and finite-limit theories impose different categorical structure. See Definition 18.1 and Theorem 18.5.
    Generating, separating, and detecting isomorphisms
    Our generating families separate parallel arrows by their Hom tests; cogenerating families do so dually. Riehl calls them separating and coseparating. Kashiwara–Schapira’s corrected system of generators instead requires its Hom tests jointly to detect isomorphisms. A bijective monotone map need not be a poset isomorphism, so singleton tests separate maps of posets without detecting all isomorphisms. Nor can componentwise tests automatically be replaced by their product: an empty factor can hide the others. See Definition 5.29 and the source comparison and counterexamples immediately following it.
    Two meanings of idempotent
    An idempotent endomorphism satisfies \(e^2=e\text{.}\) An idempotent monad has invertible multiplication \(T^2\Rightarrow T\text{,}\) with its specified monad laws. The latter need not be a literal equality of endofunctors. See Definition 11.1 and Definition 9.23.
    Right and left fractions
    Our right multiplicative systems follow Stacks and give roofs \(X\leftarrow A\to Y\text{,}\) with the left leg inverted. Li and Kashiwara–Schapira call this handedness left. The roof and its axioms determine the translation; see Definition 10.9.
    Monad algebra and module
    Our \(T\)-algebras are Li’s \(T\)-modules. The structure is a map \(TA\to A\) satisfying two laws, without an implicit scalar action or additive structure. Coalgebras reverse the structure map and the laws. See Definition 9.3 and Definition 9.25.
    Cartesian closed and locally cartesian closed
    Cartesian closure requires finite products and exponentials. Local cartesian closure here requires finite limits and cartesian closure of every slice. The slice criterion itself only needs pullbacks; the global terminal object is a separate condition. See Definition 14.1 and Theorem 14.9.
    Regular, strong, and effective
    A regular epimorphism is a coequalizer; a strong epimorphism has a unique lifting property against monomorphisms. They agree in a regular category, whose finite limits and stable image factorizations are additional hypotheses. An effective equivalence relation is a kernel pair, whereas an arbitrary internal equivalence relation need not be effective. Barr exactness requires every such relation to be effective. These uses of regular and exact do not assume additive structure; see Definition 15.2 and Definition 15.13.
    Fibration, cleavage, and indexed category
    A fibration is a functor with specified cartesian lifting properties. A cleavage chooses lifts; it produces restriction functors with coherent comparison isomorphisms. A split cleavage makes those comparisons identities. Coherent indexing data recover a category over the base by the Grothendieck construction. Equality of restrictions, natural isomorphism between restrictions, and equivalence of total categories over the base are different assertions. See Definition 16.3, Definition 16.6, and Proposition 16.9.
    Monoidal, tensor, and closed
    Monoidal here includes a unit and specified associativity and unit constraints. Tensor category has additional or different requirements in some sources. A closed structure supplies adjoints to tensoring, whereas a dual object supplies those adjoints by tensoring with a fixed object. For lax and oplax functors, the displayed direction of the comparison maps fixes the meaning. For left and right internal Hom objects, the side of the fixed tensor factor fixes it. See Definition 17.1, Definition 17.5, Definition 17.13, and Definition 17.16.

    Section A.4 Choosing a construction

    Presheaves: freely add small colimits
    For small \(\mathcal C\text{,}\) Yoneda embeds it into the locally small, generally larger category \(\widehat{\mathcal C}\text{.}\) Every presheaf is a small colimit of representables. Functors to a cocomplete target extend essentially uniquely to colimit-preserving functors; see Theorem 8.9.
    Karoubi completion: split idempotents
    \(\operatorname{Kar}(\mathcal C)\) adjoins splittings of idempotent endomorphisms. Local smallness is preserved, and a small source gives a small completion. Its universal targets are idempotent-complete categories, without a general cocompleteness assumption. See Definition 11.5 and Theorem 11.7.
    Ind and Pro: add filtered or cofiltered constructions
    For small \(\mathcal C\text{,}\) \(\operatorname{Ind}_{\U}(\mathcal C)\) freely adds small filtered colimits; its opposite counterpart \(\operatorname{Pro}_{\U}(\mathcal C)\) freely adds small cofiltered limits. Both are locally small and generally larger than their source. They solve these particular extension problems, rather than automatically adding all colimits or all limits. See Theorem 12.7 and Proposition 12.11. A pro object is a formal inverse system; maps between realized limits need not come from pro maps.
    Localization and reflection
    Localization makes specified arrows invertible, universally. It always has a set-encoded path model, but local smallness needs care for a source that is only locally small. A reflection instead provides a universal arrow into a full subcategory. It yields a localization at the arrows the reflector inverts; a general localization need not be a reflection inside its original category. See Definition 10.1, Proposition 10.5, and Proposition 10.14.

    Section A.5 Tests involving representables

    For a small category \(\mathcal C\text{,}\) the following statements concern different ambient categories. Their similar conclusions should not be substituted for one another.
    Small projectives in \(\widehat{\mathcal C}\)
    These are precisely retracts of a single representable, by Theorem 11.10.
    Projectives in \(\widehat{\mathcal C}\)
    These are precisely retracts of small coproducts of representables, by Proposition 15.25. The lifting test allows such a coproduct; preservation of every small colimit by Hom forces a retract of one representable.
    Finitely presentable objects in \(\operatorname{Ind}(\mathcal C)\)
    These are again retracts of a single representable, now because only filtered colimits within Ind are tested; see Proposition 12.6.
    Finitely presentable objects in \(\widehat{\mathcal C}\)
    These are precisely finite colimits of representables, including the empty finite colimit. Allowing retracts gives the same class, since a retract is obtained by one more finite coequalizer; see Theorem 13.10. An inclusion of full subcategories does not by itself identify their internal presentability conditions.

    Section A.6 Reading paths

    The foundational chapters run from Chapter 0 through Chapter 5: categories, universal constructions, Yoneda, limits, and adjunctions. Then Chapter 6, Chapter 7, and Chapter 8 develop reindexing, extension, and density.
    For reconstruction from operations, continue to Chapter 9. The optional chapter Chapter 18 connects finitary monads on sets with finite operations and equations; it can be read after Chapter 9, independently of the intervening chapters. For inversion of arrows, use Chapter 10. For completions and finiteness, follow Chapter 11, Chapter 12, and Chapter 13. For function objects and families over varying bases, use Chapter 14 and Chapter 16. For images and quotients, use Chapter 15. For multiplication, its constraints, and internal function objects, use Chapter 17. Each chapter states its dependencies and size assumptions.
    When checking whether a hypothesis can be dropped, use Appendix B to locate representative obstructions. Its entries lead back to the full constructions. The index locates definitions and results, including alternate names recorded in these notes.

    Section A.7 Using the references

    The references serve different purposes. The chapter introductions identify the principal sources; citations beside a definition or proof identify the particular comparison being made. The following guide groups the reading by the question under study. It does not assert that the sources use identical hypotheses or terminology.
    Language, universal properties, and adjunctions
    For Chapter 1 through Chapter 5, read [LiI, Sections 2.1–2.7] alongside [Rie, Chapters 1–4]. [KS06, Chapters 1–2] supplies a systematic universe-based comparison. The adjoint existence theorems also use [LiII, Section 1.11] and Riehl Section 4.7. Keep the actual Hom-label convention and the corrected KS generator definition in view when translating.
    Reindexing, extension, and density
    For Chapter 6 and Chapter 7, use [LiII, Sections 1.6–1.8]; compare Riehl Sections 3.8 and 6.1–6.3. For Chapter 8, use [Lor21, Sections 1.1–1.4 and 2.2], Riehl Section 6.5, and LiII Appendix A.1. These last comparisons use ordinary set-valued weights only; their more general settings need not be developed to use the stated results.
    Algebras, inversion, and completions
    For Chapter 9, use Riehl Chapter 5 and LiII Section 7.6. For Chapter 10, compare LiII Section 1.9, KS Section 7.1, and [Stacks, Section 4.27], translating the handedness of fractions. For Chapter 12, use LiII Appendix B and KS Chapter 6; Stacks Section 4.22 gives diagram formulas. The ordinary idempotent-completion arguments in Chapter 11 are proved here, including their universal properties, without importing the additive setting of LiII’s corresponding exercise.
    Presentability, images, and categories over a base
    For Chapter 13, LiII Appendix A.2 provides a concise entry to the structural results, with [AR94] as the specialized reference. For Chapter 15, use [Bar71, Chapter I] and [Rie08, Section 1]. For Chapter 16, use Stacks Sections 4.33, 4.36, and 4.38. Each of these branches requires its own structural hypotheses; a statement about one class of categories should not be transferred to another merely because the examples overlap.
    Function objects and multiplication
    For Chapter 14, compare Riehl Sections 4.4 and 4.6. For Chapter 17, use LiI Sections 3.1–3.3, LiII Section 7.1, and KS Section 4.2; monoidal coherence is also referenced to [ML98, Chapter VII, Section 2]. The displayed comparison maps fix the conventions when strong, lax, and oplax structures are discussed.
    Finite operations and equations
    For Chapter 18, use [Bor94, Chapter 3] for algebraic theories and [Gar13, Sections 4–6] for the correspondence with finitary monads and its interpretation in categories with finite powers. The constructions in the chapter use ordinary categories and the colimit results already established in these notes.
