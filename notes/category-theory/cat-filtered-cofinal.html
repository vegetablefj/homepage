    Chapter 6 Filtered diagrams and cofinality

    A diagram often contains more stages than are needed to compute its colimit. Cofinality identifies changes of indexing that preserve every cocone, hence preserve the universal problem itself. Filteredness has a different role: it allows finitely many stages and finitely many equations to be handled at one later stage. Together these ideas explain the particularly concrete behavior of filtered colimits of sets.
    Our indexing categories are \(\U\)-small unless another size is explicitly stated. Ambient categories are set-encoded and locally \(\U\)-small. Thus the sets, colimits, and functor categories used below have the size bounds established earlier. We use the cone conventions of Chapter 4 and the adjunctions of Chapter 5. The principal sources are [LiII, Section 1.6], [LiI, Definition 2.7.6 and the following discussion], and [Rie, Section 3.8]. We compare terminology at the relevant definitions.

    Section 6.1 Connected categories and components

    The connectedness needed here is a condition on finite chains of arrows. Arrows in a chain may point in either direction; they need not be invertible, and their composites need not exist along the direction in which the chain is traversed.

    Definition 6.1 Connectedness and the component set.

    Two objects of a category \(I\) are in the same component if they can be joined by a finite zigzag
    \begin{equation*} i=i_0\longleftrightarrow i_1\longleftrightarrow\cdots\longleftrightarrow i_n=i', \end{equation*}
    where each displayed step is an arrow in either direction. A zigzag of length zero is allowed. This is an equivalence relation on \(\Ob(I)\text{;}\) its quotient is denoted \(\pi_0(I)\text{.}\) The category \(I\) is connected if it is nonempty and any two objects are in the same component, equivalently if \(\pi_0(I)\) is a singleton.
    Reflexivity is supplied by the length-zero zigzag, symmetry by reversing its traversal, and transitivity by concatenation. Reversing the traversal does not reverse any arrow in the category. One can write zigzags in alternating directions by inserting identity arrows, so conventions that specify an alternating pattern give the same notion. The empty category has empty component set and is not connected. Each component determines a full subcategory, and there are no arrows between distinct such subcategories.
    For example, a category with an initial or a terminal object is connected: every object is joined to that distinguished object by one arrow. A nonempty poset with a greatest element is connected. A one-object category is connected regardless of its endomorphism monoid. Connectedness therefore says nothing by itself about uniqueness of arrows or about the possibility of equalizing parallel arrows.

    Proposition 6.2.

    For a small category \(I\text{,}\) let \(\Delta A:I\to\Set_\U\) be the constant diagram at a set \(A\in\U\text{,}\) with identity transition maps. Then
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{colim}_I\Delta A\cong\pi_0(I)\times A. \end{equation*}
    In particular, \(\operatorname{colim}_I\Delta1\cong\pi_0(I)\text{.}\) More generally, for \(F:I\to\Set_\U\text{,}\) there is a canonical bijection
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{colim}_I F\cong\pi_0\bigl(\operatorname{el}(F)\bigr), \end{equation*}
    with the covariant element-category convention of Definition 3.6.

    Proof.
    A cocone from \(\Delta A\) to \(S\) is a family of maps \(A\to S\) constant along every arrow, hence constant on each component. Such a family is exactly a map \(\pi_0(I)\times A\to S\text{.}\) For the second assertion, the quotient construction of a set colimit identifies \((i,x)\) with \((j,F(a)(x))\) for each \(a:i\to j\text{.}\) These are exactly the identifications generated by the arrows in \(\operatorname{el}(F)\text{,}\) so its component relation is the colimit relation.
    For a nonempty connected index, a cocone under a constant diagram must use the same map at every object. Nonemptiness provides a map to recover, and connectedness makes it independent of the chosen object. These are separate requirements; both will recur in the proof of the cofinality theorem. Component sets also make sense for all our set-encoded categories. Smallness is needed above to place the displayed constructions in \(\Set_\U\text{,}\) rather than merely to form them as ordinary sets.

    Section 6.2 Final and initial functors

    Let \(u:J\to I\) be a functor. To extend a cocone on the reindexed diagram to a stage \(i\) of the original diagram, we need arrows \(i\to u(j)\text{.}\) Their choices and comparisons are organized by a comma category, not just by the collection of objects in the image of \(u\text{.}\)

    Definition 6.3 The two cofinality conditions.

    1. The functor \(u:J\to I\) is final, or cofinal, if \((i\downarrow u)\) is nonempty and connected for every \(i\in I\text{.}\) Its objects are pairs \((j,a:i\to u(j))\text{;}\) an arrow to \((j',a')\) is \(b:j\to j'\) with \(u(b)a=a'\text{.}\)
    2. The functor \(u\) is initial, or coinitial, if \((u\downarrow i)\) is nonempty and connected for every \(i\in I\text{.}\) Its objects are pairs \((j,a:u(j)\to i)\text{;}\) an arrow to \((j',a')\) satisfies \(a'u(b)=a\text{.}\)
    Thus \(u\) is initial exactly when \(u^\op:J^\op\to I^\op\) is final. A subcategory has either property when its inclusion does.
    Connected already includes nonempty in our convention; we display both words to emphasize what the comma condition requires. The first requirement supplies at least one way to reach the reindexed diagram from \(i\text{.}\) The second connects every two such ways by a finite chain of commuting triangles. Merely requiring that every object admit some arrow to the image does not establish finality.
    Our final is the cofinal of [LiII, Definition 1.6.4] and [Stacks, Tag 04E6]. Stacks calls the opposite condition initial, as we do. Kashiwara–Schapira call these conditions cofinal and co-cofinal, respectively, in [KS06, Definition 2.5.1]. Li’s cofinality result is also applied to diagrams on \(I^\op\text{;}\) this is the limit statement for \(u^\op\text{,}\) not a statement that a final \(u\) preserves limits of diagrams on \(I\text{.}\)1

    Example 6.4 Tails and distinguished objects.

    1. The inclusion of a tail \(\{n\in\N:n\ge n_0\}\) into the ordered category \(\N\) is final. For each \(i\text{,}\) its comma category is the nonempty tail of indices at least both \(i\) and \(n_0\text{,}\) and any two have a common upper bound. More generally, an order-preserving \(v:\N\to\N\) is final exactly when its values are unbounded.
    2. A functor \(\mathbf1\to I\) selecting \(t\) is final exactly when \(t\) is terminal. Indeed, \((i\downarrow t)\) is the discrete category of arrows \(i\to t\text{;}\) it is nonempty and connected exactly when there is one such arrow. Dually, selection of an initial object is an initial functor.
    A selected weakly terminal object, meaning one that receives at least one arrow from every object, is therefore insufficient in the second example. Multiple choices of arrows have no comparisons inside the one-object source category. The connectedness condition records precisely this missing uniqueness after comparisons are allowed.
    The empty cases also agree with the definition. There is no final functor from the empty category to a nonempty category, because its comma categories are empty. A functor to the empty category can only have empty source; that unique functor is both final and initial, since there are no target objects to test. Its reindexing theorem is simply the identity comparison for an empty diagram. Keeping these cases visible prevents a vacuous connectedness condition from entering a nonempty indexing problem.

    Section 6.3 Restriction and extension of cocones

    The reindexing theorem compares entire cocone categories, including their maps of vertices. It makes no assumption that the ambient category already has the relevant colimits.

    Theorem 6.5 Cofinality theorem.

    Let \(u:J\to I\) be final and \(D:I\to\mathcal C\) a diagram. Restriction induces an isomorphism of categories over \(\mathcal C\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Cocone}(D)\longrightarrow \operatorname{Cocone}(Du),\qquad (Q,(\lambda_i)_i)\longmapsto(Q,(\lambda_{u(j)})_j). \end{equation*}
    Consequently \(\operatorname{colim}_I D\) exists if and only if \(\operatorname{colim}_J Du\) exists, and the canonical comparison
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{colim}_J Du\longrightarrow\operatorname{colim}_I D \end{equation*}
    is then an isomorphism. Dually, if \(u\) is initial, restriction identifies the cone categories and gives a canonical isomorphism
    \begin{equation*} \lim_I D\longrightarrow\lim_J Du \end{equation*}
    whenever either side exists.

    Proof.
    Let \((Q,(\mu_j)_j)\) be a cocone under \(Du\text{.}\) For each \(i\in I\text{,}\) choose an object \((j,a:i\to u(j))\) of \((i\downarrow u)\) and set
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_i=\mu_jD(a):D(i)\longrightarrow Q. \end{equation*}
    We first check independence of this choice. An arrow \(b:(j,a)\to(j',a')\) in the comma category satisfies \(a'=u(b)a\text{.}\) The cocone equation gives
    \begin{equation*} \mu_{j'}D(a') =\mu_{j'}D(u(b))D(a)=\mu_jD(a). \end{equation*}
    The same equality can be read in either direction. Connectedness therefore proves equality along a zigzag between any two choices.
    For an arrow \(c:i\to i'\text{,}\) choose \(a':i'\to u(j)\text{.}\) The composite \(a'c\) is an allowed choice for \(i\text{,}\) so independence gives
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_{i'}D(c)=\mu_jD(a')D(c) =\mu_jD(a'c)=\lambda_i. \end{equation*}
    Thus \(\lambda\) is a cocone. At \(i=u(j)\text{,}\) the choice \((j,\id_{u(j)})\) gives \(\lambda_{u(j)}=\mu_j\text{;}\) extension followed by restriction is the identity. Conversely, any extension of \(\mu\) must satisfy \(\lambda_i=\lambda_{u(j)}D(a)=\mu_jD(a)\text{,}\) so it is the unique extension. Restriction followed by extension is also the identity.
    It remains to check arrows between cocones. If \(q:Q\to Q'\) satisfies \(q\mu_j=\mu'_j\) at every \(j\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} q\lambda_i=q\mu_jD(a)=\mu'_jD(a)=\lambda'_i. \end{equation*}
    The converse follows by restriction. Hence extension sends the vertex map \(q\) to the same arrow \(q\) and is a strict inverse to restriction. Initial objects of these cocone categories correspond, which proves existence in both directions and the asserted comparison isomorphism. Taking opposite categories proves the statement about limits and initial functors.
    The construction is the cocone proof in [LiII, Proposition 1.6.5]. For the colimit formulation see also [Stacks, Tag 04E7]. Although choices were used to write the formula, independence shows that the extended cocone is determined without any remaining choice. Extension keeps the original cocone’s vertex and determines its structure maps from that cocone and the arrows of the indexing categories.
    The same proof works for arbitrary set-encoded indexing categories: all the families and comma categories still exist as ordinary sets. The smallness convention here ensures that the cone sets and set-valued colimits under discussion lie in \(\U\text{.}\) It is not an additional step in the cocone-extension argument. In particular, the theorem compares existence of two universal objects; it does not require a preexisting colimit functor on the entire ambient category.
    The direction of the comparison is fixed by its structure maps. A colimit cocone for \(D\) restricts to one for \(Du\text{,}\) so the universal property gives a map from the latter colimit to the former. For limits, restriction gives a cone with the original limit as vertex, and the comparison runs the other way. This supplies a reliable way to reconstruct both directions when an indexing opposite is present.
    For an inverse diagram \(E:I^\op\to\mathcal C\text{,}\) a final \(u:J\to I\) does give a limit comparison: its opposite is initial, and the relevant reindexed diagram is \(Eu^\op\) on \(J^\op\text{.}\) The theorem then gives \(\lim_{I^\op}E\cong\lim_{J^\op}Eu^\op\text{.}\) Thus the same unbounded subsequence of an ordered index works for its forward colimit diagrams and its inverse limit diagrams, with the domains explicitly reversed in the latter statement.

    Section 6.4 Tests for finality and the role of adjoints

    The comma condition is sufficient for every diagram, and it is also necessary if reindexing is to work uniformly. A small family of set-valued diagrams already detects it.

    Proposition 6.6.

    For \(u:J\to I\) between small categories, the following conditions are equivalent.
    1. The functor \(u\) is final.
    2. For every \(D:I\to\Set_\U\text{,}\) the canonical map \(\operatorname{colim}_JDu\to\operatorname{colim}_ID\) is bijective.
    3. For every \(i\in I\text{,}\) \(\operatorname{colim}_{j\in J}\Hom_I(i,u(j))\) is a singleton.
    Final functors compose. If \(v:K\to J\) and both \(v\) and \(uv\) are final, then \(u\) is final. A final functor induces a bijection \(\pi_0(J)\to\pi_0(I)\text{.}\) Every right adjoint is final, and every left adjoint is initial.

    Proof.
    The cofinality theorem proves the first implication. For the second, apply the set-valued condition to \(D=\Hom_I(i,-)\text{.}\) Its colimit is a singleton: every element \(a:i\to i'\) is the image of \(\id_i\) under \(D(a)\text{,}\) so all elements are identified. For the last implication, the covariant element category of \(j\mapsto\Hom_I(i,u(j))\) is \((i\downarrow u)\text{.}\) The component description in Proposition 6.2 identifies its colimit with \(\pi_0(i\downarrow u)\text{.}\) A singleton component set is exactly nonempty connectedness.
    The comparison for a composite is the composite of the two comparisons. The equivalence just proved therefore gives composition and the stated cancellation property. Apply the comparison to the constant singleton diagram to obtain the assertion about components.
    If \(L:I\to J\) is left adjoint to \(u:J\to I\text{,}\) its unit \(i\to uL(i)\) is an initial object of \((i\downarrow u)\) by the universal-arrow criterion for an adjunction. Thus each comma category is connected and nonempty, and \(u\) is final. If \(u\) is a left adjoint, its counit instead provides a terminal object of \((u\downarrow i)\text{,}\) proving initiality.
    The set-valued characterization is part of [KS06, Proposition 2.5.2]. Its use of the diagrams \(\Hom_I(i,-)\) explains why an objectwise reachability condition is insufficient: their colimits count components of entire comma categories. Checking that they are nonempty would detect reachability, but checking that they are singletons also imposes the necessary compatibility.
    The adjoint direction deserves attention. In the sentence \(L\dashv u\text{,}\) it is the right adjoint \(u\) that is final and may reindex colimits. This concerns changing the indexing category. It is a different assertion from a left adjoint between ambient categories preserving colimits. An ambient functor is applied to the objects and arrows of a diagram; an indexing functor is precomposed with that diagram to select and compare its stages. The comma categories make the distinction explicit.
    A comparison being invertible for one particular diagram does not imply finality. For example, selecting an object of a connected category gives a bijective comparison for the constant singleton diagram, but that selection functor is final only when the object is terminal. The characterization requires all set-valued diagrams, or the full indicated family of Hom tests. Dually, the initial condition is the uniform reindexing criterion for limits.

    Section 6.5 Filtered categories and finite cocones

    Connectedness joins stages by zigzags. Filteredness allows finite collections of stages and relations to be moved forward to one common stage. Its equalization clause is indispensable when different arrows can have the same endpoints.

    Definition 6.7 Filtered, cofiltered, and directed.

    A category \(I\) is filtered if it satisfies all three conditions:
    1. It has at least one object.
    2. For any \(i,j\in I\text{,}\) there are an object \(k\) and arrows \(i\to k\text{,}\) \(j\to k\text{.}\)
    3. For any parallel arrows \(a,b:i\to j\text{,}\) there is an arrow \(c:j\to k\) with \(ca=cb\text{.}\)
    It is cofiltered if \(I^\op\) is filtered. A filtered diagram in these notes is a diagram whose indexing category is filtered. A directed poset is a nonempty poset in which every pair of elements has a common upper bound; regarded as a category, it is filtered.
    In a poset the parallel-arrow clause is automatic, so directedness reduces to the common-upper-bound condition together with nonemptiness. The same is true for a preorder. We reserve directed poset for the antisymmetric case. The terms inductive system and inverse system refer to diagrams on an ordered category and its opposite, respectively; the actual arrow directions remain part of the data.2
    Nonemptiness is explicit in [LiI, Definition 2.7.6]. The displayed list in [LiII, Definition 1.6.6] gives the two amalgamation conditions and refers back to that definition. We retain nonemptiness explicitly: without it, the empty category would satisfy the two remaining clauses vacuously. Kashiwara–Schapira use the word filtrant for filtered and cofiltrant for cofiltered.

    Proposition 6.8.

    A category is filtered if and only if every diagram in it indexed by a finite category admits a cocone. Here finite means finitely many arrows, and includes the empty category.

    Proof.
    Suppose \(I\) is filtered and \(D:K\to I\) has finite index. If \(K\) is empty, any object of \(I\) is a cocone vertex. Otherwise, repeated use of common targets gives an object \(q\) and arrows \(s_k:D(k)\to q\) for every object of \(K\text{.}\) These arrows need not yet form a cocone. For each arrow \(a:k\to l\) of \(K\text{,}\) the pair
    \begin{equation*} s_k,\ s_lD(a):D(k)\rightrightarrows q \end{equation*}
    can be equalized by postcomposition. Process the finitely many arrows one at a time, postcomposing every \(s_k\) at each step. Equalities already obtained remain true after further postcomposition. At the end all cocone equations hold.
    Conversely, a cocone on the empty diagram supplies an object. A cocone on two discrete objects supplies their common target. A cocone on a parallel pair \(i\rightrightarrows j\) has a leg from \(j\) that equalizes the pair. These three finite shapes give exactly the defining conditions.
    This is the formulation used in [Rie, Definition 3.8.7]. It shows why filteredness is suited to finite data: finitely many objects can be brought together, and finitely many equations can then be enforced without losing earlier ones.
    The cocone in this criterion need not be universal. Its vertex need not be unique, and there need not be a preferred way to choose it. Only existence is required, so the criterion does not assert that the category has finite colimits. For example, a directed poset may provide several upper bounds for two objects without providing a least upper bound. Both suffice as common stages, while their categorical coproduct would require a least one. This distinction separates the indexing property from a completeness property of the category.

    Example 6.9 Common examples and a non-poset example.

    The ordered category \(\N\text{,}\) every nonempty ordinal category, and the poset of finite subsets of a set in \(\U\) are filtered. A category with a terminal object is filtered, since its terminal arrows provide both common targets and equalizations. The parallel-pair category \(0\rightrightarrows1\) is connected and has common targets, but is not filtered: the two distinct arrows have no later equalization.
    Let \(M=\{1,0\}\) be the monoid with identity \(1\) and absorbing element \(0\text{.}\) Its one-object category is filtered, because postcomposition by \(0\) equalizes every pair. It is not equivalent to any poset category: a fully faithful equivalence would identify its two-element endomorphism set with a singleton endomorphism set.

    Section 6.6 Equality at a common stage

    For a general set-valued diagram, its colimit relation is generated by identifications and may require a zigzag to witness equality. Filteredness replaces that generated relation by one explicit common-stage condition. This is the reason element calculations with filtered colimits are manageable.

    Theorem 6.10.

    Let \(I\) be small and filtered, and \(F:I\to\Set_\U\text{.}\) For \(x\in F(i)\) and \(y\in F(j)\text{,}\) set \(x\sim y\) if there exist arrows \(a:i\to k\text{,}\) \(b:j\to k\) with \(F(a)(x)=F(b)(y)\text{.}\) Then this is an equivalence relation on the tagged disjoint union, and
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{colim}_IF\cong \left(\coprod_{i\in\Ob(I)}F(i)\right)\big/\sim. \end{equation*}
    Write \([i,x]\text{,}\) or \([x]\) when unambiguous, for the corresponding class. For two elements at the same stage, the sharper criterion is
    \begin{equation*} [i,x]=[i,y]\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{there exists }c:i\to k\text{ with }F(c)(x)=F(c)(y). \end{equation*}

    Proof.
    Identities prove reflexivity, and symmetry follows by exchanging the two arrows. For transitivity, suppose \(x\in F(i)\text{,}\) \(y\in F(j)\text{,}\) and \(z\in F(l)\text{,}\) with witnesses
    \begin{equation*} a:i\to p,\quad b:j\to p,\quad c:j\to q,\quad d:l\to q, \end{equation*}
    so that \(F(a)(x)=F(b)(y)\) and \(F(c)(y)=F(d)(z)\text{.}\) Choose common-target arrows \(r:p\to t\) and \(s:q\to t\text{.}\) The composites \(rb,sc:j\to t\) may be different. Choose \(e:t\to v\) equalizing them. Then
    \begin{equation*} F(era)(x)=F(erb)(y)=F(esc)(y)=F(esd)(z). \end{equation*}
    The arrows \(era:i\to v\) and \(esd:l\to v\) witness \(x\sim z\text{.}\)
    Every generating colimit identification \(x\sim F(a)(x)\) satisfies this relation, using \(a\) and the identity of its target. Conversely, a common-stage equality relates both elements to the same image through generating identifications. Thus the equivalence relation is precisely the colimit relation from the set construction in Theorem 4.9. The quotient belongs to \(\U\) because the index and all values are small.
    For the sharper assertion, the common-stage criterion initially gives possibly different arrows \(a,b:i\to k\) with \(F(a)(x)=F(b)(y)\text{.}\) Equalize \(a,b\) by \(e:k\to l\) and put \(c=ea=eb\text{.}\) Applying \(F(e)\) gives \(F(c)(x)=F(c)(y)\text{.}\) The converse uses \(c\) as both common-stage arrows.
    The transitivity proof is given explicitly after [LiI, Definition 2.7.6]; see also [LiII, Proposition 1.6.11]. The equalization step cannot be replaced by merely choosing a common target. Two routes out of the middle stage act on the same middle element, and common targets alone do not make those routes agree.
    Likewise, when two representatives lie at the same stage, the phrase eventually equal should mean equality after one common transition arrow, as in the sharper criterion. The first form of the relation permits two distinct arrows. Moving between these formulations uses the equalization axiom, rather than a tacit assumption that the index is a poset.
    The canonical maps \(F(i)\to\operatorname{colim}F\) need not be injective. Their failure of injectivity is exactly the occurrence of distinct elements made equal by a transition arrow. On the other hand, if every transition map is injective, each canonical map is injective by the sharper criterion. This includes increasing unions of subsets, but no inclusions or injectivity assumptions are needed for the theorem itself.
    There is likewise no preferred common stage attached to an equality. A witness can always be transported farther along any available arrow, and several unrelated witnesses may exist. The theorem asserts existence of a finite witness of a specific form. It does not equip the colimit with a stage function or make its representatives canonical. Calculations should therefore be phrased in terms of choosing a witness and proving that the eventual conclusion is independent of that choice.

    Section 6.7 Moving finite data to one stage

    Many uses of filtered colimits rely on more than testing one equality. We need to represent several elements at once and enforce several equations at once. The following formulation separates those two operations and applies even when the elements belong to different diagrams.

    Lemma 6.11.

    Let \(I\) be small and filtered.
    1. Given finitely many classes in colimits of set-valued diagrams on \(I\text{,}\) there is one stage at which all these classes have representatives.
    2. Let \(F_1,\ldots,F_r:I\to\Set_\U\text{,}\) and suppose \(x_s,y_s\in F_s(i)\) have equal colimit classes for each \(s\text{.}\) There is one arrow \(a:i\to j\) such that \(F_s(a)(x_s)=F_s(a)(y_s)\) for every \(s\text{.}\)
    Both assertions include empty finite collections.

    Proof.
    For the first assertion, choose a representative for each class and then a common target for the finitely many chosen stages. Transporting the representatives gives the result. For no classes, choose any stage, using nonemptiness.
    For the second assertion, use the same-stage criterion to make the first pair equal after an arrow out of \(i\text{.}\) Transport all remaining pairs by that arrow. Their classes are still equal, so apply the criterion at the new stage to the next pair. Continue finitely many times. Previously established equalities are preserved by subsequent transition maps, and the composite arrow works for every pair. For no equations, take \(\id_i\text{.}\)
    The finiteness is used twice: in finding one upper stage for representatives and in terminating the process of enforcing equations. Nothing in the definition supplies an upper bound for infinitely many stages, or one transition that enforces infinitely many separate equalities. This distinction will account for the countable-product counterexample below.

    Proposition 6.12.

    A componentwise injective natural transformation \(\alpha:F\Rightarrow G\) of set-valued diagrams on a small filtered category induces an injective map on colimits. If a directed poset indexes subsets \(A_i\) of one set, with \(A_i\subseteq A_j\) for \(i\le j\) and the inclusion transitions, their colimit is their union.

    Proof.
    If representatives \(x\in F(i)\text{,}\) \(y\in F(j)\) have equal images in \(\operatorname{colim}G\text{,}\) common-stage arrows \(a:i\to k\text{,}\) \(b:j\to k\) give \(\alpha_k(F(a)(x))=\alpha_k(F(b)(y))\text{.}\) Injectivity of \(\alpha_k\) gives equality in \(F(k)\text{,}\) hence equality of the original classes. For subsets, the map from the colimit to the union sends a representative to its underlying element. It is surjective by definition of the union; two representatives of the same element agree at any common upper stage, so it is injective.
    In a general filtered diagram of injections, the stages need not have been given as subsets of one original set. They can nevertheless be identified with their canonical images in the colimit. Under those identifications the colimit is a union of stage images. This is a conclusion about that diagram, not permission to replace arbitrary transition maps by inclusions at the outset.
    For example, the finite subsets of a set \(A\in\U\) form a directed poset under inclusion, and their inclusion diagram has \(A\) as colimit. Every element belongs to a finite subset, and every finite collection belongs to one common finite subset. The second statement is exactly the simultaneous-representative property. By contrast, an infinite collection need not belong to a finite subset; the colimit contains all of its elements without requiring them to originate together at one stage.

    Section 6.8 Finite limits commute with filtered colimits of sets

    A limit of sets is a compatible family of elements. For a finite indexing category, that family uses finitely many elements subject to finitely many equations. The preceding lemma puts the entire family, with all its equations, at one stage. This is the finite-data mechanism behind the commutation theorem.

    Theorem 6.13.

    Let \(I\) be small and filtered, \(K\) a finite category, and \(X:I\times K\to\Set_\U\) a functor. The canonical comparison is a bijection:
    \begin{equation*} \kappa:\operatorname{colim}_{i\in I}\lim_{k\in K}X(i,k) \longrightarrow \lim_{k\in K}\operatorname{colim}_{i\in I}X(i,k). \end{equation*}
    It is natural in \(X\text{.}\) In particular, the functor \(\operatorname{colim}_I:\Fun(I,\Set_\U)\to\Set_\U\) preserves finite limits, including the terminal object, finite products, equalizers, and pullbacks.

    Proof.
    For each \(i\text{,}\) the maps \(X(i,k)\to\operatorname{colim}_{i'}X(i',k)\) give a map from the limit over \(k\) to the limit on the right. These maps are compatible in \(i\text{,}\) so they induce \(\kappa\text{.}\) This construction uses only the universal properties and proves its naturality.
    First suppose \(K\) is nonempty. An element of the right-hand limit is a family \((z_k)_k\) of colimit classes, compatible under the arrows of \(K\text{.}\) Since there are finitely many objects, Lemma 6.11 supplies one stage \(i\) and representatives \(x_k\in X(i,k)\text{.}\) For every arrow \(b:k\to l\text{,}\) compatibility says
    \begin{equation*} [X(i,b)(x_k)]=[x_l] \quad\text{in }\operatorname{colim}_{i'}X(i',l). \end{equation*}
    There are finitely many such equations. Apply the second part of the lemma to the diagrams \(X(-,l)\text{,}\) allowing repeated diagrams for different equations. We obtain one arrow \(a:i\to j\) after which they all hold. The transported elements form an actual member of \(\lim_kX(j,k)\text{.}\) Its colimit class maps to \((z_k)_k\text{,}\) proving surjectivity.
    For injectivity, represent two elements of the left-hand colimit by compatible families at stages \(i\) and \(j\text{.}\) Move both to a common stage \(q\text{.}\) If their images under \(\kappa\) agree, each pair of coordinates has equal class in the corresponding colimit. There are only finitely many coordinates, so one arrow out of \(q\) makes all the pairs equal simultaneously. At its target the two compatible families are identical elements of the limit. The common-stage criterion, now applied to the diagram \(i\mapsto\lim_kX(i,k)\text{,}\) proves equality in the left-hand colimit.
    If \(K\) is empty, every limit over \(K\) is a singleton. The right-hand side is therefore a singleton, and the left-hand side is \(\operatorname{colim}_I\Delta1\text{.}\) A filtered category is nonempty and connected, so this is a singleton by Proposition 6.2. Thus \(\kappa\) is bijective in the empty case as well. Pointwise computation of limits in the functor category gives the final preservation statement.
    This proof makes explicit the finite-stage choices in [LiII, Proposition 1.6.12] and [Rie, Theorem 3.8.9]. The corresponding Stacks result is [Stacks, Tag 002W]. The bijection is canonical even though representatives are chosen in its proof: \(\kappa\) was defined before any such choices, and the argument proves that this fixed map is bijective.
    For a product, the theorem says that finitely many colimit classes can be represented simultaneously. For an equalizer, it says that an equality of colimit classes can be enforced at a stage. A pullback combines both operations. The terminal-object case detects the nonempty connectedness of the index. These are distinct aspects of one theorem, rather than separate assumptions on the transition maps; in particular, those maps need not be injective.
    The assertion here is about sets. To transfer it to another category through an underlying-set functor, one must know that the relevant limits and filtered colimits are computed by that functor. Filteredness of the index alone is not a substitute for those additional facts about the ambient category.

    Section 6.9 Characterization and the finite boundary

    The commutation property also detects filteredness, so the three defining clauses have not merely been sufficient conveniences for the proof.

    Proposition 6.14.

    A small category \(I\) is filtered if and only if \(\operatorname{colim}_I:\Fun(I,\Set_\U)\to\Set_\U\) preserves finite limits.

    Proof.
    Only the converse remains. Preservation of the terminal object gives \(\operatorname{colim}_I\Delta1\cong1\text{,}\) so \(I\) is nonempty. For \(i,j\in I\text{,}\) consider \(A=\Hom_I(i,-)\) and \(B=\Hom_I(j,-)\text{.}\) Each has singleton colimit, as in Proposition 6.6. Product preservation implies that \(\operatorname{colim}_I(A\times B)\) is a singleton and in particular nonempty. Some value \(A(k)\times B(k)\) is therefore nonempty. Its element supplies arrows from both \(i\) and \(j\) to \(k\text{.}\)
    For parallel arrows \(a,b:i\to j\text{,}\) precomposition gives transformations \(\Hom_I(j,-)\rightrightarrows\Hom_I(i,-)\text{.}\) Their equalizer has value
    \begin{equation*} E(k)=\{c:j\to k:ca=cb\}. \end{equation*}
    Both original colimits are singletons, so equalizer preservation makes \(\operatorname{colim}_I E\) a singleton. Consequently some \(E(k)\) is nonempty, giving the required equalization. These are all three filteredness clauses.
    The characterization is [KS06, Theorem 3.1.6]. The proof exhibits tests for each part of filteredness: the terminal diagram detects nonemptiness, a product of outgoing Hom functors detects common targets, and an equalizer of transformations between them detects equalization of parallel arrows.
    Notice that the converse did not use a common-stage formula before filteredness was known. It used only the general quotient construction: a colimit of sets is nonempty precisely when at least one of its values is nonempty. This avoids a circular argument. Once the three clauses have been recovered, the common-stage theorem becomes available and supplies the stronger elementwise description.

    Example 6.15 A countable product that does not commute.

    Let \(I=\N\) with its usual order, and let \(K\) be the discrete category of positive integers. Put
    \begin{equation*} X(n,m)= \begin{cases}\{0,1\},&m\le n,\\ \{0\},&m>n,\end{cases} \end{equation*}
    with inclusions as \(n\) increases. For each fixed \(m\text{,}\) its colimit is \(\{0,1\}\text{.}\) Thus the product of the colimits is the set of all infinite binary sequences.
    At stage \(n\text{,}\) the product \(\prod_{m\ge1}X(n,m)\) consists of sequences that are zero after their first \(n\) entries. These products form an increasing sequence of subsets. Their colimit is the set of binary sequences with finite support. The canonical comparison is the inclusion of this set into all binary sequences and is not surjective; the sequence constantly equal to one is missing.
    Every individual coordinate can be represented at a stage, but no one stage supplies all coordinates of this missing sequence. All the sets and the countable product still belong to \(\U\text{;}\) the obstruction is the absence of a uniform finite stage, rather than a size failure. This gives a concrete boundary to the finite-data argument.

    Section 6.10 Reindexing filtered categories by directed posets

    A filtered category need not be equivalent to a poset, as the absorbing-monoid example showed. Nevertheless a directed poset can always supply enough stages and comparisons to compute its colimits. The appropriate map is final, which is a weaker and more useful requirement than an equivalence for this purpose.

    Theorem 6.16.

    For every small filtered category \(I\text{,}\) there exist a small directed poset \(P\) and a final functor \(p:P\to I\text{.}\) Consequently, for every diagram \(D:I\to\mathcal C\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{colim}_PDp\cong\operatorname{colim}_ID \end{equation*}
    if either side exists. For \(E:I^\op\to\mathcal C\text{,}\) there is similarly a canonical isomorphism
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{I^\op}E\cong\lim_{P^\op}Ep^\op \end{equation*}
    if either side exists.

    Proof.
    For the existence of \(P\) and \(p\text{,}\) we use the construction and proof of [Stacks, Tag 0032, Lemma 4.21.5]. Its construction produces a partially ordered directed set, not only a preorder. Applied to a \(\U\)-small category, it stays within \(\U\text{:}\) the construction uses finite generating data from \(I\times\N\text{,}\) subcategories of this small category, and their inclusion order. The assumption \(\N\in\U\) and the closure properties of the universe provide the indicated smallness. The cofinality theorem gives both displayed comparisons, the second by applying it in the opposite ambient category.
    The replacement is a theorem about indexing. It does not remove the multiple arrows of \(I\text{,}\) and the functor \(p\) need not be full, faithful, or injective on objects. Different elements of \(P\) may map to the same object while their transition arrows encode different amounts of comparison data. This is why replacing a category by the poset of its objects, ordered merely by existence of an arrow, does not prove the theorem: that relation discards the parallel-arrow information that must eventually be equalized.

    Proposition 6.17.

    Let \(I\) be filtered and \(J\subseteq I\) a full subcategory. Its inclusion is final if and only if every \(i\in I\) admits an arrow to an object of \(J\text{.}\) When this holds, \(J\) is filtered.

    Proof.
    The condition is necessary by nonemptiness of each comma category. Conversely, it gives an object of \(J\text{,}\) and common targets in \(I\) can be followed by an arrow back into \(J\text{.}\) Fullness then gives common targets for pairs of objects of \(J\text{.}\) Likewise, an equalization in \(I\) followed by an arrow into \(J\) gives the required equalization in \(J\text{,}\) so \(J\) is filtered.
    For two objects \((j,a)\text{,}\) \((j',a')\) of \((i\downarrow J)\text{,}\) first choose arrows from \(j,j'\) to a common target in \(I\text{.}\) Equalize the resulting two composites out of \(i\text{,}\) and follow by an arrow into an object \(k\in J\text{.}\) Fullness makes the two resulting arrows from \(j,j'\) arrows of \(J\text{.}\) They give a common target for the two comma objects. Thus that comma category is nonempty and connected, proving finality.
    This criterion is [LiII, Proposition 1.6.8]. Fullness and filteredness are the reasons that reachability alone suffices here, whereas it failed for a general functor.
    There is an explicit replacement for the one-object category of \(\{1,0\}\text{.}\) Send every \(n\in\N\) to its sole object, every strict transition \(n<m\) to \(0\text{,}\) and every identity to \(1\text{.}\) This is a functor. In its comma category, any two objects map to one at a strictly larger stage labeled by \(0\text{,}\) so it is final. A diagram of sets on the monoid category is a set with an idempotent self-map; this reindexing becomes a sequence repeatedly applying that map. Its colimit is the image of the idempotent. The replacement retains that information through transition arrows, even though all its stages have the same image object.
    Finally, the canonical comparisons are independent of replacement choices in the appropriate universal sense. If two directed replacements are used, both resulting colimit cocones extend uniquely to the original diagram. Each is therefore a colimit of that diagram, and there is a unique isomorphism compatible with those extended cocones. The comparison is natural in the diagram by the same uniqueness. This does not identify the two directed posets or their stagewise diagrams; it identifies the universal objects they compute.

    Section 6.11 Sifted categories and the diagonal criterion

    Filteredness controls finite equations as well as finite collections of representatives. If only finite products are to commute with a colimit, the appropriate condition is weaker. Indexing categories remain \(\U\)-small, and the commutation assertion concerns \(\Set_\U\text{.}\)

    Definition 6.18 Sifted category.

    A small category \(I\) is sifted if \(\operatorname{colim}_I:\Fun(I,\Set_\U)\to\Set_\U\) preserves finite products. Explicitly, for every finite family \((F_s:I\to\Set_\U)_{s\in S}\text{,}\) the canonical map
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{colim}_{i\in I}\prod_{s\in S}F_s(i)\longrightarrow\prod_{s\in S}\operatorname{colim}_{i\in I}F_s(i) \end{equation*}
    is bijective. The empty family is included. A sifted colimit in any category means a colimit indexed by a small sifted category.
    It suffices to test binary products and the terminal object, then iterate. The empty-product condition says \(\operatorname{colim}_I\Delta1\cong1\text{,}\) equivalently that \(I\) is nonempty and connected. Thus nonemptiness is part of the definition, rather than an optional convention.

    Theorem 6.19.

    A small category \(I\) is sifted if and only if it is nonempty and its diagonal \(\delta:I\to I\times I\) is final. Equivalently, \(I\) is nonempty and, for every \(a,b\in I\text{,}\) the category of cospans
    \begin{equation*} a\xrightarrow{u}k\xleftarrow{v}b \end{equation*}
    is nonempty and connected. Its morphisms are arrows \(h:k\to k'\) satisfying \(hu=u'\) and \(hv=v'\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Suppose first that \(I\) is sifted. The empty-product test makes it nonempty. Each covariant representable \(\Hom_I(a,-)\) has singleton colimit: every arrow from \(a\) is the image of \(\id_a\text{.}\) Binary-product preservation therefore gives
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{colim}_{k\in I}\bigl(\Hom_I(a,k)\times\Hom_I(b,k)\bigr)\cong1. \end{equation*}
    The covariant element category of this product functor is precisely the displayed cospan category \(((a,b)\downarrow\delta)\text{.}\) The component description of set colimits makes that category nonempty and connected, proving finality.
    Conversely, finality of \(\delta\) supplies a cospan between every two objects. Together with nonemptiness this makes \(I\) connected, so the terminal-object test holds. For \(F,G:I\to\Set_\U\text{,}\) apply cofinality to \(H(i,j)=F(i)\times G(j)\text{.}\) It identifies the diagonal colimit with \(\operatorname{colim}_{I\times I}H\text{.}\) The latter is canonically
    \begin{equation*} \bigl(\operatorname{colim}_I F\bigr)\times\bigl(\operatorname{colim}_I G\bigr). \end{equation*}
    Indeed, every pair of classes has representatives at independently chosen stages. Equality of the first representatives is generated by a zigzag of transition identifications while the second representative is held fixed; then vary the second coordinate while holding the first fixed. These are exactly the identifications in the product-indexed colimit. This proves bijectivity, including the case of an empty colimit on either side. The resulting composite is the canonical product comparison, so binary products are preserved.
    This is the criterion in [ARV10, Remark 2.1(i), June 2010 manuscript]. The explicit nonemptiness condition matters: the diagonal of the empty category is final vacuously, but that category is not sifted. The proof also shows why connectedness of each cospan category, rather than uniqueness of a common target, is the relevant condition.
    Connectedness alone is much weaker. Let a nontrivial small group \(G\) define a one-object category. A covariant functor from it to sets is a left action, and its colimit is the orbit set. The regular action of \(G\) on itself has one orbit, whereas the diagonal action on \(G\times G\) has orbit set in bijection with \(G\text{:}\) the invariant is \(g^{-1}h\text{,}\) and equal invariants give elements in the same orbit. Thus this connected category fails the binary-product test. The test concerns arbitrary diagrams, not merely constant diagrams with identity transition maps.

    Section 6.12 Filtered diagrams and reflexive coequalizers

    Every filtered category is sifted by Theorem 6.13. Every small category with finite coproducts is also sifted: it is nonempty, and the coproduct cospan \(a\to a\amalg b\leftarrow b\) is initial in its cospan category. A further example separates siftedness from filteredness.

    Definition 6.20 Reflexive pair and its indexing category.

    A parallel pair \(d_0,d_1:A\rightrightarrows B\) is reflexive if it has a common section \(s:B\to A\text{,}\) so \(d_0s=d_1s=\id_B\text{.}\) A reflexive coequalizer is a coequalizer of such a pair. Let \(\mathcal R\) be the category presented by these two objects, three generating arrows, and those two relations; no further relations are imposed.
    A diagram of shape \(\mathcal R\) specifies the pair together with its common section. Its colimit is exactly the coequalizer of the pair: a cocone is determined by its leg \(q:B\to Q\text{,}\) with \(qd_0=qd_1\text{;}\) the leg from \(A\) is then \(qd_0\text{,}\) and its compatibility with \(s\) follows from \(d_0s=\id_B\text{.}\) This argument works in any category and requires no preexisting coequalizers.

    Proposition 6.21.

    The category \(\mathcal R\) is sifted and is not filtered. Consequently reflexive coequalizers commute with finite products in sets.

    Proof.
    Take two reflexive pairs of sets \(d_0,d_1:A\rightrightarrows B\) and \(e_0,e_1:A'\rightrightarrows B'\text{,}\) with common sections \(s,t\text{.}\) Write their coequalizers as \(B/{\sim}\) and \(B'/{\approx}\text{.}\) Let \(\equiv\) be the equivalence relation generated on \(B\times B'\) by
    \begin{equation*} (d_0a,e_0a')\equiv(d_1a,e_1a'). \end{equation*}
    Each generating identification respects \(\sim\) and \(\approx\text{,}\) so \(\equiv\) is contained in the coordinatewise equivalence relation. Conversely, choosing \(a'=t(b')\) shows
    \begin{equation*} (d_0a,b')\equiv(d_1a,b'), \end{equation*}
    and choosing \(a=s(b)\) allows the second coordinate to change while the first stays fixed. Taking equivalence closures gives every coordinatewise equivalence. Hence
    \begin{equation*} (B\times B')/{\equiv}\cong(B/{\sim})\times(B'/{\approx}). \end{equation*}
    This is the canonical comparison for the two diagrams. The constant singleton diagram has singleton coequalizer, so the empty-product test also holds. Binary and empty products establish siftedness.
    To disprove filteredness, reduce words using \(d_i s=\id_B\text{.}\) The only arrows with source \(B\) are \(\id_B\) and \(s\text{.}\) Neither equalizes \(d_0,d_1\text{:}\) both \(d_0\ne d_1\) and \(sd_0\ne sd_1\text{.}\) These inequalities can be checked in a set-valued diagram with \(A=\{0,1,2\}\text{,}\) \(B=\{0,1\}\text{,}\) the inclusion as section, and \(d_0,d_1\) fixing \(0,1\) but sending \(2\) to \(0,1\text{,}\) respectively. Thus no arrow out of \(B\) equalizes the generating pair.
    The quotient argument is a direct verification of the example in [ARV10, Example 2.2, June 2010 manuscript]. The common sections supply stationary coordinates. They do not supply a later stage equalizing the two arrows, which is the extra demand made by filteredness.

    Section 6.13 What siftedness does not imply

    Siftedness does not guarantee commutation with equalizers. In \(\mathcal R\text{,}\) precomposition with \(d_0,d_1\) gives transformations
    \begin{equation*} \Hom_{\mathcal R}(B,-)\rightrightarrows\Hom_{\mathcal R}(A,-). \end{equation*}
    Their pointwise equalizer is empty everywhere, since no arrow out of \(B\) equalizes the pair. Its colimit is empty, whereas both representable colimits are singletons and their equalizer is a singleton. This directly witnesses the failure of finite-limit commutation.
    Nor are arbitrary coequalizer shapes sifted. For the category freely generated by a parallel pair, take a diagram \(\{*\}\rightrightarrows\{0,1\}\) sending the generator to \(0\) and \(1\text{.}\) Its coequalizer is a singleton. In the product of this diagram with itself, only \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\) are identified; the off-diagonal elements remain separate. Its coequalizer has three elements, so the product comparison is not bijective.

    Proposition 6.22.

    Let \(T\) be a small category with finite products. The full subcategory of \(\Fun(T,\Set_\U)\) consisting of finite-product-preserving functors is closed under sifted colimits computed in that functor category.

    Proof.
    For a sifted diagram of such functors \(M_i\text{,}\) form its pointwise colimit \(M\text{.}\) For every finite family \((t_s)\) in \(T\text{,}\) the maps supplied by its product projections give natural comparison bijections for each \(M_i\text{.}\) Taking their colimit and using siftedness gives
    \begin{equation*} M\left(\prod_s t_s\right)\cong\operatorname{colim}_i\prod_s M_i(t_s)\cong\prod_s M(t_s). \end{equation*}
    The composite is precisely the comparison induced by those projections. The empty family is included, so the terminal object is preserved too. Thus \(M\) belongs to the full subcategory. Its cocone is still universal there, since it is already universal against every vertex in the ambient functor category.
    This gives a useful scope for the set-theoretic commutation theorem. Whenever objects of interest are described by product-preserving set-valued functors on a specified small category, their sifted colimits can be formed pointwise. It uses no assertion about arbitrary equations, arbitrary limits, or the behavior of an unspecified underlying-set functor. The product comparisons and the terminal comparison are exactly the structure retained.
    Thus filtered colimits and reflexive coequalizers are two important families of sifted colimits, with different mechanisms. Reducing all sifted constructions to those two families requires further hypotheses on the ambient category.3 As with the filtered theorem, the defining product commutation takes place in sets. Using it in another ambient category requires an argument that its relevant products and colimits have the corresponding behavior.
