    Appendix B. Hypotheses and counterexamples

    The examples below locate specific hypotheses in results already established. Each entry identifies an inference that fails, recalls the obstruction, and points to the statement that can be used instead. The linked discussions contain the constructions and proofs; sizes and structural maps retain the conventions of their chapters.

    Section B.1 Natural data and information carried by objects

    Objectwise bijections do not give a representation.

    On the one-object category of a nontrivial finite group \(M\text{,}\) the representable presheaf and the constant presheaf with value \(M\) have the same value set. Nevertheless every natural transformation from the representable to that constant presheaf is constant, so none is an isomorphism. The action on arrows changes the answer. Representability requires a natural isomorphism, equivalently a universal element with its factorization property; see Example 3.12 and Proposition 3.5.

    Equivalence does not recover the original object set.

    The groupoid with two distinct objects and exactly one arrow between every pair is equivalent to the terminal category. It is not isomorphic to it: an isomorphism of categories would give a bijection on objects. Full faithfulness and essential surjectivity give an equivalence, allowing isomorphic copies to be replaced. A strict inverse requires the stronger data of a category isomorphism. See Example 1.21 and Section 1.8.

    Limits of algebras need not be free.

    The product of two free monoids on one generator is the monoid of pairs of nonnegative exponents. It is commutative but cannot be generated by one element, so it is not a free monoid. The creation theorem applies to limits in the category of all monad algebras; it does not assert closure of free algebras under those limits. The underlying limit receives a uniquely determined algebra structure. See Section 9.5 and Theorem 9.9.

    An invertible transformation can have a noninvertible mate.

    The left adjoint \([1]\to\mathbf1\) has the greatest-object functor as right adjoint. Relative to an identity adjunction on the other side, the identity comparison of the left adjoint has mate \(\id_{[1]}\Rightarrow\operatorname{const}_1\text{.}\) Its component at \(0\) is not invertible. Conjugation between fixed adjunctions does preserve invertibility; its identity connecting functors are an additional restriction. See Example 5.14 and Proposition 5.13.

    Section B.2 Size bounds in existence statements

    Local smallness does not control all component families.

    Take a discrete source with object set \(\U\) and constant functors to a two-element set. Natural transformations are unconstrained families of self-maps indexed by \(\U\text{;}\) their set is too large to belong to \(\U\text{.}\) Both original categories are locally small at that scale. A \(\U\)-small source does ensure that actual transformation sets belong to \(\U\text{;}\) controlling the entire functor collection requires further bounds. See Section 0.5 and Proposition 0.2.

    Preserving small limits does not supply solution sets.

    The inclusion \(\Set_\U\to\Set_\V\) preserves \(\U\)-small limits but has no left adjoint. At the set \(\U\text{,}\) an adjunction would equate a small function set with all functions \(\U\to2\text{,}\) contradicting their cardinalities. Freyd’s general theorem additionally requires a solution family indexed by a member of \(\U\text{.}\) Its codomain may be set-encoded without being locally \(\U\)-small, as in this example. See Example 5.25 and Theorem 5.24.

    Existence of intersections does not make them small limits.

    The reverse-inclusion poset of subsets of \(\U\) has all set-indexed intersections of subobjects. The functor to \(\{0\lt1\}\) recording whether a subset belongs to \(\U\) preserves all \(\U\)-small limits, yet has no left adjoint. A required largest small subset does not exist. The special adjoint theorem uses well-poweredness to bound its intersection; a larger-intersection variant requires preservation of the relevant limit cones. See Example 5.35 and Theorem 5.33.

    Section B.3 Diagram shape and preservation

    Finite completeness does not imply completeness.

    Finite sets have finite limits, but no product of countably many two-element sets. Maps from a singleton into a proposed finite product would have to parametrize all infinite binary sequences. Finite limits therefore control finite diagram shapes, not every small diagram. The appropriate existence theorem must cover the actual indexing category; preservation of limits by a functor also concerns limits which exist. See Section 4.6 and Definition 4.6.

    A universal Kan extension need not be pointwise.

    In the finite-poset example, the conditions at three target objects force one global extension, although the comma diagram at the middle object is empty and the value category has no initial object. Its unit is not invertible despite the indexing functor being fully faithful. Existence of the local comma colimits gives the pointwise construction and the fully faithful comparison theorem. Absoluteness additionally concerns preservation by every postcomposition functor. See Example 7.13, Proposition 7.9, and Section 7.7.

    A right adjoint need not preserve filtered colimits.

    For infinite \(S\in\U\text{,}\) the power functor \((-)^S\) on sets is a right adjoint. Applied to the filtered union of finite subsets of \(S\text{,}\) its comparison misses the identity of \(S\text{,}\) which factors through no finite subset. It does preserve \(\kappa\)-filtered colimits when \(\kappa\) is regular and \(|S|\lt\kappa\text{.}\) Accessibility has a specified rank; the right-adjoint preservation theorem guarantees limits. See Example 13.13 and Proposition 13.2.

    Finite-product commutation does not give finite-limit commutation.

    The reflexive-pair indexing category is sifted, so its set colimits commute with finite products. But no arrow out of its target equalizes its two generating arrows. The equalizer of the corresponding transformations between covariant representables is empty everywhere, while the equalizer after taking their singleton colimits is a singleton. Thus equalizers require the stronger filteredness condition in the general commutation theorem. See Proposition 6.21, Section 6.13, and Proposition 6.14.

    Existence of an underlying colimit does not lift its algebra structure.

    The filtered union of the powersets of finite initial segments is the set of finite subsets of \(\N\text{.}\) The colimit of the same free-algebra diagram for the powerset monad is instead the full powerset of \(\N\text{.}\) No complete join structure on the finite-subset union makes all its inclusion maps algebra maps. Preservation of a specified underlying colimit by both the monad and its square does give a unique algebra structure and its colimit property; see Example 9.15 and Theorem 9.10.

    Section B.4 Formal completions and internal finiteness

    Realized limits do not determine all pro morphisms.

    The pro system of finite binary prefixes realizes as the set of infinite binary sequences. Its pro maps to \(2\) depend on a finite prefix; the function detecting precisely the all-zero sequence does not. Realization is therefore not full. The same system has no limit among finite sets, although its formal pro limit exists. Use the pro Hom formula or its universal completion property, rather than identifying pro objects solely with realized sets. See Example 12.12 and Proposition 12.11.

    Finite presentability is weaker than small projectivity.

    For the empty base category, the presheaf category is terminal. Its unique object is finitely presentable, but its Hom functor fails to preserve the empty colimit and hence is not small projective. In a general presheaf category, finite presentables are finite colimits of representables, with retracts already included; small projectives are retracts of single representables. Within Ind, finite presentables are instead retracts of single representables, testing its internal filtered colimits. See Theorem 13.10, Theorem 11.10, and Proposition 12.6.

    A reflective presheaf embedding need not preserve coproducts.

    The restricted nerve of \(\mathsf{Fin}\to\Set_\U\) is fully faithful and has a left adjoint. At the two-element test object, the pointwise coproduct of two nerves of singletons has two elements, whereas the nerve of their coproduct has four. To compute a colimit in a locally presentable category through its presheaf realization, take the pointwise colimit and then apply the reflector. See Corollary 13.9 and the example following it.

    Section B.5 Quotients, multiplication, and duality

    Free objects require a specified class of lifting maps.

    The polynomial ring \(\Z[t]\) lifts maps through surjective unital ring homomorphisms, but cannot lift \(t\mapsto1/2\) through the categorical epimorphism \(\Z\to\Q\text{.}\) It is regular projective in commutative rings and is not projective against all epimorphisms. A free-forgetful adjunction transfers lifting against the arrows whose underlying maps lie in the specified base class. See Example 15.23 and Proposition 15.22.

    Regularity does not make every relation effective.

    Torsion-free groups form a regular category. On the infinite cyclic group, parity of exponents defines an internal equivalence relation, but its coequalizer within torsion-free groups is the trivial group. Its kernel pair identifies more pairs than parity does. Effectiveness requires the comparison to that kernel pair to be invertible; Barr exactness imposes this for every internal equivalence relation. Even the regular inclusion into groups fails to preserve this coequalizer. See Proposition 15.16 and Proposition 15.14.

    A monoidal product need not have internal Homs.

    Sets with coproduct and empty unit form a symmetric monoidal category. For nonempty \(A\text{,}\) tensoring by \(A\) sends the initial set to \(A\text{,}\) so that functor cannot have a right adjoint. The same underlying category is closed for cartesian product. Closedness requires the stated right adjoints for the chosen tensor and the specified side; associators, unitors, and symmetry do not provide them. See Example 17.3 and Section 17.8.

    Internal Homs do not imply dualizability.

    Cartesian sets are closed, but only singletons have duals. Coevaluation would select a pair, and either duality triangle then makes the identity of a factor a constant map, forcing that factor to be a singleton. A dual supplies a particular tensor formula for an internal Hom; closedness only asserts that a representing object exists. The two conditions therefore have different force even in sets. See Section 17.9 and Proposition 17.17.

    The hexagons do not force symmetry.

    In the strict example with objects \(\N\) and integer endomorphisms, the braiding \(\beta_{m,n}=mn\) satisfies both hexagons. Exchanging the two factors at \(m=n=1\) twice gives the endomorphism \(2\text{,}\) whereas the identity is \(0\text{.}\) Symmetry adds the double-exchange equation. Monoidal coherence governs reassociation and units; it does not identify arbitrary paths involving braidings. See Example 17.9, Definition 17.8, and Theorem 17.2.
