    Chapter 2 The BBF form and intersection theory

    On a surface, the intersection of two degree-two classes is a number. On a \(2n\)-fold it is a degree-four class, so the same construction no longer gives a pairing on \(H^2\text{.}\) For an IHS manifold, the top intersection polynomial is the \(n\)-th power of a quadratic form, up to a positive constant. This form relates the Hodge decomposition, the positive cone, and the intersection theory of line bundles.

    Section 2.1 The quadratic form

    Let \(X\) be IHS of dimension \(2n\text{.}\) The group \(H^2(X,\Z)\) is torsion-free by Proposition 1.17. We distinguish a quadratic function \(a\mapsto q_X(a)\) from its polarized bilinear form by writing \(q_X(a,b)\text{,}\) with the convention
    \begin{equation*} q_X(a)=q_X(a,a),\qquad q_X(a,b)=\frac{q_X(a+b)-q_X(a)-q_X(b)}2. \end{equation*}

    Theorem 2.1 (Beauville–Bogomolov–Fujiki).

    There is a nondegenerate symmetric integral bilinear form \(q_X\) on \(H^2(X,\Z)\text{,}\) primitive and positive on Kähler classes, and a constant \(c_X\in\Q_{>0}\) such that
    \begin{equation} \int_X a^{2n}=c_Xq_X(a)^n\qquad(a\in H^2(X,\C)).\tag{2.1} \end{equation}
    The form with these properties is unique. Both \(q_X\) and \(c_X\) are preserved by parallel transport in smooth families of IHS manifolds.
    See [Bea83, §8] and [Mar11, §1.1]. Primitivity means that the values \(q_X(a,b)\text{,}\) for integral \(a,b\text{,}\) generate \(\Z\text{,}\) or equivalently that the entries of a Gram matrix have greatest common divisor one.1

    Definition 2.2.

    For a nonzero integral class \(a\in H^2(X,\Z)\text{,}\) its divisibility is the positive generator
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{div}_X(a):= \gcd\{q_X(a,b):b\in H^2(X,\Z)\} \end{equation*}
    of the nonzero ideal \(q_X(a,H^2(X,\Z))\subseteq\Z\text{.}\) The class \(a\) is primitive if \(a=kb\) with \(b\in H^2(X,\Z)\) and an integer \(k\geq1\) forces \(k=1\text{.}\) A primitive class can have divisibility greater than one. Both its square and its divisibility are preserved by lattice isometries, hence by parallel transport.
    Two forms satisfying the Fujiki identity have proportional \(n\)-th powers as polynomials. Over \(\C\text{,}\) their quotient is therefore constant; positivity on a Kähler class makes the factor positive. Two proportional primitive integral Gram matrices have proportionality factor one, proving uniqueness. Evaluating on a rational class of nonzero square shows \(c_X\in\Q\text{.}\)
    In a smooth proper family, local differentiable trivializations identify the integral cohomology groups and cup products of nearby fibers. Continuing these identifications along a path is called parallel transport. The polynomial \(\int_X a^{2n}\) is preserved by these identifications. The uniqueness just explained determines the pairing up to its sign. Positivity on a continuously varying local Kähler class fixes that sign along a path, giving the deformation invariance in the theorem.
    For calculations with the Hodge decomposition, one may normalize \(\sigma\) by \(\int_X(\sigma\bar\sigma)^n=1\text{.}\) The following expression is a positive multiple of \(q_X\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} q_\sigma(a)={}&\frac n2\int_X a^2(\sigma\bar\sigma)^{n-1}\\ &+(1-n)\left(\int_Xa\sigma^{n-1}\bar\sigma^n\right)\left(\int_Xa\sigma^n\bar\sigma^{n-1}\right). \end{aligned}\tag{2.2} \end{equation}
    See [Huy99, §1.9] for this analytic description. The notation \(q_X\) retains the primitive integral normalization.

    Proposition 2.3.

    The decomposition \(H^2(X,\C)=\C\sigma\oplus H^{1,1}(X)\oplus\C\bar\sigma\) is orthogonal except for the pairing of \(\sigma\) with \(\bar\sigma\text{.}\) More precisely,
    \begin{equation*} q_X(\sigma)=0,\quad q_X(\sigma,\bar\sigma)>0,\quad q_X(\sigma,H^{1,1}(X))=q_X(\bar\sigma,H^{1,1}(X))=0. \end{equation*}
    In particular, \(H^{1,1}(X)\) is the orthogonal complement of \(\C\sigma\oplus\C\bar\sigma\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Write \(a=x\sigma+\beta+y\bar\sigma\) with \(\beta\in H^{1,1}(X)\text{.}\) In (2.2), type considerations eliminate the cross terms involving \(\beta\) and one of \(\sigma,\bar\sigma\text{.}\) The first term contributes \(nxy\text{,}\) and the product in the second term contributes \((1-n)xy\text{.}\) Consequently,
    \begin{equation*} q_\sigma(a)=xy+\frac n2\int_X\beta^2(\sigma\bar\sigma)^{n-1}. \end{equation*}
    It follows that \(q_\sigma(\sigma,\bar\sigma)=1/2\) and all the stated orthogonalities hold. Positive rescaling gives the assertions for \(q_X\text{.}\) Nondegeneracy identifies the indicated orthogonal complement.

    Section 2.2 Polarization and signature

    A pairing of \(2n\) indices is a partition of \(\{1,\ldots,2n\}\) into unordered two-element sets. There are \((2n-1)!!=(2n)!/(2^n n!)\) such pairings.

    Proposition 2.4.

    For \(a_1,\ldots,a_{2n}\in H^2(X,\C)\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation} \int_X a_1\cdots a_{2n}=\frac{c_X}{(2n-1)!!}\sum_{P}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in P}q_X(a_i,a_j),\tag{2.3} \end{equation}
    where \(P\) runs through all pairings. In particular, for \(\omega,a,b\in H^2(X,\C)\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \int_Xa\omega^{2n-1}&=c_Xq_X(a,\omega)q_X(\omega)^{n-1},\\ \int_Xab\omega^{2n-2}&=\frac{c_X}{2n-1}\left(q_X(a,b)q_X(\omega)^{n-1}+2(n-1)q_X(a,\omega)q_X(b,\omega)q_X(\omega)^{n-2}\right). \end{aligned}\tag{2.4} \end{equation}
    When \(n=1\text{,}\) the second identity means simply \(\int_Xab=c_Xq_X(a,b)\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    The two sides of (2.3) are symmetric multilinear functions of \(2n\) variables. On the diagonal, the sum has \((2n-1)!!\) identical terms \(q_X(a)^n\text{,}\) so the functions agree by (2.1). A symmetric multilinear function over a characteristic-zero field is determined by its diagonal polynomial: its value on \(a_1,\ldots,a_{2n}\) is \(1/(2n)!\) times the coefficient of \(t_1\cdots t_{2n}\) in that polynomial evaluated at \(\sum t_i a_i\text{.}\)
    For the two remaining identities, differentiate \(\int_X(\omega+sa+tb)^{2n}=c_Xq_X(\omega+sa+tb)^n\text{.}\) One derivative in \(s\) gives the first identity after division by \(2n\text{.}\) A derivative in each of \(s,t\) gives the second after division by \(2n(2n-1)\text{.}\)

    Proposition 2.5.

    The signature of \(q_X\) on \(H^2(X,\R)\) is \((3,b_2(X)-3)\text{,}\) and its signature on \(H^{1,1}(X,\R)\) is \((1,b_2(X)-3)\text{.}\) For a Kähler class \(\omega\text{,}\) the subspace
    \begin{equation*} P_\omega^{1,1}:=\left\{a\in H^{1,1}(X,\R):\int_Xa\omega^{2n-1}=0\right\} \end{equation*}
    equals \(\omega^{\perp_{q_X}}\cap H^{1,1}(X,\R)\text{,}\) and \(q_X\) is negative definite on it.

    Proof.
    The first derivative identity and \(q_X(\omega)>0\) show that the two definitions of \(P_\omega^{1,1}\) agree. On this subspace the second derivative identity reduces to
    \begin{equation*} \int_X a^2\omega^{2n-2}=\frac{c_X}{2n-1}q_X(a)q_X(\omega)^{n-1}. \end{equation*}
    The Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations make the left side negative for every nonzero real primitive \((1,1)\) class. The factor multiplying \(q_X(a)\) is positive. Since \(\omega\) is a positive vector, the signature on \(H^{1,1}(X,\R)=\R\omega\oplus P_\omega^{1,1}\) follows.
    Write \(\sigma=u+iv\) with \(u,v\) real. The identity \(q_X(\sigma)=0\) gives \(q_X(u)=q_X(v)\) and \(q_X(u,v)=0\text{;}\) positivity of \(q_X(\sigma,\bar\sigma)\) makes both squares positive. By Proposition 2.3, their plane is orthogonal to \(H^{1,1}(X,\R)\text{.}\) These are the other two positive directions.
    For \(a,b\in H^{1,1}(X,\R)\text{,}\) the BBF pairing is generally not a fixed multiple of \(\int_Xab\omega^{2n-2}\) on the whole space: the second term of (2.4) must be included. It disappears on the primitive subspace.

    Section 2.3 Line bundles and the positive cone

    Proposition 2.6.

    For an IHS manifold, the first Chern class gives an isomorphism
    \begin{equation*} c_1:\Pic(X)\xrightarrow{\sim}H^2(X,\Z)\cap H^{1,1}(X). \end{equation*}
    This group is free abelian, and \(\Pic^0(X)=0\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    The exponential sequence \(0\to\Z\to\cO_X\to\cO_X^*\to1\) gives an exact sequence
    \begin{equation*} H^1(X,\cO_X)\longrightarrow\Pic(X)\xrightarrow{c_1}H^2(X,\Z)\longrightarrow H^2(X,\cO_X). \end{equation*}
    Hodge theory and simple connectedness give \(H^1(X,\cO_X)=H^{0,1}(X)=0\text{.}\) The last map is projection to \(H^{0,2}(X)\text{.}\) An integral class is real, so its \((0,2)\) part vanishes if and only if both its \((0,2)\) and \((2,0)\) parts vanish. Thus the kernel is exactly the integral \((1,1)\) classes. Freeness follows from the torsion-freeness of \(H^2(X,\Z)\text{.}\)

    Definition 2.7.

    The positive cone \(\mathcal C_X\) is the connected component of \(\{a\in H^{1,1}(X,\R):q_X(a)>0\}\) containing a Kähler class. The Kähler cone \(\mathcal K_X\) consists of the classes of Kähler forms.
    The Kähler cone is convex: a positive linear combination of Kähler forms is Kähler. It is contained in one component of the positive-square locus, so \(\mathcal C_X\) is independent of the Kähler class chosen in its definition. There are exactly two components because the restricted form has signature \((1,b_2-3)\text{.}\)
    The Kähler cone is open in \(H^{1,1}(X,\R)\text{.}\) Choose closed real \((1,1)\) forms representing a basis of this space. Compactness ensures that adding a sufficiently small linear combination of these forms to a Kähler form preserves positivity. The sum is still closed, hence is Kähler.

    Lemma 2.8.

    Let \(\omega\in\mathcal C_X\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal C_X=\{a:q_X(a)>0,\ q_X(a,\omega)>0\}. \end{equation*}
    If \(0\ne a\in\overline{\mathcal C_X}\text{,}\) then \(q_X(a,\omega)>0\text{.}\) For \(a,b\in\mathcal C_X\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} q_X(a,b)\geq\sqrt{q_X(a)q_X(b)}, \end{equation*}
    with equality exactly when \(a,b\) are positive multiples of one another.

    Proof.
    Choose orthogonal real coordinates in which the form is \(t^2-\lVert x\rVert^2\) and the component containing \(\omega\) is \(t>\lVert x\rVert\text{.}\) The boundary has \(t\geq\lVert x\rVert\text{.}\) In particular, a nonzero vector in its closure has strictly positive pairing with every vector in the open component. For the last assertion, scale \(a\) to square one and decompose \(b=\lambda a+v\) with \(v\perp a\text{.}\) The complement of \(a\) is negative definite, so \(q_X(b)=\lambda^2+q_X(v)\leq\lambda^2\text{;}\) here \(\lambda=q_X(a,b)>0\text{.}\) Equality holds precisely when \(v=0\text{.}\)

    Theorem 2.9 (Huybrechts).

    An IHS manifold \(X\) is projective if and only if there is a line bundle \(L\) with \(q_X(c_1(L))>0\text{.}\)
    See [Huy03, Theorem 2] for the corrected proof. The forward implication follows by taking an ample line bundle. In the converse, the given \(L\) or its dual need not be ample: its class can lie on a wall or in a different chamber.
    For comparison, the Kodaira embedding theorem requires an integral Kähler class. Huybrechts’s theorem starts with an integral Hodge class of positive BBF square. Moving from that weaker condition to projectivity is the geometric content of the theorem. The primitive integral form has signature \((1,\rho(X)-1)\) on \(\NS(X)_\R\) when \(X\) is projective, because an ample class supplies the positive direction and its orthogonal complement is negative definite.
    A real \((1,1)\) class is nef if it belongs to \(\overline{\mathcal K_X}\text{.}\) Equivalently, for a Kähler class \(\omega\text{,}\) its sum with \(\varepsilon\omega\) is Kähler for every \(\varepsilon>0\text{.}\) A line bundle is nef if its first Chern class is nef.
    The Picard group of a nonprojective IHS manifold need not contain a positive vector, and its restricted pairing can be negative definite or degenerate. The ambient group \(H^2(X,\Z)\) is always a nondegenerate lattice. In particular, the term Picard lattice requires care when the restricted form is degenerate.

    Section 2.4 Generalized Fujiki constants

    Theorem 2.10.

    Let \(0\leq j\leq n\) and \(\eta\in H^{4j}(X,\Q)\text{.}\) Suppose its parallel transport has type \((2j,2j)\) on every sufficiently small deformation of \(X\text{.}\) Then there is a constant \(C_X(\eta)\in\Q\) such that
    \begin{equation} \int_X\eta a^{2n-2j}=C_X(\eta)q_X(a)^{n-j}\qquad(a\in H^2(X,\C)).\tag{2.5} \end{equation}
    In particular, \(C_X(1)=c_X\text{.}\) The same statement holds with real coefficients and a real constant.
    See [BS22, Theorem 2.1] and [Huy03, §1]; an LLV proof is given in Proposition 11.16. The hypothesis requires Hodge type on all small deformations, not just on \(X\text{.}\) Products of Chern classes of \(T_X\) satisfy it because they are restrictions of the Chern classes of the relative tangent bundle.
    If one replaces \(q_X\) by \(q'=tq_X\text{,}\) \(t>0\text{,}\) while keeping the intersection identity, then
    \begin{equation*} c'_X=t^{-n}c_X,\qquad C_{q'}(\eta)=t^{-(n-j)}C_X(\eta). \end{equation*}
    This accounts for different numerical constants in references using the analytic form, an integral form, or a normalization adapted to Riemann–Roch.
    2

    Definition 2.11.

    The inverse of the bilinear form \(q_X\) is a tensor in \(\Sym^2H^2(X,\Q)\text{.}\) Its image under cup product is the inverse quadratic class \(Q_X=q_X^\vee\in H^4(X,\Q)\text{.}\) In a basis \(e_1,\ldots,e_b\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} Q_X=\sum_{i,j}q^{ij}e_i e_j,\qquad(q^{ij})=(q_X(e_i,e_j))^{-1}. \end{equation*}
    The tensor definition makes this independent of the basis.
    The Hodge orthogonality in Proposition 2.3 shows that \(Q_X\) has type \((2,2)\text{.}\) The pairing and cup product are preserved by parallel transport, so \(Q_X\) remains of that type on every deformation. Under a rescaling \(q'=tq_X\text{,}\) its inverse class becomes \((q')^\vee=t^{-1}Q_X\text{.}\)

    Proposition 2.12.

    For a class \(\eta\in H^{4j}(X,\Q)\) satisfying Theorem 2.10, with \(j<n\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} C_X(\eta Q_X)=\frac{b_2(X)+2n-2j-2}{2n-2j-1}C_X(\eta). \end{equation*}
    Hence, for \(0\leq k\leq n\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} C_X(Q_X^k)=c_X\prod_{r=1}^k\frac{b_2(X)+2n-2r}{2n-2r+1}. \end{equation*}

    Proof.
    Let \(m=n-j\text{,}\) write \(a=\sum t_i e_i\text{,}\) and apply the differential operator \(\Delta_q=\sum q^{ij}\partial_{t_i}\partial_{t_j}\) to (2.5). On its left side the result is
    \begin{equation*} 2m(2m-1)\int_X\eta Q_Xa^{2m-2}. \end{equation*}
    Since \(\Delta_q q_X(a)=2b_2(X)\) and \(\sum q^{ij}(\partial_i q_X)(\partial_j q_X)=4q_X(a)\text{,}\) the right side becomes
    \begin{equation*} 2m\bigl(b_2(X)+2m-2\bigr)C_X(\eta)q_X(a)^{m-1}. \end{equation*}
    Comparison gives the first formula. Repeated application with \(\eta=1,Q_X,\ldots,Q_X^{k-1}\) gives the second. This proof is a coordinate form of contraction by the inverse pairing; compare [BS22, Proposition 2.4].

    Section 2.5 Riemann–Roch and isotropic classes

    The odd Chern classes vanish by Proposition 1.8. Consequently the Todd class has only components \(\td_{2j}(X)\in H^{4j}(X,\Q)\text{;}\) in particular \(\td_0=1\) and \(\td_2=c_2(X)/12\text{.}\)

    Proposition 2.13.

    There is a degree-\(n\) polynomial \(\operatorname{RR}_X(t)\in\Q[t]\) such that
    \begin{equation*} \chi(X,L)=\operatorname{RR}_X(q_X(c_1(L)))\qquad(L\in\Pic(X)). \end{equation*}
    Explicitly,
    \begin{equation} \operatorname{RR}_X(t)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{C_X(\td_{2n-2k}(X))}{(2k)!}t^k.\tag{2.6} \end{equation}
    Its leading coefficient is \(c_X/(2n)!\text{,}\) and its constant coefficient is \(n+1\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch gives \(\chi(X,L)=\int_Xe^{c_1(L)}\td(X)\text{.}\) The part of degree \(4n\) is
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{k=0}^n\frac1{(2k)!}\int_Xc_1(L)^{2k}\td_{2n-2k}(X). \end{equation*}
    Each Todd component is a polynomial in Chern classes and therefore satisfies the deformation hypothesis of Theorem 2.10. Applying that theorem gives (2.6). The leading coefficient is obtained from \(\td_0=1\text{,}\) and the constant term is \(\int_X\td_{2n}(X)=\chi(X,\cO_X)=n+1\text{,}\) as in Chapter 1.
    This is a polynomial identity for Euler characteristics. For an ample line bundle, Kodaira vanishing and \(K_X\cong\cO_X\) give \(h^0(X,L)=\chi(X,L)\text{.}\) Without a vanishing theorem, an Euler characteristic alone does not determine the number of sections.
    The polynomial \(\operatorname{RR}_X\) is constant on a deformation type. Indeed, parallel transport preserves \(q_X\text{,}\) cup product, integration, and the Chern classes of the tangent bundle. It therefore preserves each polynomial \(a\mapsto\int_X\operatorname{td}_{2j}(X)a^{2n-2j}\) occurring in its construction. The coefficients of \(\operatorname{RR}_X\) are determined by these polynomials.

    Proposition 2.14.

    Let \(L\) be a line bundle with nonzero class \(\lambda=c_1(L)\in\overline{\mathcal K_X}\) and \(q_X(\lambda)=0\text{.}\) For every Kähler class \(\omega\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \int_X\lambda^k\omega^{2n-k}=0\quad(n<k\leq2n),\qquad \int_X\lambda^n\omega^n=\frac{2^nc_X}{\binom{2n}{n}}q_X(\lambda,\omega)^n>0. \end{equation*}
    Thus its numerical dimension, defined by the largest \(k\) with \(\int_X\lambda^k\omega^{2n-k}>0\text{,}\) is \(n\text{.}\) Also \(\chi(X,L^{\otimes m})=n+1\) for every integer \(m\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Because \(\lambda\) is a nonzero vector in \(\overline{\mathcal C_X}\text{,}\) Lemma 2.8 gives \(q_X(\lambda,\omega)>0\text{.}\) The Fujiki identity becomes
    \begin{equation*} \int_X(\omega+t\lambda)^{2n}=c_X\bigl(q_X(\omega)+2tq_X(\lambda,\omega)\bigr)^n. \end{equation*}
    The right side has degree \(n\) in \(t\text{.}\) Comparing coefficients yields both intersection assertions. The Euler-characteristic assertion follows by evaluating (2.6) at \(q_X(m\lambda)=0\text{.}\)
    If a positive power of \(L\) is generated by global sections, its morphism has \(n\)-dimensional image. This condition on \(L\) is called semiampleness.
