    Chapter 11 Cohomology and the LLV algebra

    Hard Lefschetz associates a representation of \(\mathfrak{sl}_2\) to each Kähler class. Allowing all degree-two classes with the Lefschetz property gives a larger Lie algebra acting on the same total cohomology. For an IHS manifold this algebra is an orthogonal Lie algebra determined by the BBF form. It contains the degree and Hodge operators, so its representations organize both Betti and Hodge numbers.
    Let \(X\) be IHS of dimension \(2n\text{,}\) and let \(b=b_2(X)\text{.}\) We use the BBF normalization of Chapter 2. The principal reference is [GKLR22, §2].

    Section 11.1 Lefschetz operators and the LLV algebra

    For \(a\in H^2(X,\C)\text{,}\) let \(L_a\) be cup product by \(a\text{.}\) Let \(h\) be the degree operator on total cohomology, defined by
    \begin{equation*} L_a(u)=a\smile u,\qquad h|_{H^k(X,\C)}=(k-2n)\operatorname{id}. \end{equation*}
    Thus \([h,L_a]=2L_a\text{.}\) Throughout this chapter the bracket of endomorphisms is the commutator \([A,B]=A\circ B-B\circ A\text{.}\)

    Definition 11.1.

    A class \(a\in H^2(X,\C)\) is a Lefschetz class if
    \begin{equation*} L_a^j:H^{2n-j}(X,\C) \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }H^{2n+j}(X,\C) \qquad(0\leq j\leq2n). \end{equation*}
    For such a class, its lowering operator \(\Lambda_a\) is the unique degree-minus-two operator satisfying
    \begin{equation} [h,L_a]=2L_a,\qquad [h,\Lambda_a]=-2\Lambda_a,\qquad [L_a,\Lambda_a]=h.\tag{11.1} \end{equation}
    The existence and uniqueness of \(\Lambda_a\) are the linear-algebra form of the Lefschetz decomposition. More explicitly, let \(P_a^{2n-j}=\ker(L_a^{j+1}:H^{2n-j}\to H^{2n+j+2})\text{.}\) The decomposition expresses cohomology as the sum of strings \(u,L_au,\ldots,L_a^ju\) with \(u\in P_a^{2n-j}\text{.}\) On such a string, \(\Lambda_a(u)=0\) and
    \begin{equation*} \Lambda_a(L_a^r u)=r(j-r+1)L_a^{r-1}u \qquad(1\leq r\leq j). \end{equation*}
    The commutator \([L_a,\Lambda_a]\) acts on \(L_a^ru\) by \(r(j-r+1)-(r+1)(j-r)=2r-j\text{,}\) the eigenvalue of \(h\text{.}\) This verifies the sign convention in (11.1).

    Lemma 11.2.

    For a Lefschetz class \(a\) and \(0\leq k\leq2n\text{,}\) let \(P_a^k=\ker L_a^{2n-k+1}|_{H^k(X,\C)}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} H^k(X,\C)=P_a^k\oplus L_aH^{k-2}(X,\C), \qquad \dim P_a^k=b_k-b_{k-2}, \end{equation*}
    where groups of negative degree are zero.

    Proof.
    For \(k=0,1\) the assertion follows from degree bounds. For \(k\geq2\text{,}\) hard Lefschetz identifies \(L_a^{2n-k+2}:H^{k-2}\to H^{4n-k+2}\text{.}\) Given \(\alpha\in H^k\text{,}\) there is therefore a unique \(\beta\in H^{k-2}\) with \(L_a^{2n-k+2}\beta=L_a^{2n-k+1}\alpha\text{.}\) Then \(\alpha-L_a\beta\in P_a^k\text{.}\) If \(L_a\beta\) also belongs to \(P_a^k\text{,}\) the same isomorphism forces \(\beta=0\text{.}\) This proves the direct sum. The map \(L_a:H^{k-2}\to H^k\) is injective, since any vector it kills is also killed by the indicated isomorphism. Taking dimensions gives the formula.
    Iterating this decomposition below the middle degree and applying hard Lefschetz above it gives the strings defining \(\Lambda_a\text{.}\) These formulas recover the lowering operator from the graded ring and \(a\text{.}\)
    For a Kähler class \(\omega\text{,}\) this notion of primitive cohomology depends on \(\omega\text{.}\) For example,
    \begin{equation*} P_\omega^2=\{v\in H^2(X,\C):q_X(v,\omega)=0\}. \end{equation*}
    Indeed, its defining condition is \(\int_Xv\omega^{2n-1}=0\text{,}\) which is equivalent to BBF orthogonality by (2.4) and \(q_X(\omega)>0\text{.}\) This codimension-one subspace lies inside the subalgebra generated by \(H^2\text{.}\) Lefschetz-primitive classes are therefore not the same as classes outside that subalgebra.
    Every Kähler class is a Lefschetz class. More generally, the Lefschetz property defines a nonempty Zariski open subset of \(H^2(X,\C)\text{,}\) by nonvanishing of finitely many cup-product determinants; its classes need not have Hodge type \((1,1)\text{.}\)

    Definition 11.3.

    The Looijenga–Lunts–Verbitsky algebra is the complex Lie subalgebra
    \begin{equation*} \mathfrak g\subset\operatorname{End}_{\C}H^*(X,\C) \end{equation*}
    generated by \(L_a,h,\Lambda_a\) for all Lefschetz classes \(a\text{.}\) Its degree-zero subalgebra and its reduced LLV algebra are
    \begin{equation} \mathfrak g_0:=\{D\in\mathfrak g:[h,D]=0\}, \qquad \overline{\mathfrak g}:=[\mathfrak g_0,\mathfrak g_0].\tag{11.2} \end{equation}
    The second expression means the linear span of the commutators of pairs of elements of \(\mathfrak g_0\text{.}\)
    Elements of \(\mathfrak g_0\) preserve cohomological degree because they commute with \(h\text{.}\) Rational Lefschetz classes give a rational form \(\mathfrak g_{\Q}\text{,}\) with \(\mathfrak g=\mathfrak g_{\Q}\otimes_{\Q}\C\text{;}\) see [LL97] and [GKLR22, Definition 2.2 and §2.1.1].1
    A representation of a Lie algebra \(\mathfrak a\) on a vector space \(U\) means a linear map \(\rho:\mathfrak a\to\operatorname{End}(U)\) satisfying \(\rho([A,B])=\rho(A)\rho(B)-\rho(B)\rho(A)\text{.}\) The inclusion defining \(\mathfrak g\) is the LLV action on cohomology. Its Lie bracket is the commutator; closure under composition is not required.

    Definition 11.4.

    Let \(V=H^2(X,\C)\) and \(W=\C e_-\oplus V\oplus\C e_+\text{,}\) where \(e_-,e_+\) are basis vectors of an auxiliary two-dimensional space. Define a symmetric bilinear form \(Q\) on \(W\) by
    \begin{equation} Q|_V=q_X,\qquad Q(e_-,e_-)=Q(e_+,e_+)=0,\qquad Q(e_-,e_+)=1,\qquad Q(e_\pm,V)=0.\tag{11.3} \end{equation}
    Thus \(W\) is the orthogonal sum of \((V,q_X)\) and a hyperbolic plane.
    For a quadratic space \((U,Q)\text{,}\) the orthogonal Lie algebra is
    \begin{equation*} \mathfrak{so}(U,Q)= \{A\in\operatorname{End}(U):Q(Au,v)+Q(u,Av)=0 \text{ for all }u,v\in U\}. \end{equation*}
    The space \(W\) is the standard orthogonal representation; \(H^*(X,\C)\) is the cohomology representation.2

    Theorem 11.5 (Looijenga–Lunts–Verbitsky).

    The LLV algebra of \(X\) has only degrees \(-2,0,2\) for the adjoint action of \(h\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{gathered}\mathfrak g=\mathfrak g_{-2}\oplus\mathfrak g_0 \oplus\mathfrak g_2,\qquad \mathfrak g_j=\{A\in\mathfrak g:[h,A]=jA\}, \\ \mathfrak g_0=\C h\oplus\overline{\mathfrak g}.\end{gathered} \end{equation*}
    Restriction to \(V=H^2(X,\C)\) identifies \(\overline{\mathfrak g}\) with \(\mathfrak{so}(V,q_X)\text{.}\) There is an isomorphism
    \begin{equation*} \mathfrak g\cong\mathfrak{so}(W,Q) \cong\mathfrak{so}(b+2,\C) \end{equation*}
    under which \(\overline{\mathfrak g}\) annihilates \(e_-,e_+\text{,}\) and \(h\) acts on \(W\) with eigenvalues \(-2,0,2\) on \(\C e_-,V,\C e_+\text{,}\) respectively. The space \(\mathfrak g_2\) consists of all cup-product operators \(L_a\text{,}\) \(a\in V\text{.}\) The corresponding real forms are \(\mathfrak g_{\R}\cong\mathfrak{so}(4,b-2)\) and \(\overline{\mathfrak g}_{\R}\cong\mathfrak{so}(3,b-3)\text{.}\) See [GKLR22, Theorem 2.7 and equations (2.3)–(2.10)].
    Every \(D\in\overline{\mathfrak g}\) acts as a derivation of the cup-product algebra:
    \begin{equation} D(u\smile v)=D(u)\smile v+u\smile D(v).\tag{11.4} \end{equation}
    Here \(u,v\in H^*(X,\C)\text{;}\) see [GKLR22, equation (2.6)].
    The full LLV action includes operators that are not derivations: for \(a\ne0\text{,}\) \(L_a(1)=a\text{,}\) while every derivation kills the unit.
    Semisimplicity of \(\mathfrak g\) gives the LLV decomposition:
    \begin{equation} H^*(X,\C)\cong\bigoplus_{\mu}V_\mu\otimes M_\mu,\tag{11.5} \end{equation}
    Here \(V_\mu\) are pairwise nonisomorphic irreducible \(\mathfrak g\)-modules, and \(M_\mu\) are their multiplicity spaces, with trivial \(\mathfrak g\)-action. The summands are stable under the Lie algebra, but need not be ideals for cup product.

    Proposition 11.6.

    The cup-product maps \(H^i(X,\C)\otimes H^j(X,\C)\to H^{i+j}(X,\C)\) are equivariant for the reduced LLV algebra. The Poincaré pairings \(H^i(X,\C)\times H^{4n-i}(X,\C)\to\C\) satisfy
    \begin{equation*} \int_XD(u)\smile v+\int_Xu\smile D(v)=0 \qquad(D\in\overline{\mathfrak g}). \end{equation*}

    Proof.
    On the tensor product, \(D\) acts by \(D(u\otimes v)=D(u)\otimes v+u\otimes D(v)\text{.}\) Equivariance of cup product is therefore exactly the derivation identity (11.4). Elements of \(\mathfrak g_0\) act on the one-dimensional space \(H^{4n}(X,\C)\) by scalars. Their commutators act by zero there, so the derived algebra \(\overline{\mathfrak g}=[\mathfrak g_0,\mathfrak g_0]\) acts trivially on the top cohomology. Applying integration to \(D(u\smile v)=D(u)\smile v+u\smile D(v)\) proves the pairing identity.
    If the reduced LLV algebra annihilates two classes, it also annihilates their product. The irreducible components of a product are restricted by the tensor product of the two reduced representations. An equivariant projection to a chosen summand therefore gives an equivariant product map; determining that map requires further information.

    Section 11.2 The subalgebra generated by degree two

    Definition 11.7.

    The subalgebra \(\SH^*(X)\subset H^*(X,\Q)\) generated by \(H^2(X,\Q)\) is the image of the cup-product map from its symmetric algebra. We retain ordinary cohomological grading:
    \begin{equation*} \SH^{2k}(X):= \operatorname{im}\bigl(\operatorname{Sym}^kH^2(X,\Q) \longrightarrow H^{2k}(X,\Q)\bigr),\qquad \SH^{2k+1}(X)=0. \end{equation*}
    In particular, \(\SH^0(X)=\Q\cdot1\) and \(\SH^2(X)=H^2(X,\Q)\text{.}\)
    Write \(\SH^*(X,\C)=\SH^*(X)\otimes\C\text{.}\)3

    Lemma 11.8.

    If \(a\in H^2(X,\C)\) has \(q_X(a)=0\text{,}\) then \(a^{n+1}=0\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Trivialize the cohomology local systems over a small deformation space of \(X\text{.}\) For every nearby fibre, its symplectic form satisfies \(\sigma_t^{n+1}=0\text{,}\) since its Hodge type would be \((2n+2,0)\text{.}\) By local Torelli, Theorem 4.9, the period lines \([\sigma_t]\) fill a nonempty analytic open subset of the projective quadric \(q_X=0\text{.}\) The map \(a\mapsto a^{n+1}\in H^{2n+2}(X,\C)\) is a homogeneous polynomial map defined by the fixed cup product. Each of its coordinate polynomials therefore vanishes on the cone over that analytic open subset. The nondegenerate complex quadric is irreducible because \(b\geq3\text{;}\) such an analytic open subset is Zariski dense in it. The polynomial map consequently vanishes on the entire isotropic cone, proving the assertion.

    Theorem 11.9 (Verbitsky–Bogomolov).

    The subalgebra \(\SH^*(X,\C)\) is an irreducible \(\mathfrak g\)-module. In the orthogonal highest-weight coordinates specified below its highest weight is \((n,0,\ldots,0)\text{;}\) it occurs with multiplicity one in total cohomology. For \(0\leq k\leq n\text{,}\) the map \(\operatorname{Sym}^kH^2(X,\Q)\to H^{2k}(X,\Q)\) is injective. Over \(\C\text{,}\) the relations in the subalgebra are precisely the ideal generated by \(a^{n+1}\) for \(a\in H^2(X,\C)\) with \(q_X(a)=0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation} \SH^*(X,\C)\cong \operatorname{Sym}^*V/ \left\langle a^{n+1}:a\in V,\ q_X(a)=0\right\rangle.\tag{11.6} \end{equation}
    Each element of \(V\) has degree two. The irreducibility and injection statements are due to Verbitsky; the ideal presentation in this form is due to Bogomolov. See [Ver96c] and [GKLR22, Theorem 2.15, Remark 2.17, and Proposition 2.32].
    The presentation is stated over \(\C\) so that the generators range over the full complex isotropic quadric. Replacing that set by rational isotropic vectors without checking the rational quadratic space is an additional assertion. The rational injectivity statement follows from its complex version by extension of scalars.
    The subalgebra is stable under \(L_a\) by definition, under \(h\) by its grading, and under \(\overline{\mathfrak g}\) by the derivation property. For \(B\in\mathfrak g_{-2}\text{,}\) one has \(B(1)=0\) for degree reasons. Inductively,
    \begin{equation*} B(L_au)=L_aB(u)+[B,L_a]u. \end{equation*}
    The first term belongs to \(\SH^*\) by induction, and the second does because \([B,L_a]\in\mathfrak g_0=\C h\oplus\overline{\mathfrak g}\text{.}\) Thus \(\SH^*\) is a \(\mathfrak g\)-submodule. Irreducibility and the precise relations are the further content of Verbitsky’s theorem.

    Corollary 11.10.

    For \(0\leq k\leq2n\text{,}\) let \(r=\min(k,2n-k)\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation} \dim_\Q\SH^{2k}(X)=\binom{b+r-1}{r}.\tag{11.7} \end{equation}
    In particular, \(b_{2k}(X)\geq\binom{b+k-1}{k}\) for \(k\leq n\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    For \(k\leq n\text{,}\) injectivity identifies the degree-\(2k\) part with \(\operatorname{Sym}^kH^2(X)\text{,}\) whose dimension is \(\binom{b+k-1}{k}\text{.}\) Choose a Kähler class \(\omega\text{.}\) The subspace \(\SH^*(X,\C)\) is stable under the associated Lefschetz triple, so hard Lefschetz on each of its finite-dimensional \(\mathfrak{sl}_2\)-summands identifies its degree-\(2k\) and degree-\(4n-2k\) parts. This gives the formula above the middle degree as well. The inequality follows from inclusion in total cohomology.
    We call this irreducible submodule the Verbitsky component and denote it by \(V_{(n)}\text{.}\) Complete reducibility gives a decomposition
    \begin{equation*} H^*(X,\C)=V_{(n)}\oplus R. \end{equation*}
    The complement \(R\) collects the other irreducible types. The ring structure is specified by (11.6) on \(V_{(n)}\text{;}\) products involving \(R\) require further information. In particular, a product of two classes in \(R\) may have a nonzero component in \(V_{(n)}\text{.}\)

    Example 11.11.

    For a K3 surface, \(n=1\) and \(b=22\text{.}\) The dimension formula gives graded dimensions \(1,22,1\) in degrees zero, two, and four. These are all the Betti numbers, so \(\SH^*(S)=H^*(S,\Q)\text{.}\) Its LLV representation is the standard representation of \(\mathfrak{so}(24,\C)\text{.}\) In this case the auxiliary space \(W\) has the same dimension as total cohomology; in higher dimensions \(W\) is generally much smaller than \(H^*(X,\C)\text{.}\)

    Section 11.3 Hodge bidegrees and representation weights

    The Hodge decomposition supplies a second semisimple operator
    \begin{equation*} f|_{H^{p,q}(X)}=i(q-p)\operatorname{id}. \end{equation*}
    Complex conjugation exchanges \(p,q\) and sends \(i\) to \(-i\text{,}\) so this operator is defined over \(\R\text{.}\) The LLV theorem includes \(f\in\overline{\mathfrak g}_{\R}\text{;}\) see [GKLR22, Proposition 2.24]. The operators \(h\) and \(f\) commute. Thus the degree and Hodge decompositions are both eigenspace decompositions of operators in the same Lie algebra.
    Let \(N=b+2\) and \(\ell=\lfloor N/2\rfloor\text{.}\) Choose a Cartan subalgebra of \(\mathfrak{so}(W,Q)\) containing \(h\) and \(f\text{.}\) We use orthogonal weight coordinates \(\varepsilon_0,\ldots,\varepsilon_{\ell-1}\text{,}\) in which the standard representation has weights \(\pm\varepsilon_j\text{,}\) together with zero when \(N\) is odd. A weight is written \(\nu=\sum_j\nu_j\varepsilon_j\text{.}\) A weight space means the simultaneous eigenspace for the Cartan subalgebra with these eigenvalues. The coordinates here are not coefficients in the basis of fundamental weights.

    Theorem 11.12.

    The Cartan subalgebra, orthogonal coordinates, and positive chamber can be chosen so that the weight \(\nu=(\nu_0,\nu_1,\ldots)\) contributes to
    \begin{equation} (p,q)=(n+\nu_0+\nu_1,\ n+\nu_0-\nu_1).\tag{11.8} \end{equation}
    In particular, its cohomological degree is \(2n+2\nu_0\text{.}\) See [GKLR22, equations (2.28)–(2.31)].
    Indeed, in these coordinates \(h\) acts with eigenvalue \(2\nu_0\text{,}\) and \(f\) with eigenvalue \(-2i\nu_1\text{.}\) Solving \(p+q-2n=2\nu_0\) and \(i(q-p)=-2i\nu_1\) gives the displayed bidegrees. To recover a Hodge number, sum the dimensions of all weight spaces that give that bidegree, including multiplicities from \(M_\mu\) in (11.5).
    This prescription can be written as a substitution in a character. If \(F_\mu(x_0,\ldots,x_{\ell-1})=\sum_\nu \dim V_\mu(\nu)\prod_jx_j^{\nu_j}\) is the formal character and \(m_\mu=\dim M_\mu\text{,}\) then the Hodge polynomial with positive coefficients is
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{p,q}h^{p,q}(X)u^pv^q =(uv)^n\sum_\mu m_\mu F_\mu(uv,u/v,1,\ldots,1). \end{equation*}
    The exponents become integers for the representations occurring in cohomology, including the half-integral case, because they are precisely the Hodge indices in (11.8). See [GKLR22, Corollary 3.8] for this expression in terms of characters.

    Example 11.13.

    For a K3 surface the standard module \(W\) has dimension twenty-four. The weights \(-\varepsilon_0,\varepsilon_0\) contribute to \((0,0),(2,2)\text{,}\) while \(\varepsilon_1,-\varepsilon_1\) contribute to \((2,0),(0,2)\text{.}\) Its remaining twenty weights have \(\nu_0=\nu_1=0\) and contribute to \((1,1)\text{.}\) Thus the standard LLV representation recovers the K3 Hodge diamond, not just its total dimension.
    An irreducible representation of \(\mathfrak{so}(W,Q)\) has highest weight \(\mu=(\mu_0,\ldots,\mu_{\ell-1})\) with coordinates either all integral or all strict half-integers. When \(N\) is odd, dominance is \(\mu_0\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{\ell-1}\geq0\text{.}\) When \(N\) is even, it is \(\mu_0\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{\ell-2}\geq|\mu_{\ell-1}|\text{.}\) We quote the highest-weight classification for types \(B_\ell\) and \(D_\ell\) from finite-dimensional representation theory.
    Integral highest weights occur in even cohomology and strict half-integral highest weights in odd cohomology; see [GKLR22, Proposition 2.35]. Hodge symmetry also makes every odd Betti number even: when \(p+q\) is odd, \(p\ne q\text{,}\) so the terms \(h^{p,q}\) occur in conjugate pairs.

    Proposition 11.14.

    The Verbitsky component contains every Hodge component on the boundary of the Hodge diamond, meaning \(p=0,2n\) or \(q=0,2n\text{.}\) Every other highest weight in the LLV decomposition satisfies
    \begin{equation*} \mu_0+\mu_1\leq n-1. \end{equation*}

    Proof.
    By Proposition 1.17, the holomorphic forms are \(1,\sigma,\ldots,\sigma^n\text{,}\) so they all belong to \(\SH^*(X,\C)\text{.}\) Complex conjugation gives the assertion on \(p=0\text{.}\) Multiplication by the nowhere-zero volume form \(\sigma^n\) identifies \(H^q(X,\mathcal O_X)\) with \(H^q(X,K_X)=H^{2n,q}(X)\text{.}\) Thus the boundary with \(p=2n\) is represented by products of \(\sigma^n\) and antiholomorphic forms, also in \(\SH^*\text{.}\) Conjugation gives the remaining boundary.
    The highest-weight vector of a remaining summand has \(p=n+\mu_0+\mu_1\text{.}\) A Hodge component has \(p\leq2n\text{,}\) so \(\mu_0+\mu_1\leq n\text{.}\) Equality would put a nonzero vector in \(p=2n\text{,}\) where the Verbitsky component already supplies the full Hodge component. The direct-sum decomposition has no room for an additional vector there. Therefore \(\mu_0+\mu_1<n\text{.}\) The sum is an integer in both the integral and half-integral cases, proving the bound. This is [GKLR22, Proposition 2.34].
    The algebra \(\mathfrak g\cong\mathfrak{so}(b+2,\C)\) and \(V_{(n)}\) are determined by \(b,n\text{;}\) the remaining multiplicities are further cohomological data.

    Section 11.4 Deformation-invariant Hodge classes

    A class \(\alpha\in H^{2p}(X,\C)\) remains of type \((p,p)\) if its parallel transport has that type on every fibre of a sufficiently small full deformation of \(X\text{.}\) Parallel transport preserves the graded cup product and the degree operator \(h\text{.}\) It therefore identifies the Lefschetz classes and their uniquely determined lowering operators from Definition 11.1, and hence the LLV algebra and its reduced subalgebra.

    Proposition 11.15.

    A class \(\alpha\in H^{2p}(X,\C)\) remains of type \((p,p)\) on all small deformations if and only if \(D\alpha=0\) for every \(D\in\overline{\mathfrak g}\text{.}\) In particular, the reduced LLV algebra annihilates every Chern class \(c_i(T_X)\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    If the reduced algebra annihilates \(\alpha\text{,}\) the Hodge operator \(f_t\) of every nearby complex structure annihilates its parallel transport, since \(f_t\) belongs to that reduced algebra. On cohomological degree \(2p\text{,}\) the zero eigenspace of \(f_t\) is exactly type \((p,p)\text{.}\)
    Conversely, suppose \(\alpha\) remains of type \((p,p)\text{.}\) Let \(V_{\R}=H^2(X,\R)\text{.}\) By Proposition 4.6 and local Torelli, the periods of small deformations range over an open set of oriented positive two-planes in \(V_{\R}\text{.}\) For an oriented \(q_X\)-orthonormal basis \(u,v\) of such a plane, the period line is \(\C(u+iv)\text{.}\) The Hodge operator on \(V_{\R}\) is
    \begin{equation*} f_{u,v}(w)=2\bigl(q_X(u,w)v-q_X(v,w)u\bigr). \end{equation*}
    Indeed, it sends \(u+iv\) to \(-2i(u+iv)\text{,}\) its conjugate to \(2i(u-iv)\text{,}\) and annihilates the orthogonal complement, which is the real \((1,1)\) subspace. Since \(\alpha\) has type \((p,p)\text{,}\) every corresponding operator in \(\overline{\mathfrak g}_{\R}\) annihilates it.
    These operators span \(\mathfrak{so}(V_{\R},q_X)\text{.}\) To see this, identify that algebra with \(\bigwedge^2V_{\R}\) by
    \begin{equation*} u\wedge v\longmapsto\bigl(w\longmapsto q_X(u,w)v-q_X(v,w)u\bigr). \end{equation*}
    If a linear functional on \(\bigwedge^2V_{\R}\) vanished on the wedges from the indicated open set of positive planes, its value on \(u\wedge v\) would vanish for an open set of pairs \((u,v)\in V_{\R}^2\text{:}\) orthonormalizing a positively oriented basis only multiplies its wedge by a nonzero scalar. This value is a bilinear polynomial in \(u,v\text{;}\) vanishing on an open set makes it identically zero. Decomposable wedges span the exterior square, so the functional is zero. Restriction to \(V_{\R}\) identifies the reduced LLV algebra with this orthogonal algebra. Hence the Hodge operators span the reduced algebra itself, which therefore annihilates \(\alpha\text{.}\)
    In a smooth holomorphic deformation, the relative tangent bundle restricts to the tangent bundle of each fibre. Its Chern classes form flat sections of the cohomology local systems and have type \((i,i)\) on each fibre. They satisfy the stated condition.
    The proof applies for every \(b\geq3\text{,}\) including \(b=3,4\text{.}\) It uses the full local period domain. On a polarized deformation space, the polarization remains of type \((1,1)\) although the full reduced algebra does not annihilate it. The relation with generic Mumford–Tate algebras is discussed in [GKLR22, §2.3].

    Proposition 11.16.

    Let \(0\leq k\leq2n\text{,}\) and let \(\eta\in H^{2k}(X,\C)\) remain of type \((k,k)\) on all small deformations. If \(k\) is odd, then
    \begin{equation*} \int_X\eta a^{2n-k}=0\qquad(a\in H^2(X,\C)). \end{equation*}
    If \(k=2j\text{,}\) its intersection polynomial is a constant multiple of \(q_X^{n-j}\text{,}\) as in Theorem 2.10. The constant is rational for rational \(\eta\) and real for real \(\eta\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Let \(m=2n-k\) and \(P_\eta(a)=\int_X\eta a^m\text{.}\) If \(m=0\text{,}\) the polynomial is constant and the assertion follows. Assume \(m>0\text{.}\) By Proposition 11.15, every reduced LLV element annihilates \(\eta\text{.}\) It also acts trivially on \(H^{4n}(X,\C)\text{:}\) that space is one-dimensional, and a semisimple Lie algebra has no nontrivial one-dimensional representation. Because its elements are derivations,
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}P_\eta(a+tDa) =m\int_X\eta a^{m-1}Da =\int_XD(\eta a^m)=0. \end{equation*}
    This proves infinitesimal invariance of the polynomial under \(\mathfrak{so}(H^2(X,\C),q_X)\text{,}\) and hence invariance under the connected complex group \(SO(H^2(X,\C),q_X)\text{,}\) acting on its variables in \(H^2(X,\C)\text{.}\)
    Here is the elementary invariant-polynomial calculation when \(m>0\text{.}\) Choose \(e\) with \(q_X(e)=1\text{.}\) The special orthogonal group is transitive on the vectors of square one. Indeed, extend two such vectors to orthonormal bases to obtain an orthogonal transformation between them. If its determinant is \(-1\text{,}\) compose with reflection in another vector of the target orthonormal basis. This reflection fixes the target vector and changes the determinant to one. Here \(\dim H^2(X,\C)\geq3\text{,}\) so another basis vector exists. In particular, \(e\) and \(-e\) are in the same orbit.
    If \(m\) is odd, homogeneity gives \(P_\eta(e)=P_\eta(-e)=-P_\eta(e)\text{,}\) so both values vanish. Every vector \(a\) with \(q_X(a)\ne0\) is a nonzero scalar multiple of a square-one vector; hence \(P_\eta\) vanishes on that dense open set and therefore everywhere. If \(m\) is even, the same argument gives
    \begin{equation*} P_\eta(a)=P_\eta(e)q_X(a)^{m/2} \end{equation*}
    on the nonisotropic locus and hence everywhere by polynomial identity. Since \(m\) has the parity of \(k\text{,}\) these are precisely the two assertions.
    In the even case, for rational \(\eta\text{,}\) evaluate at any rational \(a\) of nonzero square. Both \(P_\eta(a)\) and the nonzero value \(q_X(a)^{m/2}\) are rational, so their ratio is rational. The same evaluation with real classes proves the real assertion.
    This proves the generalized Fujiki relation from the LLV structure and local Torelli. It does not require \(\eta\) to belong to the subalgebra generated by degree two. The vanishing in odd \(k\) concerns this intersection polynomial; it does not by itself assert that \(\eta=0\text{.}\)

    Corollary 11.17.

    Assume \(n\geq2\text{.}\) The subspace of \(\SH^4(X,\C)\) annihilated by the reduced LLV algebra is the line generated by the inverse quadratic class \(Q_X\) of Definition 2.11. Consequently,
    \begin{equation*} c_2(T_X)\in\SH^4(X) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad c_2(T_X)\in\Q Q_X. \end{equation*}

    Proof.
    Verbitsky’s injection identifies \(\SH^4(X,\C)\) with \(\operatorname{Sym}^2V\text{.}\) Through \(q_X\text{,}\) a symmetric tensor in this space determines a self-adjoint endomorphism of \(V\text{.}\) It is invariant under \(\mathfrak{so}(V,q_X)\) precisely when this endomorphism commutes with the standard orthogonal representation. That representation is irreducible for \(b\geq3\text{,}\) so Schur’s lemma makes the endomorphism scalar. The corresponding symmetric tensor is a multiple of \(q_X^{-1}\text{,}\) whose cup-product image is \(Q_X\text{.}\) The second Chern class is invariant by Proposition 11.15. If it lies in the subalgebra, it therefore lies on this line; the coefficient is rational because both classes are rational and \(Q_X\ne0\text{.}\) The reverse implication follows from the definition of \(Q_X\text{.}\)
    Reduced invariance does not imply membership in the Verbitsky component. For example,
    \begin{equation*} c_2(T_{S^{[n]}})\notin\SH^4(S^{[n]})\qquad(n\geq4) \end{equation*}
    for a K3 surface \(S\text{.}\) This is recorded in [BS22, Introduction], with its source in Markman’s computation of the Beauville–Bogomolov class. Thus a characteristic class annihilated by the reduced LLV algebra can have a nonzero component outside \(\SH^*(X)\text{.}\) The generalized Fujiki relations in Theorem 2.10 apply to these classes without requiring them to be polynomials in degree-two classes.

    Section 11.5 Salamon’s relation

    For a compact manifold \(X\text{,}\) write \(b_k=b_k(X)=\dim_\Q H^k(X,\Q)\text{,}\) \(P_X(t)=\sum_k b_kt^k\text{,}\) and \(\chi_{\mathrm{top}}(X)=P_X(-1)\text{.}\) This topological Euler characteristic differs from \(\chi(X,\mathcal O_X)=n+1\) for an IHS \(2n\)-fold. The latter was computed from holomorphic forms in Proposition 1.17.

    Theorem 11.18 (Salamon).

    For a compact hyperkähler manifold of dimension \(2n\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation} 3\sum_{k=0}^{4n}(-1)^k(k-2n)^2b_k =n\,\chi_{\mathrm{top}}(X).\tag{11.9} \end{equation}
    Equivalently,
    \begin{equation} n b_{2n} =2\sum_{j=1}^{2n}(-1)^j(3j^2-n)b_{2n-j}.\tag{11.10} \end{equation}
    See [Sal96, Theorem 1].
    Salamon proves the theorem using index theory and Hodge symmetries. It applies to hyperkähler products and tori as well as IHS manifolds. Salamon writes the real dimension as \(d=4n\) and gives the identity \(6P_X''(-1)+\frac12d(5-3d)P_X(-1)=0\text{.}\)

    Lemma 11.19.

    Under Poincaré duality in real dimension \(4n\text{,}\) Salamon’s derivative formula, (11.9), and (11.10) are equivalent.

    Proof.
    Poincaré duality gives \(b_k=b_{4n-k}\text{.}\) Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \sum_k(-1)^k k b_k =2n\,\chi_{\mathrm{top}}(X). \end{equation*}
    Since \(P_X''(-1)=\sum_k(-1)^k(k^2-k)b_k\text{,}\) substituting Salamon’s derivative identity gives
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \sum_k(-1)^k(k-2n)^2b_k &=P_X''(-1)+(2n-4n^2)\chi_{\mathrm{top}}(X)\\ &=\frac n3\,\chi_{\mathrm{top}}(X). \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    This is (11.9).
    Pair the terms of degrees \(2n-j\) and \(2n+j\text{.}\) The middle term in the weighted square sum is zero, and
    \begin{equation*} \sum_k(-1)^k(k-2n)^2b_k =2\sum_{j=1}^{2n}(-1)^j j^2b_{2n-j},\qquad \chi_{\mathrm{top}}(X)=b_{2n} +2\sum_{j=1}^{2n}(-1)^j b_{2n-j}. \end{equation*}
    Substituting these two equalities into (11.9) and isolating \(n b_{2n}\) gives (11.10). The rearrangement is reversible.

    Example 11.20.

    In dimension two, the relation is \(b_2=22-4b_1\text{.}\) It gives \(b_2=22\) for a K3 surface and \(b_2=6\) for a two-dimensional torus with \(b_1=4\text{.}\) In dimension four it gives
    \begin{equation*} b_4=46-25b_1+10b_2-b_3. \end{equation*}
    For IHS fourfolds \(b_1=0\text{,}\) so the relation becomes \(b_4=46+10b_2-b_3\text{.}\) The coefficient of \(b_3\) remains present even though odd Betti numbers vanish for the K3 Hilbert-square example.
    Salamon’s relation is linear in the graded dimensions. After substituting an LLV decomposition, it becomes a linear relation between the nonnegative representation multiplicities.

    Section 11.6 Betti numbers of K3 Hilbert schemes

    For a smooth projective surface, Göttsche’s formula computes the Betti numbers of all its Hilbert schemes at once. Let \(P_S(t)=\sum_{i=0}^4 b_i(S)t^i\text{,}\) with positive Betti coefficients as above, and let \(u\) be a second formal variable. We set \(S^{[0]}=\{\mathrm{pt}\}\text{.}\)

    Theorem 11.21 (Göttsche).

    For a smooth connected projective surface \(S\) over \(\C\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation} \sum_{n\geq0}P_{S^{[n]}}(t)u^n =\prod_{m\geq1} \frac{ (1+t^{2m-1}u^m)^{b_1(S)} (1+t^{2m+1}u^m)^{b_3(S)} }{ (1-t^{2m-2}u^m)^{b_0(S)} (1-t^{2m}u^m)^{b_2(S)} (1-t^{2m+2}u^m)^{b_4(S)} }.\tag{11.11} \end{equation}
    The identity is in \(\Z[t][[u]]\text{;}\) each coefficient uses only finitely many factors. See [Got90] and the author’s exposition [Got02, §2, equation (2.1)].
    The signs in the numerator depend on the convention for the Poincaré polynomial. Göttsche’s survey writes \(p_S(z)=\sum_i(-1)^i b_i(S)z^i\text{.}\) Substituting \(z=-t\) in that convention gives (11.11).
    For a K3 surface, \(b_0=b_4=1\text{,}\) \(b_2=22\text{,}\) and \(b_1=b_3=0\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation} \sum_{n\geq0}P_{S^{[n]}}(t)u^n =\prod_{m\geq1} \frac{1}{ (1-t^{2m-2}u^m)(1-t^{2m}u^m)^{22} (1-t^{2m+2}u^m)}.\tag{11.12} \end{equation}
    Every exponent of \(t\) is even, so \(H^{\mathrm{odd}}(S^{[n]},\Q)=0\) for every \(n\text{.}\) The same Betti numbers hold on every deformation of \(S^{[n]}\text{,}\) including nonprojective members, by invariance of singular cohomology in smooth proper families.

    Proposition 11.22.

    For a K3 surface \(S\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} P_{S^{[2]}}(t)=1+23t^2+276t^4+23t^6+t^8. \end{equation*}
    In particular, \(H^*(S^{[2]},\Q)=\SH^*(S^{[2]})\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Only the factors with \(m=1,2\) can contribute to the coefficient of \(u^2\text{.}\) For \(m=1\text{,}\) the factor is \((1-u)^{-1}(1-t^2u)^{-22}(1-t^4u)^{-1}\text{.}\) Its coefficient of \(u^2\) is
    \begin{equation*} 1+22t^2+254t^4+22t^6+t^8. \end{equation*}
    The term \(254t^4\) consists of \(\binom{23}{2}t^4\) from two degree-two factors and one further \(t^4\) from the degree-zero and degree-four factors. The \(m=2\) factor contributes \((t^2+22t^4+t^6)u^2\) at its first nonconstant term. Adding gives the stated polynomial.
    Here \(n=2\) and \(b=23\text{.}\) Formula (11.7) gives dimensions \(1,23,\binom{24}{2},23,1\text{,}\) with \(\binom{24}{2}=276\text{,}\) for the subalgebra generated by degree two. These equal the full Betti numbers in every degree. Inclusion is therefore equality.
    The Hilbert cube already has additional cohomology. Extracting the coefficient of \(u^3\) gives
    \begin{equation*} P_{S^{[3]}}(t)= 1+23t^2+299t^4+2554t^6+299t^8+23t^{10}+t^{12}. \end{equation*}
    The coefficient of \(t^4u^3\) can be checked without expanding the full product. Three contributions correspond to the partitions \(1+1+1\text{,}\) \(2+1\text{,}\) and \(3\) of the length. They are respectively \(254\text{,}\) \(44\text{,}\) and \(1\text{,}\) giving \(299\text{.}\) But \(\dim\SH^4(S^{[3]})=\binom{24}{2}=276\text{.}\) Thus the quotient \(H^4(S^{[3]},\Q)/\SH^4(S^{[3]})\) has dimension twenty-three.

    Example 11.23.

    The extra classes in the Hilbert cube form a single further irreducible LLV module. For \(X\) of \(\mathrm{K3}^{[3]}\) type, one has
    \begin{equation*} H^*(X,\C)\cong V_{(3)}\oplus\bigwedge^2W, \qquad \dim W=25, \end{equation*}
    by [GKLR22, Corollary 3.2 and Remark 3.3]. The exterior square of the standard orthogonal representation has highest weight \((1,1,0,\ldots,0)\text{.}\) We cite the decomposition; its cohomological grading can be checked directly.
    On \(W=\C e_-\oplus V\oplus\C e_+\text{,}\) the degree operator has weights \(-2,0,2\text{.}\) Its action on the exterior square therefore gives
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{c|c|c} h\text{-eigenvalue}&\text{subspace}&\text{dimension}\\ \hline -2&e_-\wedge V&23\\ 0&\bigwedge^2V\oplus\C(e_-\wedge e_+)&254\\ 2&e_+\wedge V&23 \end{array} \end{equation*}
    Since \(n=3\text{,}\) these eigenspaces lie in cohomological degrees four, six, and eight. They account for the differences \(299-276=23\) and \(2554-\binom{25}{3}=2554-2300=254\) between the full Betti numbers and the Verbitsky dimensions.
    The additional cohomology in degrees four, six, and eight forms one 300-dimensional LLV module.

    Example 11.24.

    The generalized Kummer fourfold has different behaviour:
    \begin{equation*} P_{K_2(A)}(t)= 1+7t^2+8t^3+108t^4+8t^5+7t^6+t^8. \end{equation*}
    See [GKLR22, Corollary 3.6]. In particular its third cohomology is nonzero. The subalgebra generated by \(H^2\) has degree-four dimension \(\binom{8}{2}=28\text{,}\) leaving eighty further degree-four classes, as well as all the odd cohomology. Salamon’s fourfold formula is satisfied: \(108=46+10\cdot7-8\text{.}\) In particular, odd cohomology need not vanish for IHS manifolds.
    Setting \(t=-1\) gives Euler characteristics:
    \begin{equation} \sum_{n\geq0}\chi_{\mathrm{top}}(S^{[n]})u^n =\prod_{m\geq1}(1-u^m)^{-24} =1+24u+324u^2+3200u^3+\cdots.\tag{11.13} \end{equation}
    For the Hilbert square the sum of its Betti numbers is \(1+23+276+23+1=324\text{;}\) for the Hilbert cube it is 3200. Since odd cohomology vanishes, these sums equal the Euler characteristics.

    Section 11.7 Riemann–Roch and Chern numbers of Hilbert schemes

    The Riemann–Roch polynomial of Section 2.5 can be computed for the Hilbert-scheme deformation types using line bundles induced from the surface. The calculation also gives characteristic numbers without computing the full cohomology ring.

    Proposition 11.25.

    For an IHS manifold \(X\) of \(\mathrm{K3}^{[n]}\) type,
    \begin{equation} \operatorname{RR}_X(t)=\binom{t/2+n+1}{n}.\tag{11.14} \end{equation}
    The binomial expression denotes the polynomial \(\frac1{n!}\prod_{j=2}^{n+1}(t/2+j)\text{.}\) Thus \(\chi(X,L)=\binom{q_X(c_1(L))/2+n+1}{n}\) for every line bundle \(L\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    By deformation invariance it suffices to compute on \(X=S^{[n]}\) for a projective K3 surface. Fix an ample bundle \(A\) on \(S\text{,}\) let \(a=\int_Sc_1(A)^2>0\text{,}\) and let \(A_n\) be the pullback to the Hilbert scheme of the descent of \(A^{\boxtimes n}\) to \(S^{(n)}\text{.}\) The descended bundle is ample, since its pullback by the finite quotient map is ample. Consequently \(A_n\) is nef. Moreover \(q_X(c_1(A_n))=a\text{,}\) so it is big by Proposition 7.2.
    We use Kawamata–Viehweg vanishing in the following form: if \(Y\) is smooth projective and \(B\) is nef and big, then \(H^i(Y,K_Y\otimes B)=0\) for \(i>0\text{;}\) see [Laz04, Chapter 4]. Since \(K_X\cong\cO_X\text{,}\) it gives \(\chi(X,A_n^{\otimes m})=h^0(X,A_n^{\otimes m})\) for every \(m>0\text{.}\)
    The symmetric product is normal, so the proper birational Hilbert–Chow map has \(\rho_*\cO_X=\cO_{S^{(n)}}\text{.}\) Descent and the projection formula identify sections with the \(\mathfrak S_n\)-invariant sections on \(S^n\text{.}\) The Künneth isomorphism in degree zero gives
    \begin{equation*} H^0(X,A_n^{\otimes m}) \cong\bigl(H^0(S,A^{\otimes m})^{\otimes n}\bigr)^{\mathfrak S_n} \cong\Sym^nH^0(S,A^{\otimes m}). \end{equation*}
    On the K3 surface, Riemann–Roch and Kodaira vanishing give \(h^0(S,A^{\otimes m})=m^2a/2+2\text{.}\) Consequently,
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{RR}_X(m^2a) =\chi(X,A_n^{\otimes m}) =\binom{m^2a/2+n+1}{n}. \end{equation*}
    The two degree-\(n\) polynomials agree at the infinitely many distinct numbers \(m^2a\text{.}\) They are equal.
    For a K3 surface the formula is \(\chi(S,L)=q_S(c_1(L))/2+2\text{.}\) For \(\mathrm{K3}^{[2]}\) type it is
    \begin{equation*} \chi(X,L)=\frac18q_X(c_1(L))^2+\frac54q_X(c_1(L))+3. \end{equation*}
    Its leading coefficient is \(c_X/4!=3/24=1/8\text{,}\) as required by (2.6).

    Proposition 11.26.

    For \(X\) of \(\mathrm{K3}^{[2]}\) type,
    \begin{equation*} c_2(T_X)=\frac65Q_X\in H^4(X,\Q),\qquad \int_Xc_2(T_X)^2=828,\qquad\int_Xc_4(T_X)=324. \end{equation*}
    For every \(a\in H^2(X)\text{,}\) one has \(\int_Xc_2(T_X)a^2=30q_X(a)\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    On a symplectic fourfold, \(\operatorname{td}_2=c_2/12\text{.}\) The coefficient of \(q_X(c_1(L))\) in Riemann–Roch is therefore \(C_X(c_2)/24\text{.}\) Comparing with the polynomial above gives \(C_X(c_2)=30\text{,}\) hence the last identity by generalized Fujiki.
    By Proposition 11.22, all of \(H^4(X)\) is \(\SH^4(X)\text{.}\) The line annihilated by the reduced LLV algebra in this space is \(\Q Q_X\text{,}\) by Corollary 11.17, and \(c_2\) belongs to that line. The inverse-class contraction formula Proposition 2.12 gives \(\int_XQ_Xa^2=25q_X(a)\text{,}\) since \(b_2=23\) and \(c_X=3\text{.}\) Comparing the two constants gives \(c_2=6Q_X/5\text{.}\)
    Polarization yields \(\int_XQ_Xab=25q_X(a,b)\text{.}\) In a basis \(e_i\) with inverse BBF matrix \((q^{ij})\text{,}\) contraction gives
    \begin{equation*} \int_XQ_X^2 =\sum_{i,j}q^{ij}\int_XQ_Xe_ie_j =25\sum_{i,j}q^{ij}q_{ij} =25\cdot23=575. \end{equation*}
    Thus \(\int_Xc_2^2=(36/25)575=828\text{.}\) Finally Chern–Gauss–Bonnet and (11.13) give \(\int_Xc_4=\chi_{\mathrm{top}}(X)=324\text{.}\)
    As a check, the fourfold Todd formula gives
    \begin{equation*} \chi(X,\cO_X) =\frac{1}{720}\int_X(3c_2^2-c_4) =\frac{3\cdot828-324}{720}=3. \end{equation*}

    The same polynomial invariant is explicit for generalized Kummer manifolds and the two O’Grady types. We retain the primitive integral BBF form of Chapter 2, with \(t=q_X(c_1(L))\) and \(c_X=C_X(1)\text{.}\) In particular, \(c_X\) denotes the coefficient in \(\int_Xa^{2n}=c_Xq_X(a)^n\text{,}\) rather than the small Fujiki constant.

    Theorem 11.27.

    For each deformation type in the table, the Riemann–Roch polynomial and Fujiki constant have the following values. The Riemann–Roch identity holds for every line bundle, including on nonprojective members.
    Here \(\binom{u}{r}=u(u-1)\cdots(u-r+1)/r!\) is a polynomial.     Proposition 11.29.

    The second Chern class has generalized Fujiki constant
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \mathrm{K3}^{[n]},\ n\geq1:&\quad C_X(c_2)=\frac{6(n+3)}{2n-1}c_X,\\ K_n(A),\ n\geq2:&\quad C_X(c_2)=\frac{6(n+1)}{2n-1}c_X. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}

    \begin{equation*} \mathrm{OG}_6:\ C_X(c_2)=288,\qquad \mathrm{OG}_{10}:\ C_X(c_2)=5040. \end{equation*}
    Thus, for example, every class \(a\in H^2(X)\) on an \(\mathrm{OG}_{10}\) manifold satisfies \(\int_Xc_2(T_X)a^8=5040q_X(a)^4\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Since \(\td_2=c_2/12\text{,}\) the coefficient of \(t^{n-1}\) in \(\operatorname{RR}_X(t)\) is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{C_X(c_2)}{12(2n-2)!}. \end{equation*}
    Write \(A_n,A_{n-1}\) for its two highest coefficients. As \(A_n=c_X/(2n)!\text{,}\) their ratio gives
    \begin{equation*} \frac{C_X(c_2)}{c_X}=\frac{12}{2n(2n-1)}\frac{A_{n-1}}{A_n}. \end{equation*}
    The Hilbert-scheme polynomial is \(\frac1{n!}\prod_{j=2}^{n+1}(t/2+j)\text{,}\) whose coefficient ratio is \(A_{n-1}/A_n=2\sum_{j=2}^{n+1}j=n(n+3)\text{.}\) For the Kummer polynomial \(\frac{n+1}{n!}\prod_{j=1}^n(t/2+j)\text{,}\) it is \(2\sum_{j=1}^nj=n(n+1)\text{.}\) Substituting these ratios proves both formulas.
    The \(\mathrm{OG}_6\) polynomial is the Kummer polynomial with \(n=3\text{,}\) so \(C_X(c_2)=60\cdot24/5=288\text{.}\) The \(\mathrm{OG}_{10}\) polynomial is the Hilbert-scheme polynomial with \(n=5\text{,}\) so \(C_X(c_2)=945\cdot48/9=5040\text{.}\) The intersection statement is generalized Fujiki with this constant.

    Corollary 11.30.

    The Riemann–Roch polynomial does not determine the deformation type, even among the known IHS manifolds of a fixed dimension.

    Proof.
    The types \(\mathrm{OG}_6\) and \(K_3(A)\) have identical polynomials, but their second Betti numbers are respectively eight and seven. Likewise \(\mathrm{OG}_{10}\) and \(\mathrm{K3}^{[5]}\) have identical polynomials, but their second Betti numbers are twenty-four and twenty-three. These Betti numbers are recorded in Table 5.16 and are constant under deformation. The respective types are therefore distinct.
    For an ample line bundle \(L\text{,}\) Kodaira vanishing and \(K_X\cong\cO_X\) identify these Euler characteristics with \(h^0(X,L)\text{.}\) In particular, if an ample bundle has BBF square two, its space of sections has dimension \((n+1)^2\) for generalized Kummer type, sixteen for \(\mathrm{OG}_6\text{,}\) and twenty-one for \(\mathrm{OG}_{10}\text{.}\) These numbers follow by substituting \(t=2\) in the table; the assertion is conditional on the existence of the stated polarization.
