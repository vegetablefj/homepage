    Chapter 6 Ext, Tor, and homological dimension

    Ext measures extensions and the failure of Hom to be exact; Tor measures the failure of tensor product to be exact. Their two variables admit different kinds of resolutions, and the comparison between these computations is part of the theory. We first work in abelian categories when possible, then state tensor and change-of-ring results for modules. Main references are [Wei94, Chapters 3–4] and [LiII, §3.14].

    Section 6.1 The two computations of Ext

    Definition 6.1 Ext groups.

    If an abelian category \(\mathcal A\) has enough injectives, define
    \begin{equation*} \Ext^n_{\mathcal A}(A,B)=R^n\Hom_{\mathcal A}(A,-)(B)=H^n\underline{\Hom}^{\bullet}(A,I_B). \end{equation*}
    If it has enough projectives, one can instead define \(\Ext^n_{\mathcal A}(A,B)=H^n\underline{\Hom}^{\bullet}(P_A,B)\text{.}\) In either construction \(n\geq0\text{,}\) the first variable is contravariant, and the second is covariant.

    Proposition 6.2.

    If \(\mathcal A\) has enough projectives and injectives, the two constructions of Ext are naturally isomorphic. They agree with Hom in degree zero and give long exact sequences in each variable. Moreover, \(\Ext^n(P,B)=0\) for projective \(P\) and \(\Ext^n(A,I)=0\) for injective \(I\text{,}\) whenever \(n>0\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    For resolutions \(P\to A\) and \(B\to I\text{,}\) compare \(\underline{\Hom}^{\bullet}(P,B)\) and \(\underline{\Hom}^{\bullet}(A,I)\) with the total complex of the first-quadrant double complex \(C^{p,q}=\Hom_{\mathcal A}(P^{-p},I^q)\text{,}\) inserting the Hom signs. Exactness of \(\Hom(P^{-p},-)\) and \(\Hom(-,I^q)\) makes both comparisons quasi-isomorphisms. The first-quadrant totalization argument is proved in Chapter 7. Comparison of resolutions makes the isomorphisms natural. The horseshoe lemma gives the long exact sequences, while one-term resolutions give the vanishing statements.
    Using the opposite source category converts the first variable into a covariant left exact functor, so its long exact sequence has the direction \(\Hom(A'',B)\to\Hom(A,B)\to\Hom(A',B)\to\Ext^1(A'',B)\text{.}\) The two variances must be retained when transporting extension classes.1
    In the injective construction, every fixed injective \(I\) makes \(\Hom(-,I)\) exact. Therefore resolving only \(B\) still gives a long exact sequence in \(A\text{,}\) even if the category has no projective objects. The projective construction has the dual property. Enough objects of one kind suffice to obtain the two-variable Ext theory; requiring both kinds is needed only for the displayed double-resolution comparison.

    Section 6.2 Extensions and their products

    Definition 6.3 Yoneda extensions.

    For \(n\geq1\text{,}\) an \(n\)-extension of \(A\) by \(B\) is an exact sequence
    \begin{equation*} 0\to B\to E_{n-1}\to\cdots\to E_0\to A\to0. \end{equation*}
    Equivalence is the relation generated by morphisms of these sequences which are the identity on the two endpoints. For \(n=1\text{,}\) such a morphism is an isomorphism. The Baer sum is obtained by taking the direct sum of extensions, pulling back along the diagonal of \(A\text{,}\) and pushing out along the addition map of \(B\text{.}\) Splicing extensions gives the Yoneda product
    \begin{equation*} \Ext^p(B,C)\times\Ext^q(A,B)\longrightarrow\Ext^{p+q}(A,C). \end{equation*}

    Proposition 6.4.

    In an abelian category with enough projectives or enough injectives, equivalence classes of \(n\)-extensions form an abelian group under Baer sum, naturally isomorphic to \(\Ext^n(A,B)\text{.}\) The zero class in degree one consists of the split short exact sequences. Splicing gives an associative bilinear product, with degree-zero morphisms acting by pullback and pushout.

    Proof.
    For degree one, choose \(0\to K\to P^0\to A\to0\) with \(P^0\) projective. Pulling an extension back to \(P^0\) splits it, and a choice of splitting gives a map \(K\to B\text{.}\) A different splitting changes this map by the restriction of a map \(P^0\to B\text{.}\) Conversely, pushing out \(0\to K\to P^0\to A\to0\) along \(K\to B\) produces an extension. These constructions are inverse and identify the group with \(\coker(\Hom(P^0,B)\to\Hom(K,B))=\Ext^1(A,B)\text{.}\)
    The higher comparison follows by cutting a resolution into short exact sequences and iterating this construction. The compatibility with Baer sum and splicing is the standard Yoneda comparison; see [Wei94, §3.4] and Stacks, Tag 06XP. The injective construction is dual.
    The definition by extensions makes sense without enough projectives or injectives, but its set-theoretic size must then be checked. In Chapter 9 we shall compare it with \(\Hom_{\D(\mathcal A)}(A,B[n])\) whenever these morphisms form sets.2 The graded algebra \(\Ext^*(A,A)\) need not be graded-commutative; such a conclusion requires extra structure.

    Proposition 6.5.

    Write an extension class of \(A\) by \(B\) as \(\xi\text{.}\)
    1. A morphism \(a:A'\to A\) pulls \(\xi\) back by taking the pullback at its right end; a morphism \(b:B\to B'\) pushes it out at its left end. These operations commute and give the two variances of \(\Ext^n\text{.}\)
    2. The inverse of a class under Baer sum is its pushout along \(-\id_B\text{.}\) The Baer sum is commutative and associative, and pullback, pushout, and splicing are additive.
    3. Given \(0\to B'\to B\to B''\to0\) with class \(\eta\in\Ext^1(B'',B')\text{,}\) its connecting map sends \(\xi\in\Ext^n(A,B'')\) to the splice \(\eta\xi\in\Ext^{n+1}(A,B')\text{.}\) Given \(0\to A'\to A\to A''\to0\) with class \(\zeta\in\Ext^1(A'',A')\text{,}\) the first-variable connecting map sends \(\xi\in\Ext^n(A',B)\) to \(\xi\zeta\text{.}\)
    In degree zero, splicing at an endpoint means the corresponding pullback or pushout.3

    Proof.
    Pullbacks of epimorphisms and pushouts of monomorphisms are exact in an abelian category, so the endpoint operations preserve extensions. Their interchange follows from the universal properties. For addition, taking a direct sum and then applying diagonal and addition at the ends gives the stated formulas; the interchange laws for those maps give associativity, commutativity, and distributivity. For the connecting morphism, lift an extension through a projective resolution, or compare it with an injective resolution. The boundary is the obstruction to extending that lift across the additional short exact sequence, represented precisely by concatenating the two extensions. The degree-one calculation in Proposition 6.4 fixes this identification, and dimension shifting proves all degrees. See [Wei94, §3.4].
    For a projective resolution, the full higher comparison can also be written without choosing a derived category. Its first \(n\) steps form an \(n\)-extension of \(A\) by \(\Omega^nA\text{.}\) Pushing it out along \(u:\Omega^nA\to B\) gives an extension class, and dimension shifting identifies
    \begin{equation*} \Ext^n(A,B)\cong\frac{\Hom(\Omega^nA,B)}{\im\bigl(\Hom(P^{-n+1},B)\to\Hom(\Omega^nA,B)\bigr)}\qquad(n\geq1). \end{equation*}
    A map extending across \(P^{-n+1}\) gives the zero class. Conversely the degree-one comparison applied to \(\Omega^{n-1}A\) shows that these are all relations. This constructs every higher extension from a fixed resolution and explains why changing that resolution does not change Ext.
    For instance, for \(m\geq2\text{,}\) the class of \(0\to\Z\xrightarrow{m}\Z\to\Z/m\to0\) generates \(\Ext^1_\Z(\Z/m,\Z)\text{.}\) Pushing out along multiplication by \(r\) gives its multiple by \(r\text{.}\) Splitting is equivalent to this multiple being zero, which occurs exactly when \(m\) divides \(r\text{.}\) These are endpoint operations on the fixed extension, so no choice of generators in a second resolution is needed.

    Section 6.3 Balanced bifunctors

    The two computations of Ext are an instance of a general resolution argument. It is useful to state the argument before specializing to tensor product: the exactness hypotheses belong to the partially resolved functors, and not merely to the categories.

    Proposition 6.6.

    Let \(T:\mathcal A\times\mathcal B\to\mathcal C\) be additive in each variable, with all three categories abelian.
    1. Suppose that \(\mathcal A,\mathcal B\) have enough projectives, that \(T\) is right exact in each variable, and that \(T(P,-)\) and \(T(-,Q)\) are exact whenever \(P,Q\) are projective. For projective resolutions \(P_A\to A\) and \(Q_B\to B\text{,}\) there are natural isomorphisms \(H^{-n}T(P_A,B)\cong H^{-n}T(A,Q_B)\text{.}\) These bifunctors have homological long exact sequences in each variable.
    2. Dually, suppose that both source categories have enough injectives, that \(T\) is left exact in each variable, and that \(T(I,-)\) and \(T(-,J)\) are exact for injective \(I,J\text{.}\) Then \(H^nT(I_A,B)\cong H^nT(A,J_B)\) naturally, with cohomological long exact sequences in both variables.

    Proof.
    In the projective case form \(T(P_A^p,Q_B^q)\text{,}\) with both indices nonpositive. Augmenting in either direction gives a map from its total complex to one of the single-resolution complexes. The cone has exact rows or columns by the partially resolved exactness hypotheses. Each diagonal is finite, so the double-complex theorem of Chapter 7 makes both augmentations quasi-isomorphisms. Comparison of resolutions proves naturality. The horseshoe lemma gives the long exact sequences. Reverse the degrees and arrows for the injective statement.
    Apply the second statement to \(\Hom:\mathcal A^{\op}\times\mathcal A\to\Ab\text{:}\) projective objects of \(\mathcal A\) are injective in its opposite category. For tensor product of modules, apply the first statement. These special cases are called balanced computations. A general additive bifunctor need not satisfy either exactness condition, so resolving the two variables separately need not give the same result.4

    Section 6.4 Tor and flat resolutions

    Definition 6.7 Tor groups.

    Let \(R\) be an associative unital ring, \(M\) a right \(R\)-module, and \(N\) a left \(R\)-module. For a projective resolution \(P\to M\) in right modules, put \(\Tor_n^R(M,N)=H^{-n}(P\otimes_RN)\text{.}\) A right module is flat if \(M\otimes_R-\) is exact; left flatness is defined using \(-\otimes_RN\text{.}\)

    Proposition 6.8.

    Tor can be computed using a projective or flat resolution of either variable. It is covariant in both variables, agrees with tensor product in degree zero, and has the homological long exact sequence in each variable. A right module \(M\) is flat if and only if \(\Tor_1^R(M,N)=0\) for every left module \(N\text{;}\) equivalently all its positive Tor groups vanish. There are corresponding assertions for left modules.

    Proof.
    If \(P\to M\) and \(Q\to N\) are projective resolutions, the maps from \(\Tot_\oplus(P\otimes_RQ)\) to \(P\otimes_RN\) and \(M\otimes_RQ\) are quasi-isomorphisms by exactness in the resolved variable. Both complexes are bounded above, so each total diagonal is finite. The same argument compares a flat resolution in one variable with a projective resolution in the other. The horseshoe lemma gives long exact sequences. Finally \(\Tor_1(M,-)=0\) makes tensor preserve monomorphisms by that sequence, and tensor is already right exact. See also Stacks, Tag 00LY.
    Functoriality can be seen before making any balance comparison. For \(a:M\to M'\) between right modules, choose a lift between projective resolutions; for \(b:N\to N'\text{,}\) tensor that lift with \(b\text{.}\) Different lifts are homotopic, and tensoring the homotopy with \(b\) gives a homotopy of the resulting total maps. Thus the map on Tor is independent of the lift and respects composition. Using flat resolutions computes these same maps by comparison with the projective construction, even when a map between the chosen flat resolutions cannot be lifted directly.
    The connecting morphism also has a concrete meaning. For \(0\to N'\to N\to N''\to0\text{,}\) choose a projective resolution \(P\to M\text{.}\) Every \(P^j\) is flat, so tensoring gives a short exact sequence of complexes. To compute \(\Tor_1(M,N'')\to M\otimes_RN'\text{,}\) lift a cycle in \(P^{-1}\otimes_RN''\) to \(P^{-1}\otimes_RN\text{,}\) take its differential, and regard the result in \(P^0\otimes_RN'\text{.}\) Its image in \(M\otimes_RN'\) is independent of the lift. This is exactly the cohomology connecting map, with index lowered by one.

    Example 6.9.

    Let \(R\) be commutative and let \(r\in R\) be a non-zero-divisor. The resolution \(0\to R\xrightarrow{r}R\to R/(r)\to0\) gives
    \begin{equation*} \Tor_1^R(R/(r),N)=\{x\in N:rx=0\},\qquad \Ext_R^1(R/(r),N)=N/rN. \end{equation*}
    Both functors vanish in degrees greater than one. Thus \(\Tor_1^\Z(\Z/m,\Z/n)\) and \(\Ext_\Z^1(\Z/m,\Z/n)\) are cyclic of order \(\gcd(m,n)\) for positive \(m,n\text{.}\) If \(r\) is a zero-divisor, the displayed sequence is not a resolution and these higher-vanishing conclusions no longer follow.

    Example 6.10.

    For ideals \(I,J\) of a commutative ring, tensor \(0\to I\to R\to R/I\to0\) with \(R/J\text{.}\) The Tor sequence and \(I\otimes_RR/J\cong I/IJ\) give
    \begin{equation*} \Tor_1^R(R/I,R/J)\cong(I\cap J)/IJ. \end{equation*}
    In particular \(\Tor_1^R(R/I,R/I)\cong I/I^2\text{.}\) This calculation needs no regularity or noetherian hypothesis and is often more useful than choosing a full resolution.

    Definition 6.11 The Koszul complex.

    For elements \(r_1,\ldots,r_s\) in a commutative ring \(R\text{,}\) the Koszul complex \(K(r_1,\ldots,r_s)\) has \(K^{-a}=\bigwedge^a R^s\) and differential
    \begin{equation*} d(e_{j_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{j_a})=\sum_{t=1}^a(-1)^{t-1}r_{j_t}\,e_{j_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat e_{j_t}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{j_a}. \end{equation*}
    The sequence is regular if its ideal is proper and multiplication by \(r_i\) is injective on \(R/(r_1,\ldots,r_{i-1})\) for each \(i\text{.}\)

    Proposition 6.12.

    The Koszul complex of a regular sequence is a finite free resolution of \(R/(r_1,\ldots,r_s)\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    The displayed differential agrees with the tensor differential on \(K(r_1)\otimes_R\cdots\otimes_RK(r_s)\text{.}\) Induct on \(s\text{.}\) Adjoining the last two-term factor gives, after the degreewise sign change identifying the tensor and cone conventions, the cone of multiplication by \(r_s\) on the previous Koszul complex. Its cohomology sequence has only the kernel and cokernel of multiplication by \(r_s\) on \(R/(r_1,\ldots,r_{s-1})\text{.}\) Regularity kills that kernel and identifies the cokernel with the required quotient.
    After tensoring this resolution with its quotient ring all differentials vanish, so the resulting Tor groups are the exterior powers of \((R/(r_1,\ldots,r_s))^s\text{.}\) Koszul complexes still exist for nonregular sequences, but their negative cohomology need not vanish. We use regularity only in the resolution statement. See [Wei94, §4.5].

    Section 6.5 Homological dimensions

    Definition 6.13 Projective, injective, and flat dimension.

    In an abelian category with enough projectives, the projective dimension \(\operatorname{pd}A\) is the least \(d\geq0\) for which \(A\) has a projective resolution in degrees \([-d,0]\text{,}\) or infinity if none exists. With enough injectives, injective dimension is defined using a resolution in degrees \([0,d]\text{.}\) We assign dimension zero to the zero object. For modules, write a subscript \(R\) to specify the category. Flat dimension of an \(R\)-module uses a flat resolution in degrees \([-d,0]\text{.}\) The left global dimension of \(R\) is the supremum of projective dimensions of left modules; left weak global dimension uses flat dimensions.

    Proposition 6.14.

    For a left module \(M\) and an integer \(d\geq0\text{:}\)
    1. \(\operatorname{pd}_RM\leq d\) if and only if \(\Ext_R^{d+1}(M,N)=0\) for every left module \(N\text{.}\)
    2. \(\operatorname{id}_RM\leq d\) if and only if \(\Ext_R^{d+1}(N,M)=0\) for every left module \(N\text{.}\)
    3. \(\operatorname{fd}_RM\leq d\) if and only if \(\Tor_{d+1}^R(N,M)=0\) for every right module \(N\text{.}\)
    In each case the condition implies vanishing in all larger degrees.

    Proof.
    Let \(K_d\) be the \(d\)th syzygy in a projective resolution, with \(K_0=M\text{.}\) Dimension shifting identifies \(\Ext_R^{d+1}(M,N)\) with \(\Ext_R^1(K_d,N)\text{.}\) Vanishing for every \(N\) says that every extension ending in \(K_d\) splits, which is equivalent to projectivity. For flat dimension, dimension shifting identifies the Tor condition with \(\Tor_1^R(N,K_d)=0\text{;}\) hence \(K_d\) is flat. A given length-\(d\) flat resolution proves the converse. The injective argument uses cosyzygies and the dual extension criterion. See [Wei94, Chapter 4].

    Example 6.15.

    For \(R=k[\epsilon]/(\epsilon^2)\) and \(k=R/(\epsilon)\text{,}\) multiplication by \(\epsilon\) gives the infinite projective resolution \(\cdots\xrightarrow{\epsilon}R\xrightarrow{\epsilon}R\to k\to0\text{.}\) Its exactness follows from \(\ker(\epsilon)=\epsilon R\text{.}\) Applying either \(\Hom_R(-,k)\) or \(-\otimes_Rk\) gives zero differentials, so \(\Ext_R^n(k,k)=k\) and \(\Tor_n^R(k,k)=k\) in every nonnegative degree. Finite-dimensional modules over a finite-dimensional algebra can therefore have infinite homological dimension.
    The projective and injective definitions, dimension tests, and proofs apply in any abelian category with the respective supply of resolutions. Flat dimension requires a specified tensor setting and its supply of flat resolutions; it is not a structure carried by an arbitrary abelian category. In that more general categorical setting, the statement \(R^nF(A)=0\) for \(n>d\) is a dimension bound relative to \(F\text{,}\) and different functors can give different bounds.

    Proposition 6.16.

    In an abelian category with enough projectives, a short exact sequence \(0\to A\to B\to C\to0\) gives
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}\operatorname{pd}B&\leq\max(\operatorname{pd}A,\operatorname{pd}C),\\\operatorname{pd}A&\leq\max(\operatorname{pd}B,\operatorname{pd}C-1),\\\operatorname{pd}C&\leq\max(\operatorname{pd}B,\operatorname{pd}A+1).\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Use the convention \(\infty-1=\infty\text{.}\) The dual inequalities hold for injective dimension when there are enough injectives.

    Proof.
    Apply the first-variable long exact sequence of Ext with arbitrary second argument. Above the indicated bounds, the two groups adjacent to the group under consideration vanish. The dimension test then gives each inequality. Reversing the short exact sequence in the opposite category gives the injective inequalities.
    These inequalities explain the role of the last syzygy: a resolution of length \(d\) is possible exactly when a \(d\)th syzygy is projective. It is not enough that a chosen object appearing as a term of the resolution have small dimension; the kernels connecting the terms are what transmit the bound. Over \(\Z\text{,}\) all modules have projective dimension at most one because subgroups of free abelian groups are free. The group \(\Q\) is flat but not projective, so flat and projective dimension can already differ in this example.

    Section 6.6 Changing the ring

    For a ring homomorphism \(R\to S\text{,}\) restriction of scalars has left adjoint \(S\otimes_R-\) and right adjoint \(\Hom_R(S,-)\text{.}\) In the latter, \(S\) is an \((R,S)\)-bimodule and the left \(S\)-action is \((s\phi)(t)=\phi(ts)\text{.}\) These bimodule structures are necessary even though restriction itself does not change the underlying abelian groups.

    Proposition 6.17.

    Let \(R\to S\) be a flat homomorphism of commutative rings and let \(M,N\) be \(R\)-modules.
    1. There are natural isomorphisms \(S\otimes_R\Tor_n^R(M,N)\cong\Tor_n^S(S\otimes_RM,S\otimes_RN)\text{.}\)
    2. If \(M\) admits a projective resolution whose terms are finitely generated, there are natural isomorphisms \(S\otimes_R\Ext_R^n(M,N)\cong\Ext_S^n(S\otimes_RM,S\otimes_RN)\text{.}\)
    3. For an \(S\)-module \(T\text{,}\) there are natural isomorphisms \(\Ext_S^n(S\otimes_RM,T)\cong\Ext_R^n(M,T)\text{,}\) where the right-hand side uses restriction of scalars.

    Proof.
    Tensor a projective resolution of \(M\) with \(S\text{.}\) Flatness preserves its exactness, and extension of scalars preserves projectives. For Tor, the tensor associativity isomorphism identifies the computing complexes. For the second assertion, finite generation and projectivity give \(S\otimes_R\Hom_R(P,N)\cong\Hom_S(S\otimes_RP,S\otimes_RN)\) in each degree. Flatness commutes with their cohomology. The third assertion uses the ordinary extension-restriction adjunction degreewise, with no finiteness assumption on the resolution.
    The finiteness condition in the Ext base-change statement cannot simply be discarded. For \(R=\Z\text{,}\) \(S=\Q\text{,}\) \(M=\Q\text{,}\) and \(N=\Z\text{,}\) its degree-zero source is zero and its target is \(\Q\text{.}\) Nor does finite presentation alone justify every higher degree: the needed finite generation concerns the relevant part of a resolution. More general change-of-ring comparisons are derived adjunctions and spectral sequences; they will be stated after the machinery of Chapter 11 and Chapter 12 is available.
