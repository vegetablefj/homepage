    Chapter 1 Additive and abelian categories

    The operations on complexes require addition of morphisms; their cohomology requires kernels and quotients. These requirements lead to additive and abelian categories. We keep them separate: a category can support chain homotopies and mapping cones even when it has no cohomology objects. The definitions and exactness criteria here follow [LiII, Chapters 1–2] and [KS06, Chapter 8].

    Section 1.1 Addition and biproducts

    Definition 1.1 Preadditive and additive categories.

    1. A preadditive category is a category \(\mathcal A\) in which each \(\Hom_{\mathcal A}(X,Y)\) is an abelian group and composition is additive in each variable. Its additive identity is the zero morphism \(0_{X,Y}\text{.}\)
    2. A biproduct of \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) consists of an object \(B\) and morphisms \(i_j:X_j\to B\text{,}\) \(p_j:B\to X_j\) satisfying \(p_j i_k=\delta_{jk}\id\) and \(\sum_j i_jp_j=\id_B\text{.}\) We denote it by \(\bigoplus_jX_j\text{.}\)
    3. An additive category is a preadditive category with a zero object and all finite biproducts. The empty biproduct is a zero object. An additive functor is a functor inducing group homomorphisms on all morphism groups.
    The biproduct equations say that \(B\) is both a product and a coproduct, with the indicated projections and inclusions. A morphism between finite biproducts is a matrix, and composition is matrix multiplication. This gives a categorical meaning to block matrices without choosing bases.1

    Proposition 1.2.

    In a preadditive category, a finite product is a biproduct, and a finite coproduct is a biproduct. An additive functor preserves zero objects and finite biproducts. Conversely, a functor between additive categories that preserves zero objects and finite biproduct diagrams is additive.

    Proof.
    For a product \(B=\prod_jX_j\text{,}\) define \(i_k\) by its components \(p_ji_k=\delta_{jk}\id\text{.}\) The two endomorphisms \(\sum_ki_kp_k\) and \(\id_B\) have the same composites with every projection, so they agree. The coproduct assertion is dual. An additive functor preserves the defining equations. For the converse, addition is recovered from the diagonal and codiagonal by \(f+g=\nabla_Y(f\oplus g)\Delta_X\text{.}\) Preservation of the biproduct diagrams preserves this formula.

    Example 1.3.

    The categories \(\Mod(R)\) and finitely generated projective \(R\)-modules are additive. The latter category usually has fewer kernels and cokernels. For example, multiplication by \(2\) on \(\Z\) is both monic and epic in the category of finitely generated free abelian groups: cancellation on either side follows because the relevant morphism groups are torsion-free. It is not an isomorphism. This category is therefore not abelian.
    There is an intrinsic version of additivity. In a category with a zero object and finite products and coproducts, there is a canonical map from a coproduct to the corresponding product, whose diagonal components are identities and whose other components are zero. If these maps are invertible, the diagonal–codiagonal formula defines a commutative monoid structure on every morphism set. Requiring these monoids to be groups recovers an additive category. Thus the addition is determined by the categorical structure when it exists; it is not an arbitrary choice of addition on each set. See [LiII, §1.3].

    Proposition 1.4.

    The opposite of an additive category is additive. If \(I\) is small and \(\mathcal A\) is additive, the functor category \(\Fun(I,\mathcal A)\) is additive, with addition of natural transformations and finite biproducts defined componentwise. If \(\mathcal A\) is abelian, so is this functor category; kernels, cokernels, and exactness are computed componentwise.

    Proof.
    Adding the component equations of naturality proves that the sum of two natural transformations is natural. A morphism of diagrams carries the kernel of each component into the next kernel, by its universal property. This defines the kernel diagram and its universal map. The cokernel construction is dual. The coimage-to-image map is an isomorphism because each of its components is one.
    These constructions apply to diagrams of modules and later to complexes. A ring can itself be regarded as a preadditive category with one object: its endomorphism group is the additive group of the ring and composition is multiplication. An additive covariant functor from this category to \(\Ab\) is a left module. The one-object category is usually not additive because the required finite biproducts have not been added.
    Finite biproduct notation does not identify infinite products and coproducts. For a small family of abelian groups, \(\bigoplus_iX_i\to\prod_iX_i\) includes the families with finite support. Even when both objects exist in an abelian category, their universal properties remain different. This distinction accounts for the different totalizations used later.

    Section 1.2 Kernels, images, and exactness

    Definition 1.5 Abelian categories and exact sequences.

    Let \(\mathcal A\) be additive.
    1. A kernel of \(f:X\to Y\) represents morphisms into \(X\) annihilated by \(f\text{;}\) a cokernel represents morphisms out of \(Y\) annihilating \(f\text{.}\) When they exist, set \(\im(f)=\ker(\coker f)\) and \(\operatorname{coim}(f)=\coker(\ker f)\text{.}\)
    2. The category \(\mathcal A\) is abelian if all kernels and cokernels exist and the canonical morphism \(\operatorname{coim}(f)\to\im(f)\) is an isomorphism for every \(f\text{.}\)
    3. In an abelian category, a sequence \(X\xrightarrow{f}Y\xrightarrow{g}Z\) is exact at \(Y\) if \(\im(f)=\ker(g)\) as subobjects of \(Y\text{.}\) A short exact sequence is an exact sequence \(0\to X\to Y\to Z\to0\text{.}\)
    The canonical map in this definition comes from factoring \(f\) through its cokernel of the kernel and then through its kernel of the cokernel. Equality of subobjects means equality of their classes of monomorphisms, not equality of chosen kernel objects. The definition gives a factorization
    \begin{equation*} X\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{coim}(f)\xrightarrow{\sim}\im(f)\hookrightarrow Y. \end{equation*}
    Thus image factorization has the same form as for modules.2

    Proposition 1.6.

    Let \(\mathcal A\) be abelian.
    1. Every monomorphism is the kernel of its cokernel, and every epimorphism is the cokernel of its kernel. A morphism that is both monic and epic is an isomorphism.
    2. Finite limits and finite colimits exist. Epimorphisms are stable under pullback, and monomorphisms are stable under pushout.
    3. For subobjects \(L\subseteq M\subseteq X\text{,}\) there is a canonical isomorphism \((X/L)/(M/L)\simeq X/M\text{.}\) For subobjects \(L,M\subseteq X\text{,}\) there is a canonical isomorphism \(M/(L\cap M)\simeq(L+M)/L\text{.}\)
    The first assertion follows from image factorization. Finite limits are constructed using biproducts and kernels; for example, the pullback of \(f:X\to Z\) and \(g:Y\to Z\) is \(\ker((f,-g):X\oplus Y\to Z)\text{.}\) The dual construction gives pushouts. The stability and quotient assertions are the diagram and isomorphism lemmas in [LiII, §§2.3 and 2.6]. These arguments use the abelian axiom, so they do not extend to every additive category with kernels and cokernels.
    More explicitly, a kernel \(k:K\to X\) satisfies \(fk=0\text{,}\) and every \(u:T\to X\) with \(fu=0\) has a unique factorization through \(k\text{.}\) The cokernel has the reversed property. A morphism is monic if and only if its kernel is zero, and epic if and only if its cokernel is zero. In an additive category with kernels and cokernels these tests already hold; the stronger assertions that every monomorphism and epimorphism is normal are what the abelian axiom adds. Equivalently, an additive category with kernels and cokernels is abelian if every monomorphism is a kernel and every epimorphism is a cokernel.

    Proposition 1.7.

    In an abelian category, intersections of two subobjects are their pullbacks, and the sum \(L+M\subseteq X\) is the image of \(L\oplus M\to X\text{.}\) Inverse image along \(f:X\to Y\) is defined by pullback, and direct image is image factorization. They satisfy
    \begin{equation*} f(L)\subseteq N\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L\subseteq f^{-1}(N). \end{equation*}
    For \(L\subseteq X\text{,}\) subobjects of \(X/L\) correspond to subobjects of \(X\) containing \(L\text{.}\) The subobject lattice is modular: if \(L\subseteq N\subseteq X\text{,}\) then \((L+M)\cap N=L+(M\cap N)\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    The pullback property proves the displayed equivalence. Pulling back a subobject of \(X/L\) gives a subobject containing the kernel \(L\text{;}\) conversely, quotienting a subobject containing \(L\) gives a subobject of \(X/L\text{.}\) Image factorization and stability of epimorphisms under pullback show that these operations are inverse. To prove modularity, pass to \(X/L\text{.}\) Both sides correspond to the image of \(M\cap N\to X/L\text{,}\) since the inverse image of \(N/L\) in \(M\) is \(M\cap N\text{.}\) The correspondence gives equality in \(X\text{.}\)
    A subobject is an isomorphism class of monomorphisms with fixed target. An intersection or sum is therefore a subobject, even when no particular representative has been chosen. We do not yet assert that all subobjects form a small set; a generator will imply that size property in Chapter 2. The finite constructions in this section need no such assumption.

    Proposition 1.8.

    For a commutative square with arrows \(i:A\to B\text{,}\) \(j:A\to C\text{,}\) \(u:B\to D\text{,}\) and \(v:C\to D\text{,}\) form the sequence
    \begin{equation*} A\xrightarrow{(i,-j)}B\oplus C\xrightarrow{(u,v)}D. \end{equation*}
    The square is a pullback if and only if the first arrow is a kernel of the second. It is a pushout if and only if the second is a cokernel of the first. It is both if and only if the sequence, extended by zero at both ends, is short exact. Pulling back a short exact sequence along a map to its last term gives a short exact sequence with the same kernel. Pushing out along a map from its first term gives one with the same cokernel.

    Proof.
    A map to \(B\oplus C\) is a pair of maps, and its composite to \(D\) vanishes precisely when their two composites cancel. Changing the sign on the second component converts this condition into the pullback condition. The pushout assertion is dual. For the last assertions use stability of epimorphisms under pullback and of monomorphisms under pushout; the universal properties identify the remaining kernel and cokernel.
    Consequently a short exact sequence may be pulled back in its quotient variable and pushed out in its subobject variable. These operations explain the two variances of extension classes before any resolution is chosen. They will define the functoriality of Yoneda extensions in Chapter 6.

    Example 1.9.

    Modules over any ring form an abelian category. Finite-dimensional vector spaces and finitely generated abelian groups form abelian categories, although they have no arbitrary direct sums. Finitely generated modules over a left noetherian ring form an abelian category because kernels remain finitely generated. For a general ring this assertion can fail at kernels.

    Definition 1.10 Split sequences and idempotents.

    A sequence \(0\to X\xrightarrow{i}Y\xrightarrow{p}Z\to0\) in an additive category is split exact if it is isomorphic to the biproduct sequence. An idempotent \(e:Y\to Y\) splits if there are \(j:X\to Y\) and \(r:Y\to X\) with \(rj=\id_X\) and \(jr=e\text{.}\)

    Proposition 1.11.

    A short exact sequence in an abelian category is split if and only if its epimorphism has a section, equivalently if its monomorphism has a retraction. Every idempotent in an abelian category splits.

    Proof.
    If \(ps=\id_Z\text{,}\) then \(\id_Y-sp\) factors uniquely through \(i=\ker p\text{,}\) giving \(r:Y\to X\text{.}\) The relations \(ri=\id_X\text{,}\) \(rs=0\text{,}\) and \(ir+sp=\id_Y\) show that \((i,s):X\oplus Z\to Y\) has inverse \((r,p)\text{.}\) The retraction criterion is dual. For an idempotent \(e\text{,}\) factor \(e=jq\) through its image. Since \(ej=j\text{,}\) cancellation gives \(qj=\id\text{.}\)
    Additive functors preserve split exact sequences. Exact sequences need not split: \(0\to\Z\xrightarrow{2}\Z\to\Z/2\Z\to0\) has no section. An additive category equipped with its split exact sequences is an example of an exact category. More general exact categories specify which kernel–cokernel pairs count as exact, subject to stability and composition axioms; their theory is developed in [Buh10].3

    Section 1.3 Finiteness of objects

    Definition 1.12 Chain conditions and length.

    An object of an abelian category is noetherian if every increasing sequence of subobjects stabilizes, and artinian if every decreasing sequence stabilizes. A nonzero object is simple if it has no proper nonzero subobject. An object has finite length if it has a finite filtration by subobjects with simple successive quotients; zero has length zero.
    These conditions concern subobjects in the specified category. Finite generation and finite presentation, introduced through colimits in later chapters, are different definitions. For modules, the noetherian condition is equivalent to finite generation of every submodule, including the whole module. Finite generation of the whole module alone does not suffice over an arbitrary ring.

    Proposition 1.13.

    An object has finite length if and only if it is both noetherian and artinian. Its simple factors, counted with multiplicity, are independent of the chosen filtration up to permutation and isomorphism. In a short exact sequence, the middle object has finite length if and only if both outer objects do, and its length is then the sum of their lengths.
    For the existence assertion, a nonzero noetherian object has a maximal proper subobject. Repeat inside that subobject. The resulting decreasing sequence stops by the artinian condition and gives a composition series. Conversely, induction on a composition series proves both chain conditions, using intersection with a subobject and its image in the quotient. Uniqueness of factors and additivity of length are the Jordan–Hölder theorem; see [LiII, §2.7]. This statement counts isomorphism classes with multiplicity, not distinguished subobjects of the original object.

    Example 1.14.

    The abelian group \(\Z\) is noetherian, since every subgroup is cyclic, but is not artinian: the chain \(\Z\supset2\Z\supset4\Z\supset\cdots\) never stabilizes. A finite-dimensional vector space has length equal to its dimension. A finitely generated module over a noetherian ring can have infinite length, as the first example shows.
    These distinctions will matter when comparing general Grothendieck categories with locally finite categories in tensor theory. Exactness is a condition on sequences; finite length is a condition on objects. Neither definition should be inferred merely from the presence of the other in a familiar example.

    Proposition 1.15.

    Subobjects, quotients, and extensions of noetherian objects are noetherian. The same assertion holds for artinian objects. A nonzero morphism between simple objects is an isomorphism; consequently the endomorphism ring of a simple object is a division ring.

    Proof.
    In an extension \(0\to A\to B\to C\to0\text{,}\) an increasing chain in \(B\) gives increasing chains of intersections with \(A\) and images in \(C\text{.}\) If both stabilize, the short exact sequences for two successive terms and the short five lemma show that the original terms agree. Subobjects and quotients use these same intersection and inverse-image operations. Decreasing chains give the artinian statement. For a nonzero map between simple objects, its kernel must be zero and its image must be the whole target. Image factorization makes it an isomorphism.
    The last assertion is Schur’s lemma. It gives a division ring, not a chosen coefficient field. If the category is \(k\)-linear, \(k\) is algebraically closed, and \(\End(S)\) is finite-dimensional over \(k\text{,}\) then \(\End(S)=k\id_S\text{:}\) each endomorphism has an eigenvalue as a linear operator on the finite-dimensional algebra, so its difference from some scalar cannot be invertible and must be zero.4

    Definition 1.16 Semisimplicity in finite length.

    An object of finite length is semisimple if it is a finite direct sum of simple objects, allowing the empty sum for zero.

    Proposition 1.17.

    For a finite-length object \(X\text{,}\) the following are equivalent: \(X\) is semisimple; every subobject of \(X\) is a direct summand; every short exact sequence with middle term \(X\) splits. Subobjects and quotients of such an object are semisimple. If \(S_1,\ldots,S_r\) are pairwise nonisomorphic simple objects, then
    \begin{equation*} \End\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^r S_i^{\oplus n_i}\right)\simeq\prod_{i=1}^r M_{n_i}(\End(S_i)). \end{equation*}

    Proof.
    The equivalence between the last two conditions is the splitting criterion. If every subobject splits, choose a simple subobject, split it off, and induct on length. For the reverse implication, write \(X\) as a sum of \(n\) simple objects and induct on \(n\text{.}\) A nonzero subobject \(Y\) contains a simple subobject \(T\text{.}\) Some projection from \(T\) to a simple summand of \(X\) is nonzero, hence invertible. That projection gives a retraction onto \(T\) and a decomposition \(X=T\oplus X'\text{,}\) with \(X'\) a sum of \(n-1\) simples. Restricting the retraction to \(Y\) gives \(Y=T\oplus(Y\cap X')\text{.}\) Induction supplies the required complement and semisimplicity. Quotients are the complementary summands. Schur’s lemma removes the off-diagonal blocks in the endomorphism ring formula.
    Semisimple objects need not be closed under extensions. The sequence \(0\to\Z/p\Z\to\Z/p^2\Z\to\Z/p\Z\to0\) has simple outer terms and a nonsemisimple middle term. For modules of arbitrary size, the usual definition allows arbitrary direct sums of simple modules; the submodule and splitting characterizations still hold. We have stated the finite-length version separately because an arbitrary abelian category need not possess those sums. These conventions and the general semisimple-module result are treated in [LiII, §2.7].

    Section 1.4 Exact functors

    Definition 1.18 Exactness of additive functors.

    Let \(F:\mathcal A\to\mathcal B\) be additive between abelian categories. It is left exact if it sends every short exact sequence to an exact sequence \(0\to F(X)\to F(Y)\to F(Z)\text{;}\) it is right exact if \(F(X)\to F(Y)\to F(Z)\to0\) is exact. It is exact if it has both properties.

    Proposition 1.19.

    An additive functor is left exact if and only if it preserves kernels, equivalently finite limits; the dual criterion holds for right exactness. An exact functor preserves images and coimages. A faithful exact functor reflects exact sequences and isomorphisms. For every \(X\text{,}\) the functors \(\Hom(X,-)\) and \(\Hom(-,X)\) are left exact, the second viewed on \(\mathcal A^\op\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Factor a morphism through its image and apply the short exact sequence criterion to its kernel sequence; this proves the kernel criterion. Additivity already preserves finite biproducts. Preservation of kernels and cokernels gives preservation of images. To reflect exactness, an exact sequence test reduces to the vanishing of \(\ker(g)/\im(f)\) when \(gf=0\text{.}\) Faithfulness reflects zero morphisms and zero objects. The Hom assertions are the universal properties of kernels and cokernels.
    Contravariance matters in this assertion: applying \(\Hom(-,X)\) to \(0\to A\to B\to C\to0\) gives \(0\to\Hom(C,X)\to\Hom(B,X)\to\Hom(A,X)\text{.}\) Surjectivity of the last arrow is an additional lifting property, addressed in Chapter 5.

    Proposition 1.20.

    An additive left adjoint between abelian categories is right exact; an additive right adjoint is left exact. A functor with both a left and a right adjoint is exact, provided it is additive. If an exact functor reflects zero objects, it reflects exact sequences and is faithful.

    Proof.
    Left adjoints preserve colimits and right adjoints preserve limits. For the last assertion, exactness identifies the image of a morphism after applying the functor with the functor of its image. If a morphism becomes zero, its image becomes zero, hence was zero already. This proves faithfulness, and the preceding criterion then reflects exactness.
    Limits and colimits of diagrams explain a frequent asymmetry. When the required limits exist, the limit functor from a diagram category is right adjoint to the constant-diagram functor, hence left exact. A colimit functor is left adjoint and right exact. The assertion that a particular infinite limit or colimit is exact is an additional condition. For example, filtered colimits of modules are exact, while inverse limits need not preserve epimorphisms; this failure will give \(\lim^1\text{.}\)

    Section 1.5 Embedding and finite diagrams

    Theorem 1.21.

    Every small abelian category \(\mathcal A\) admits a fully faithful exact functor \(E:\mathcal A\to\Mod(R)\) for some ring \(R\text{.}\) The same holds for an essentially small abelian category after choosing a small equivalent category.
    This is the Freyd–Mitchell embedding theorem, in the form of [LiII, Theorem B.6.3]. That source writes right modules; taking the opposite ring gives our left-module version. The target contains an exact full copy of the given abelian category. It may contain many additional modules, and the embedded category need not be closed under their arbitrary submodules or colimits. The ring depends on the chosen construction.5
    The proof is not a choice of underlying elements for each object. One construction first embeds \(\mathcal A\) exactly into \(\operatorname{Pro}(\mathcal A)\text{.}\) The opposite category \(\operatorname{Ind}(\mathcal A^\op)\) is Grothendieck and has an injective cogenerator; reversing arrows supplies projective objects in the pro-category. A sufficiently large projective generator, chosen to cover every object of the original small category, gives an exact Hom functor to modules. The lifting arguments in [LiII, Lemmas B.6.1–B.6.2] prove full faithfulness on the original category. We use the theorem rather than develop pro-objects here.

    Proposition 1.22.

    Let \(\mathcal A\) be a locally small abelian category. Every finite diagram in \(\mathcal A\) is contained in an essentially small full abelian subcategory whose inclusion is exact. One may include any specified finite collection of its kernels, cokernels, and image factorizations. Consequently a finite diagram argument involving these constructions and their exactness can be checked after a fully faithful exact embedding of that subcategory into a module category.

    Proof.
    Start with the objects of the diagram and the specified additional objects. Add a zero object and representatives of all finite biproducts, kernels, and cokernels of maps between the objects already chosen. Repeat this operation countably many times and take the full subcategory on the resulting objects. At each stage there is only a small set of maps by local smallness, so the union has a small set of objects up to the chosen size convention. Every finite construction involving objects of the union occurs at a later stage. The subcategory is additive and closed under the ambient kernels and cokernels. Its coimage-to-image maps are the ambient isomorphisms, so it is abelian and its inclusion is exact. Apply Theorem 1.21 to a small equivalent category.
    A faithful exact functor preserves and reflects the finite exactness conditions by Proposition 1.19. This justifies using elements to prove the diagram lemmas, even when the original category has no chosen underlying-set functor. It does not justify transferring projectivity, injectivity, infinite products, infinite colimits, or a tensor product. Those properties involve extra tests or structure beyond the finite exact diagram. The Gabriel–Popescu realization in Chapter 2 makes a different assertion: it provides a generally left exact fully faithful embedding of an entire Grothendieck category, together with an exact left adjoint.

    Section 1.6 Diagram lemmas

    Proposition 1.23.

    Given a morphism of short exact sequences in an abelian category, with vertical maps \(a:A\to A'\text{,}\) \(b:B\to B'\text{,}\) and \(c:C\to C'\text{,}\) there is a natural exact sequence
    \begin{equation*} 0\to\ker a\to\ker b\to\ker c\xrightarrow{\delta}\coker a\to\coker b\to\coker c\to0. \end{equation*}
    In particular, if \(a\) and \(c\) are isomorphisms, then \(b\) is an isomorphism.
    Here is the construction of \(\delta\text{.}\) Pull back \(B\twoheadrightarrow C\) along \(\ker c\to C\text{,}\) obtaining an epimorphism \(E\to\ker c\) with kernel \(A\text{.}\) The composite \(E\to B\xrightarrow{b}B'\) factors through \(A'=\ker(B'\to C')\text{.}\) Its composite with \(A'\to\coker a\) vanishes on \(A\text{,}\) so descends to \(\delta:\ker c\to\coker a\text{.}\) Exactness and naturality follow from the kernel and cokernel calculations in [LiII, §2.3]. The last assertion is the short five lemma.
    This construction explains why the connecting arrow is canonical even when an elementwise description chooses lifts. Module calculations can help discover the arrow, but its definition uses only a pullback and a quotient. The same construction, applied degree by degree, produces the connecting morphism in the cohomology sequence of complexes.

    Proposition 1.24.

    1. Consider a commutative diagram with exact rows \(A_1\to A_2\to A_3\to A_4\to A_5\) and \(B_1\to B_2\to B_3\to B_4\to B_5\text{,}\) and vertical maps \(f_i:A_i\to B_i\text{.}\) If \(f_1\) is epic and \(f_2,f_4\) are monic, then \(f_3\) is monic. If \(f_2,f_4\) are epic and \(f_5\) is monic, then \(f_3\) is epic. Thus \(f_3\) is invertible if \(f_2,f_4\) are invertible, \(f_1\) is epic, and \(f_5\) is monic.
    2. In a commutative three-by-three diagram with all columns short exact, if the top and middle rows are short exact, then the bottom row is short exact. The dual assertion reconstructs the top row from the middle and bottom rows.
    These are the four lemma, five lemma, and the stated forms of the nine lemma. One proves the first by replacing the row maps by their image factorizations and applying the snake lemma to the resulting short exact sequences. Applying the same construction to the maps between the first two rows proves the second. See [LiII, §2.3] for the diagrams and the categorical proofs. The endpoint assumptions in the five lemma cannot be omitted: they control the kernel and cokernel contributions entering from outside its middle three terms.
    Element notation is safe for modules but is not the definition of exactness in an abstract abelian category. Nor does exactness mean that \(\Hom(T,-)\) takes an exact sequence to a sequence surjective at its last term for every \(T\text{.}\) That stronger statement splits every short exact sequence by taking \(T\) to be its quotient. The categorical kernel, image, and pullback constructions above retain the distinction.
