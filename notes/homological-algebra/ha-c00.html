    Chapter 0 Conventions and sources

    Homological algebra studies the failure of exactness and the constructions that preserve its consequences. Resolutions give explicit calculations; derived categories express the constructions independently of a resolution. These notes develop both descriptions, then study localization, monoidal structures, and an abstract form of the six operations.
    The prerequisites are modules and ordinary category theory, including adjunctions, limits, and localization. The accompanying notes on 1-category theory develop that language. Additive, abelian, and monoidal structures are established here with their exactness properties. Concrete theories of sheaves and geometric duality have separate developments.

    Section 0.1 Size, rings, and variance

    Fix a universe of small sets, contained in a larger universe when necessary. Categories are locally small at the indicated scale. Small limits, colimits, generating sets, and indexed sums refer to the fixed universe. A localization is allowed initially to live in a larger universe; local smallness at the original scale must be proved or assumed.1
    Rings are associative with identity, and ring homomorphisms preserve the identity. An \(R\)-module is a left module unless the side is specified. Tensor products over a noncommutative ring require a right action on the first factor and a left action on the second. When \(\Mod(R)\) is treated as a symmetric monoidal category under \(\otimes_R\text{,}\) the ring \(R\) is commutative. The zero ring is allowed.
    We write \(\Hom_{\mathcal A}(X,Y)\) for the group of morphisms in an additive category. An internal Hom is an object of a specified monoidal category, denoted \([X,Y]\) in the abstract monoidal chapters; the notation \(\underline{\Hom}(X,Y)\) is also used when its ambient category is specified. Its existence is extra structure. The complex of morphisms between complexes is distinguished from both: it is denoted \(\underline{\Hom}^{\bullet}(X,Y)\text{.}\)
    A functor is called exact in an abelian context if it preserves short exact sequences. In a triangulated context an exact functor includes a specified compatibility with translation and preserves distinguished triangles. The ambient category determines which meaning is intended.2

    Section 0.2 Degrees and signs

    Complexes have cohomological grading: \(d_X^n:X^n\to X^{n+1}\text{.}\) Their translations satisfy
    \begin{equation*} X[r]^n=X^{n+r},\qquad d_{X[r]}^n=(-1)^r d_X^{n+r}. \end{equation*}
    Thus \(H^n(X[r])=H^{n+r}(X)\text{.}\) An object \(A\) placed in degree \(n\) is \(A[-n]\text{.}\) A homologically graded complex can be reindexed by \(X^n=C_{-n}\text{;}\) differential and tensor signs must be translated with this convention.
    For a chain map \(f:X\to Y\text{,}\) use
    \begin{equation*} \Cone(f)^n=Y^n\oplus X^{n+1},\qquad d(y,x)=(d_Yy+f(x),-d_Xx). \end{equation*}
    For a homogeneous map \(u\) of degree \(r\text{,}\) the differential on the Hom complex is \(d(u)=d_Yu-(-1)^r u d_X\text{.}\) The tensor differential is \(d(x\otimes y)=dx\otimes y+(-1)^{|x|}x\otimes dy\text{.}\) The symmetry sends \(x\otimes y\) to \((-1)^{|x||y|}y\otimes x\text{.}\)
    Tensor complexes use direct sums on diagonals. Hom complexes use products. For an unbounded double complex these totalizations can differ, and an exactness assertion about one does not automatically apply to the other. These distinctions are treated in Chapter 4 and Chapter 7.
    The symbols \(\tau_{\leq n}\) and \(\tau_{\geq n}\) denote the truncations that preserve the retained cohomology; \(\sigma_{\leq n}\) and \(\sigma_{\geq n}\) denote brutal truncations. All four are compared in Section 4.4. In \(\D^+(\mathcal A)\) cohomology vanishes in sufficiently negative degrees; in \(\D^-(\mathcal A)\) it vanishes in sufficiently positive degrees. The bounded derived category is \(\D^b(\mathcal A)\text{.}\)

    Section 0.3 Homology, cohomology, and dual terminology

    We use complex for a cochain complex unless another grading is specified, and call its objects \(H^n\) its cohomology. We also use the standard expressions chain map, chain homotopy, and homotopy category for these complexes. Replacing every occurrence of chain by cochain would add no mathematical structure. The phrase homological algebra names the subject and includes both grading conventions.
    For a homologically graded complex \((C_n,\partial_n:C_n\to C_{n-1})\text{,}\) our reindexing is \(X^n=C_{-n}\) and \(d_X^n=\partial_{-n}\text{.}\) It gives
    \begin{equation*} H^n(X)=H_{-n}(C). \end{equation*}
    If homological translation is written \(C[r]_n=C_{n-r}\text{,}\) its differential is \((-1)^r\partial\text{;}\) it corresponds exactly to \(X[r]\text{.}\) A complex bounded below in the homological indices is bounded above after this reindexing. For example, the usual projective resolution \(\cdots\to P_1\to P_0\to A\to0\) has \(P_i\) in cochain degree \(-i\text{.}\) Statements involving \(+\text{,}\) \(-\text{,}\) or first-quadrant diagrams must therefore be translated together with the indices.
    The object \(\ker d/\im d\) is defined by the same kernel–image construction in both conventions. Calling it homology or cohomology does not by itself change covariance: a morphism of cochain complexes induces a map of cohomology in the same direction. Contravariance can instead come from a functor used to construct the complex, such as the first argument of Hom. Thus neither an upper index nor the prefix co- alone determines the variance of a functor.
    An augmented projective resolution is represented here by \(P\to A[0]\) with \(P\) in nonpositive degrees; a coaugmented injective resolution is \(A[0]\to I\) with \(I\) in nonnegative degrees. Some authors call the latter a coresolution, reserving resolution for an augmentation. We use projective resolution and injective resolution and always specify the arrow. This is separate from the choice of chain or cochain indices.3
    For a right exact additive functor and a left exact additive functor, respectively, our conventions give \(L_nF(A)=H^{-n}(\mathbf LF(A))\) and \(R^nG(A)=H^n(\mathbf RG(A))\) whenever these constructions exist and agree with classical resolutions. In particular,
    \begin{equation*} \Tor_n^R(M,N)=H^{-n}(M\Ltensor_RN),\qquad \Ext_R^n(M,N)=H^n\RHom_R(M,N). \end{equation*}
    Ext is contravariant in its first argument and covariant in its second; Tor is covariant in both. We retain their established names and indices rather than renaming one of them according to its cochain realization. For general complexes, positive and negative derived degrees may both occur. A cohomological spectral sequence has differential of bidegree \((r,1-r)\text{;}\) simultaneous negation of both indices converts it to the homological bidegree \((-r,r-1)\text{.}\)
    Other occurrences of co- describe categorical duality, not a grading convention. Limits and colimits, kernels and cokernels, products and coproducts are related by passing to the opposite category. A monad has multiplication \(T^2\to T\text{;}\) a comonad has comultiplication \(T\to T^2\text{,}\) with the correspondingly reversed unit and associativity laws. The dual of a projective object is called injective, rather than coprojective. These established names do not follow a uniform spelling rule. We specify the arrows or universal property whenever the distinction is used. Ordinary monads and comonads are treated in the category theory notes.
    We do not use cohomotopy to mean a homotopy between cochain maps, cocomplex as the default name for a cochain complex, or coexact as a replacement for exact. The word exact retains the kernel–image condition in an abelian category, and its triangulated meaning is specified separately. At later occurrences, a footnote records a different convention only when it affects interpretation of a statement or comparison with a source.

    Section 0.4 Reading and reference conventions

    The first chapters establish abelian and closed monoidal structures, complexes, classical derived functors, and spectral sequences. The derived chapters compare these constructions with their universal formulations and distinguish bounded arguments from unbounded ones. The later chapters add \(t\)-structures, representability, recollement, and duality. The last chapter specifies an indexed six-operation framework and derives consequences from its stated axioms.
    For a general abelian category, enough injectives or projectives is always a hypothesis when needed. Grothendieck categories supply a broad setting for K-injective resolutions. Flatness additionally refers to a specified tensor product. Compact generation, monoidal structure, and a six-operation system are separate hypotheses; none is silently included in the word Grothendieck.
    The supplement Appendix A explains exact structures and quasi-abelian categories. It distinguishes admissible or strict exact sequences from the unrestricted abelian theory. The main chapters continue to state their abelian hypotheses; the supplement does not implicitly extend every theorem to that wider setting.
    The organizing references are [LiII, Chapters 1–5 and Appendices A–B], [GM03, Chapters II–IV], and [KS06, Chapters 8–15]. Classical calculations and spectral sequences are compared with [Wei94, Chapters 1–5 and 10]. The unbounded constructions are also compared with [Yek19, Chapters 7–12]. References to a chapter give a thematic source; a quoted existence theorem is accompanied by a more specific location or stable tag.
    The abstract operations use [FHM03], [Lip09, Sections 3.3–3.7], and selected general constructions from [CD19]. The frameworks compared in [Gal21] have different hypotheses and levels of structure. We specify the version used here rather than treating six-operation formalism as a uniquely fixed definition.
    Proofs are retained when they explain a construction or a dependence on hypotheses. Technical general existence theorems may be cited with their precise scope. Footnotes compare terminology, conventions, and variants. A reader may omit these comparisons while retaining the assumptions and statements in the main text.
