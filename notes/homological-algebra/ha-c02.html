    Chapter 2 Grothendieck categories and abelian localization

    An abelian category provides finite exact constructions. Infinite resolutions and their comparison require further existence and exactness assumptions. Grothendieck categories give one common set of hypotheses: they have arbitrary small colimits, exact filtered colimits, and a generator. Abelian localization explains how such categories can be obtained from module categories while retaining an exact quotient functor. The basic references are [LiII, §2.10 and Appendix A] and [KS06, Chapters 8–9].

    Section 2.1 Infinite exactness and generators

    Definition 2.1 Grothendieck’s conditions.

    Let \(\mathcal A\) be an abelian category. All indexing sets and indexing categories in the following conditions are small.
    1. AB3 means that all coproducts exist. AB4 means AB3 and exactness of coproducts. AB5 means AB3 and exactness of filtered colimits.
    2. AB3* means that all products exist. AB4* means AB3* and exactness of products.
    3. An object \(G\) is a generator if \(\Hom_{\mathcal A}(G,-)\) is faithful. A Grothendieck category is an abelian category satisfying AB5 and having a generator.
    In an additive category with cokernels, coproducts give all small colimits: a colimit of \(X:I\to\mathcal A\) is the cokernel of the difference of the two maps from \(\bigoplus_{u:i\to j}X_i\) to \(\bigoplus_iX_i\text{.}\) Thus “AB5 with a generator” agrees with the convention using cocompleteness in the chapter introduction. See [Stacks, Tag 079B].1

    Proposition 2.2.

    Suppose \(\mathcal A\) has coproducts and \(G\in\mathcal A\text{.}\) The following conditions are equivalent: \(G\) is a generator; every object is a quotient of a coproduct of copies of \(G\text{;}\) for every proper subobject \(N\subsetneq X\text{,}\) some morphism \(G\to X\) does not factor through \(N\text{.}\) If these conditions hold, \(\mathcal A\) is well-powered: the subobjects of each object form a small set up to isomorphism over that object.

    Proof.
    Consider the evaluation map \(e_X:\bigoplus_{u\in\Hom(G,X)}G\to X\text{.}\) If \(G\) is a generator and \(q:X\to\coker e_X\text{,}\) then \(qu=0\) for every \(u:G\to X\text{,}\) so faithfulness gives \(q=0\text{.}\) Thus \(e_X\) is epic. If all objects are quotients of such coproducts and \(f\ne g:X\to Y\text{,}\) some component of a covering coproduct distinguishes them. This proves faithfulness. The proper subobject criterion follows by applying the same argument to its inclusion and quotient.
    A subobject \(N\subseteq X\) is determined by the subset of \(\Hom(G,X)\) consisting of maps factoring through \(N\text{:}\) the image of their evaluation map is \(N\text{.}\) Subobjects therefore inject into a power set of a small set.

    Proposition 2.3.

    AB5 implies AB4. If \((X_i)\) is a filtered diagram in an AB5 category and \(N\subseteq X=\colim_iX_i\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} N\simeq\colim_i(X_i\times_XN). \end{equation*}
    In particular, a directed union of subobjects is a subobject, and taking that union commutes with intersection with a fixed subobject.

    Proof.
    A coproduct is the filtered colimit of its finite partial biproducts. Finite biproducts are exact, so AB5 proves AB4. For the second assertion, apply exactness of the filtered colimit to the kernels defining \(X_i\times_XN\) inside \(X_i\oplus N\text{.}\) The colimit of the constant diagram \(N\) is \(N\text{,}\) since a filtered category is nonempty and connected. The resulting kernel is the pullback of \(\id_X\) along \(N\to X\text{,}\) hence is \(N\text{.}\)
    The generator need not be projective, small, or finitely presentable. Those are additional requirements on its Hom functor. A small family of generators can be replaced by its coproduct, but this replacement does not make the coproduct finitely presentable.
    Coproducts are always right exact when they exist, because they are colimits. Products are always left exact when they exist, because they are limits. Thus AB4 asks that coproducts preserve monomorphisms, and AB4* asks that products preserve epimorphisms. AB5 asks that filtered colimits preserve monomorphisms; it then preserves kernels and all finite limits by additivity. An arbitrary colimit functor need not be left exact. Even a coequalizer can destroy injectivity.

    Theorem 2.4.

    Every Grothendieck category has all small limits and enough injectives: every object admits a monomorphism into an injective object. These assertions require no monoidal structure. For unbounded complexes, the stronger existence of K-injective resolutions is established in Chapter 10.
    We use this existence theorem from [LiII, §2.10]; the construction through accessible objects is given in [KS06, Chapter 9]. Injective means that maps into the object extend across monomorphisms, as defined in Chapter 5. These hypotheses provide no corresponding general theorem for projective resolutions.

    Proposition 2.5.

    A Grothendieck category has an injective cogenerator \(E\text{:}\) the contravariant functor \(\Hom(-,E)\) is faithful. Every object has a natural monomorphism
    \begin{equation*} X\longrightarrow\prod_{u\in\Hom(X,E)}E \end{equation*}
    whose components are the maps \(u\text{.}\) A product of injective objects is injective.

    Proof.
    Choose a generator \(G\text{.}\) Its quotients form a small set up to isomorphism by well-poweredness. For each quotient \(G/K\text{,}\) choose a monomorphism to an injective object \(E_K\text{,}\) and let \(E=\prod_KE_K\text{.}\) Products of injectives are injective: a map to the product extends across a monomorphism by extending each component. To detect a nonzero morphism \(f:X\to Y\text{,}\) choose a nonzero map \(G\to\im f\text{.}\) Its image is a nonzero quotient \(G/K\) inside \(Y\text{.}\) Injectivity extends its chosen map to \(E_K\) across this inclusion, giving \(Y\to E_K\) nonzero on \(\im f\text{.}\) The product \(E\) therefore detects \(f\text{.}\) The kernel of the displayed evaluation map must be zero by this detection property.
    This proof uses enough injectives, all products, and a small collection of quotients of a generator. It explains the cogenerator conclusion of [LiII, Corollary 2.10.15]. The evaluation construction is functorial, with the map between products given by precomposition on the indexing sets. That functor need not be additive. A functorial embedding into injectives as objects and a functorial K-injective replacement of unbounded complexes are different existence results.

    Section 2.2 Finite generation and finite presentation

    The generator controls all objects through quotients of sums. Finiteness asks how much of such a sum, or of a filtered approximation, is needed for one map. The relevant notions are relative to the ambient category, rather than to a selected description of its objects.

    Definition 2.6 Finite objects and local finiteness conditions.

    Let \(\mathcal A\) be Grothendieck.
    1. An object \(X\) is finitely generated if, whenever it is the directed union of subobjects \(X_i\text{,}\) one of those subobjects is \(X\text{.}\)
    2. It is finitely presentable if \(\Hom(X,-)\) commutes with all small filtered colimits. Thus the canonical map \(\colim_i\Hom(X,Y_i)\to\Hom(X,\colim_iY_i)\) must be bijective for every filtered diagram.
    3. The category is locally finitely generated if it has a small generating family of finitely generated objects. It is locally finitely presentable if a small family of finitely presentable objects supplies every object as a filtered colimit. It is locally coherent if it is locally finitely presentable and its full subcategory of finitely presentable objects is abelian with exact inclusion. It is locally noetherian if it has a small generating family of noetherian objects.
    For filtered diagrams of monomorphisms, the finite-generation condition says that a map from \(X\) into the union factors through one stage. Apply Proposition 2.3 to its image, or pull the union back to \(X\text{,}\) to obtain this factorization. The map between Hom colimits is then bijective, since transition monomorphisms detect equality. Finite presentation makes the same demand for filtered diagrams with arbitrary transition maps. It controls both the existence of a finite stage for a map and the relations identifying two such maps.2
    The finite-presentation condition is the case \(\kappa=\aleph_0\) of the presentability conventions in [LiII, Appendix A.2]; the diagrams are ordinary filtered diagrams. Larger regular cardinals provide different bounds. No fixed finite-presentation condition is part of the definition of a Grothendieck category.

    Proposition 2.7.

    Finitely presentable objects are finitely generated, and noetherian objects are finitely generated. Finitely generated objects are closed under quotients and extensions. Finitely presentable objects are closed under finite colimits and direct summands. In a locally finitely generated Grothendieck category, every object is a directed union of finitely generated subobjects.

    Proof.
    Apply preservation of filtered colimits to a union covering a finitely presentable object and to its identity map. A noetherian object has a maximal member in any directed family of subobjects; directedness makes that member contain the whole family. For an extension with finitely generated outer terms, a union covering the middle term eventually covers its quotient and eventually contains its subobject, so eventually equals the middle term. Quotients use inverse images of a covering union. Finite colimits become finite limits under Hom, and filtered colimits of sets commute with finite limits; this proves the finite-presentation assertion. Direct summands follow by taking retracts of the Hom comparison maps. For the final assertion, cover an object by a sum of the finitely generated generators. The images of its finite partial sums are finitely generated and have union the whole object.
    For modules, these definitions recover finite generation and finite presentation by generators and relations. Every module is a filtered colimit of finitely presented modules, so \(\Mod(R)\) is locally finitely presentable for every ring. It is locally noetherian exactly when \(R\) is left noetherian. It is locally coherent exactly when \(R\) is left coherent, meaning that every finitely generated left ideal is finitely presented, equivalently that kernels of maps between finitely presented left modules are finitely presented. A ring need not be coherent for its entire module category to be abelian; coherence concerns its smaller category of finitely presented modules.

    Example 2.8.

    Let \(R=k[x_1,x_2,\ldots]\) and \(I=(x_1,x_2,\ldots)\text{.}\) The cyclic module \(R/I\) is finitely generated but not finitely presented, because the kernel \(I\) of its presentation by one free generator is not finitely generated. The free module \(R\) is finitely presented but not noetherian. Thus neither “finitely generated” nor “finitely presented” may be replaced by “noetherian” over an arbitrary ring.
    A category described as locally finite requires a further convention. In the representation-theoretic terminology recalled in Chapter 3, a locally finite \(k\)-linear abelian category has finite-length objects and finite-dimensional Hom spaces. In some discussions of Grothendieck categories, local finiteness instead means that arbitrary objects are unions of finite-length subobjects. All vector spaces over \(k\) satisfy the latter condition and include objects of infinite length; finite-dimensional vector spaces satisfy the former and lack infinite coproducts. We will state the actual finiteness conditions when using either convention.

    Section 2.3 Examples and failures of duality

    Example 2.9.

    For any ring \(R\text{,}\) \(\Mod(R)\) is Grothendieck, with generator \(R\text{.}\) Filtered colimits are exact by the element criterion: an element vanishes in a filtered colimit precisely when it vanishes at some later stage. If \(I\) is a small category, \(\Fun(I,\Mod(R))\) is Grothendieck. Limits and colimits are computed pointwise. A generating family consists of the functors \(P_i(j)=\bigoplus_{I(i,j)}R\text{,}\) since \(\Hom(P_i,F)\simeq F(i)\) as abelian groups.
    Another construction starts with a small abelian category \(\mathcal B\text{.}\) Its category \(\operatorname{Ind}(\mathcal B)\) of formal filtered colimits is Grothendieck, and the canonical inclusion is fully faithful and exact. The objects of \(\mathcal B\) are precisely its finitely presentable objects, up to isomorphism. Here finitely presentable means that the Hom functor commutes with filtered colimits. See [KS06, §8.6] and [LiII, Appendix B.5]. This construction retains the original finite exact sequences while adding filtered colimits. It does not assert that an arbitrary small abelian category already has those colimits.
    The morphism formula explains the finite-presentation assertion. For filtered diagrams \((B_i)\) and \((C_j)\) in \(\mathcal B\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \Hom_{\operatorname{Ind}(\mathcal B)}\left(\underset{i}{\text{“}\colim\text{”}}B_i,\underset{j}{\text{“}\colim\text{”}}C_j\right)=\lim_i\colim_j\Hom_{\mathcal B}(B_i,C_j). \end{equation*}
    An object of \(\mathcal B\) therefore has Hom commuting with filtered colimits. Conversely, if an ind-object is finitely presentable, its identity factors through one of the objects in a filtered presentation, making it a retract of that object. Idempotents split in \(\mathcal B\text{,}\) so this retract belongs to \(\mathcal B\) up to isomorphism. The embedding and Grothendieck assertions are [LiII, Theorems B.5.1 and B.5.7]; the Hom formula is the defining calculation of Ind-categories.
    It follows that \(\operatorname{Ind}(\mathcal B)\) is locally coherent. For a finite-dimensional \(k\)-algebra \(A\text{,}\) every \(A\)-module is a union of finite-dimensional submodules, so the ind-category of finite-dimensional \(A\)-modules is equivalent to \(\Mod(A)\text{.}\) Taking \(\mathcal B\) to be a small equivalent category of finite abelian groups instead gives the category of torsion abelian groups: finitely generated torsion groups are finite. The original small category determines which finite objects are being completed.3

    Example 2.10.

    The category \(\mathcal T\) of torsion abelian groups is Grothendieck. Kernels, cokernels, coproducts, and filtered colimits are computed in \(\Ab\text{,}\) and \(\bigoplus_{n\geq1}\Z/n\Z\) is a generator. Products are the torsion subgroups of the products in \(\Ab\text{.}\) They are not exact. Fix a prime \(p\) and take the epimorphisms \(\Z/p^{n+1}\Z\to\Z/p\Z\) for \(n\geq1\text{.}\) The element with every coordinate equal to \(1\) belongs to the target product. Any lift has components of unbounded order, so does not belong to the torsion subgroup of the source product. Thus \(\mathcal T\) fails AB4*.
    In fact, \(\mathcal T\) has no nonzero projective object. Here is a direct argument, using the lifting definition from Chapter 5. Every torsion group is a quotient of a direct sum of finite cyclic groups. A projective \(P\) would therefore be a direct summand of such a sum. If \(x\in P\) is nonzero of order \(p^r\text{,}\) some coordinate projection, followed by projection to a primary cyclic factor, gives \(f:P\to\Z/p^n\Z\) with \(f(x)\ne0\text{.}\) Projectivity lifts \(f\) through \(\Z/p^{n+r}\Z\twoheadrightarrow\Z/p^n\Z\text{.}\) Every lift of the nonzero element \(f(x)\) has order greater than \(p^r\text{,}\) contradicting the order of \(x\text{.}\)
    This example separates three assertions: products exist, products are exact, and projectives are available. It also shows why the dual of a theorem about Grothendieck categories must be stated with dual hypotheses; it cannot be used in the same category merely by reversing arrows.

    Section 2.4 Serre quotients and exact localizations

    Definition 2.11 Serre and localizing subcategories.

    A Serre subcategory \(\mathcal S\subseteq\mathcal A\) is a full subcategory closed under isomorphisms, containing zero, and closed under subobjects, quotient objects, and extensions. When \(\mathcal A\) is Grothendieck, we call \(\mathcal S\) localizing if it is also closed under small coproducts.
    The kernel of an exact functor is Serre. Conversely, a Serre quotient makes precisely the objects of \(\mathcal S\) vanish. Its morphisms allow a domain to be restricted and a target to be divided by objects that vanish.4

    Theorem 2.12.

    Let \(\mathcal A\) be Grothendieck and \(\mathcal S\) Serre. There is an abelian category \(\mathcal A/\mathcal S\) and an exact functor \(Q:\mathcal A\to\mathcal A/\mathcal S\) with kernel \(\mathcal S\text{.}\) Precomposition with \(Q\) identifies exact functors out of the quotient, including their natural transformations, with exact functors out of \(\mathcal A\) annihilating \(\mathcal S\text{.}\) One may take the same objects and morphism groups
    \begin{equation*} \Hom_{\mathcal A/\mathcal S}(QX,QY)=\colim_{X',Y'}\Hom_{\mathcal A}(X',Y/Y'), \end{equation*}
    where \(X'\subseteq X\text{,}\) \(Y'\subseteq Y\text{,}\) and \(X/X',Y'\in\mathcal S\text{.}\) The transition maps restrict the domain and enlarge the quotient of the target.
    Well-poweredness makes this a small filtered colimit of groups: intersections refine domains and sums refine the subobjects divided out. Equivalently, \(Q\) inverts exactly the morphisms whose kernel and cokernel lie in \(\mathcal S\text{.}\) The fraction construction proves the theorem; see [Stacks, Tag 02MN]. Its universal property is understood up to natural isomorphism, independently of a chosen quotient model.
    The quotient is not generally a full subcategory of \(\mathcal A\text{.}\) It keeps the objects and changes their morphisms. For an actual morphism \(f\) in \(\mathcal A\text{,}\) exactness gives the tests
    \begin{equation*} Qf=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \im f\in\mathcal S,\qquad Qf\text{ invertible}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ker f,\coker f\in\mathcal S. \end{equation*}
    A representative \(X'\to Y/Y'\) of a quotient morphism is zero precisely when its image belongs to \(\mathcal S\text{.}\) These tests do not identify the quotient with the category of objects having no subobject in \(\mathcal S\text{.}\) Such torsion-free objects can still acquire additional morphisms.

    Theorem 2.13.

    For a Serre subcategory \(\mathcal S\) of a Grothendieck category, closure under coproducts is equivalent to the existence of a right adjoint \(S\) to \(Q\text{.}\) In that case \(\mathcal A/\mathcal S\) is Grothendieck, \(S\) is fully faithful and left exact, and the counit \(QS\to\id\) is an isomorphism. The kernel and cokernel of the unit \(X\to SQX\) belong to \(\mathcal S\text{.}\)
    The existence criterion is [Gab62, Chapter III, §4, Proposition 8]. Since a Serre subcategory is closed under cokernels, closure under coproducts is equivalent to closure under all small colimits, the condition used there. In sources defining “localizing” by existence of the section functor \(S\text{,}\) this theorem supplies the equivalence with our definition. Applying the exact functor \(Q\) to the unit proves its kernel and cokernel assertion. An exact left adjoint with fully faithful right adjoint is called an exact localization; left exactness of the section does not assert that the section preserves epimorphisms.

    Proposition 2.14.

    In the setting of Theorem 2.13, an object \(X\) is in the essential image of \(S\) if and only if every morphism from an object of \(\mathcal S\) to \(X\) is zero and every short exact sequence \(0\to X\to E\to T\to0\) with \(T\in\mathcal S\) splits.

    Proof.
    For \(X=SY\text{,}\) the vanishing follows by adjunction. Apply \(Q\) to an extension with inclusion \(j:SY\to E\text{.}\) The map \(Qj\) is an isomorphism, and its inverse corresponds by adjunction to a retraction \(E\to SY\text{.}\) Conversely, the kernel of \(X\to SQX\) belongs to \(\mathcal S\text{,}\) so vanishes by the first condition. Its cokernel is some \(T\in\mathcal S\text{.}\) The second condition splits that exact sequence. A summand \(T\subseteq SQX\) must vanish, since adjunction gives \(\Hom(T,SQX)=0\text{.}\) Thus the unit is an isomorphism.
    Each object has a largest subobject \(tX\) belonging to \(\mathcal S\text{:}\) take the sum of all such subobjects. This is a small sum by well-poweredness, and its image belongs to \(\mathcal S\text{.}\) The quotient \(X/tX\) has no nonzero subobject in \(\mathcal S\text{,}\) since any such subobject would lift to an extension of objects of \(\mathcal S\) inside \(X\text{.}\) The local object \(SQX\) satisfies the further splitting condition in the proposition. Removing torsion and making an object local are therefore distinct operations; the unit performs both, with kernel \(tX\text{.}\)

    Proposition 2.15.

    For a localizing subcategory \(\mathcal S\) of a Grothendieck category \(\mathcal A\text{:}\)
    1. The category \(\mathcal S\) is Grothendieck. Its inclusion is exact and preserves small colimits, and the torsion functor \(t:\mathcal A\to\mathcal S\) is its right adjoint. In particular, \(t\) is left exact.
    2. The quotient functor \(Q\) preserves small colimits. Its section \(S\) preserves small limits and takes injective objects of the quotient to injective objects of \(\mathcal A\text{.}\)
    3. The full subcategory of local objects is closed under limits and extensions. The kernel of a map of local objects is its ambient kernel. Its cokernel in that subcategory is obtained by applying \(SQ\) to its ambient cokernel.

    Proof.
    Colimits of objects of \(\mathcal S\) are formed using sums and cokernels in \(\mathcal A\text{,}\) so remain in \(\mathcal S\text{.}\) AB5 is inherited. The quotients of a generator \(G\) that belong to \(\mathcal S\) form a small generating family there: the image of every map \(G\to T\) with \(T\in\mathcal S\) is one of these quotients, and their sum covers \(T\text{.}\) Every map from a torsion object to \(X\) factors through \(tX\text{,}\) which proves the adjunction. The second assertion follows from the adjunctions; preservation of injectives uses the exactness of \(Q\text{.}\) Local objects are closed under limits because they are the objects orthogonal to the morphisms inverted by \(Q\text{.}\) They are closed under extensions by the Hom and splitting criterion, equivalently the vanishing of \(\Hom(T,-)\) and \(\Ext^1(T,-)\) for \(T\in\mathcal S\text{.}\) Finally transport kernels and cokernels from the abelian quotient along its fully faithful section. Left exactness of \(S\) gives the ambient kernel description.
    The Ext formulation uses the extension interpretation developed in Chapter 6. The main local-object criterion above uses only short exact sequences. The section gives an abelian category of local objects, but its cokernels generally differ from the ambient cokernels. Calling its inclusion left exact records precisely this difference.5

    Example 2.16.

    In \(\Ab\text{,}\) let \(\mathcal S\) be all torsion groups. The exact functor \(\Q\otimes_{\Z}-\) has kernel \(\mathcal S\) and fully faithful right adjoint given by restriction of scalars from \(\Q\)-vector spaces. Thus \(\Ab/\mathcal S\simeq\Mod(\Q)\text{.}\) In particular, \(\Z\xrightarrow{n}\Z\) becomes invertible for each nonzero \(n\text{.}\) The quotient retains rational information, whereas the torsion category in the preceding example retains torsion information.
    For this example the torsion-free group \(\Z\) is not local: its unit is \(\Z\to\Q\text{.}\) Local objects are uniquely divisible groups, equivalently \(\Q\)-vector spaces. The torsion functor is not right exact; applying it to \(0\to\Z\to\Q\to\Q/\Z\to0\) produces \(0\to0\to0\to\Q/\Z\text{.}\) More generally, localization of modules at a multiplicative subset of a commutative ring is an exact quotient with fully faithful restriction as section. In that case the local objects are the modules on which all selected denominators act invertibly.

    Section 2.5 Realization as a quotient of modules

    Theorem 2.17.

    Let \(G\) be a generator of a Grothendieck category \(\mathcal A\text{,}\) and let \(R=\End_{\mathcal A}(G)^\op\text{.}\) The functor
    \begin{equation*} H=\Hom_{\mathcal A}(G,-):\mathcal A\to\Mod(R) \end{equation*}
    is fully faithful and has an exact left adjoint \(L\text{.}\) Here an endomorphism \(e\text{,}\) regarded as an element of \(R\text{,}\) acts on \(u:G\to X\) by \(u\circ e\text{.}\) Consequently \(\mathcal A\simeq\Mod(R)/\ker L\text{.}\)
    We use the Gabriel–Popescu theorem in [LiII, Appendix A.3]; a proof is [Stacks, Tag 0F5U]. The opposite ring translates the right modules in that source into our left-module convention. The adjoint can be described without a projective generator: replace a free presentation \(R^{(J)}\to R^{(I)}\to M\to0\) by the corresponding map \(G^{(J)}\to G^{(I)}\text{,}\) and let \(L(M)\) be its cokernel. The adjunction shows independence of the presentation. Exactness of this adjoint and full faithfulness of \(H\) are the substantive assertions of the theorem.
    The embedding \(H\) is generally only left exact: \(G\) is not assumed projective. Hence an abelian category realized inside modules by this theorem need not inherit module cokernels through that embedding.6 The quotient description and an exact full embedding are different assertions. This distinction will remain necessary when exact localization is compared with localization of derived categories in Chapter 15.
    If \(G\) is projective and finitely presentable, its Hom functor is exact and preserves coproducts; a free-presentation argument then makes it an equivalence with modules. The precise recognition theorem is [LiII, Theorem 7.8.4]. The additional assumptions are substantive. Even in \(\Ab\text{,}\) the generator \(\Z\oplus\Z/n\Z\) with \(n>1\) is not projective: its torsion projection cannot lift through \(\Z\twoheadrightarrow\Z/n\Z\text{.}\) The associated Gabriel–Popescu section is therefore not exact. Choosing a generator does not choose an exact embedding.
    The Freyd–Mitchell theorem of Theorem 1.21 and Gabriel–Popescu therefore answer different questions. The former embeds a small abelian category exactly and fully into modules and supports finite diagram arguments. The latter describes a Grothendieck category as an exact quotient of modules and supplies its section, which need not be exact. Neither theorem transports arbitrary monoidal structure or asserts that injective and projective objects correspond under the displayed embedding.
