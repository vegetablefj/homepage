    Chapter 9 Derived categories

    The derived category makes quasi-isomorphisms invertible while retaining extension classes as morphisms. Its construction is a Verdier quotient of the homotopy category. The distinction between homotopy equivalence and quasi-isomorphism is precisely the distinction between the two categories. Main references are [GM03, Chapter III], [KS06, Chapter 13], and [LiII, Sections 4.4–4.6].

    Section 9.1 Inverting quasi-isomorphisms

    Definition 9.1 Derived categories and boundedness.

    Let \(\mathcal A\) be abelian. Write \(\operatorname{Ac}(\mathcal A)\) for the strictly full subcategory of acyclic complexes in \(\K(\mathcal A)\text{.}\) The derived category is
    \begin{equation*} \D(\mathcal A)=\K(\mathcal A)/\operatorname{Ac}(\mathcal A). \end{equation*}
    Its full subcategories \(\D^+(\mathcal A)\text{,}\) \(\D^-(\mathcal A)\text{,}\) and \(\D^b(\mathcal A)\) consist respectively of objects with cohomology bounded below, above, and on both sides.

    Proposition 9.2.

    The acyclic complexes form a thick triangulated subcategory. The maps inverted by the quotient are exactly the quasi-isomorphisms. Cohomology descends to \(\D(\mathcal A)\text{,}\) and a morphism of \(\D(\mathcal A)\) is invertible if and only if it induces isomorphisms on every cohomology object.

    Proof.
    The cone long exact sequence proves closure under cones and translations; cohomology preserves finite direct sums, so it also proves closure under summands. The same sequence says that a chain map is a quasi-isomorphism exactly when its cone is acyclic. The quotient theorem applies. For an arbitrary derived morphism, complete it to a distinguished triangle. Its cone is zero exactly when all its cohomology objects vanish, which gives the last assertion.
    The phrase quasi-isomorphic complexes may mean that there is a single quasi-isomorphism between them, or that they are joined by a zigzag of quasi-isomorphisms. We use the latter meaning when no arrow is displayed. It is equivalent to their being isomorphic in the derived category.1
    For a large \(\mathcal A\text{,}\) local smallness of \(\D(\mathcal A)\) is not automatic. If every target has a K-injective replacement, derived morphisms can be calculated in the locally small homotopy category. This applies to Grothendieck categories by Chapter 10. The size issue and the convention of working first at a larger universe are discussed in [Stacks, Tag 05RR].

    Section 9.2 Bounded models and short exact sequences

    Proposition 9.3.

    1. An object with cohomology zero below \(a\) has a bounded below representative, and one with cohomology zero above \(b\) has a bounded above representative. If both conditions hold it has a bounded representative.
    2. Localization of \(\K^+(\mathcal A)\text{,}\) \(\K^-(\mathcal A)\text{,}\) or \(\K^b(\mathcal A)\) at quasi-isomorphisms identifies with the corresponding full subcategory of \(\D(\mathcal A)\text{.}\)
    3. Every short exact sequence of complexes \(0\to X\xrightarrow{i}Y\to Z\to0\) defines a distinguished triangle \(X\to Y\to Z\to X[1]\) in \(\D(\mathcal A)\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    For the first assertion use smart truncations. When both bounds are present, the zigzag from \(X\) to \(\tau_{\leq b}\tau_{\geq a}X\) consists of quasi-isomorphisms. To prove full faithfulness in the second assertion, truncate the middle objects of roofs and common refinements outside the cohomological bounds; the fraction equivalence is preserved. This standard fraction argument is given in [Stacks, Tag 05RR]. For the last assertion the map \(\Cone(i)\to Z\) sending \((y,x)\) to the image of \(y\) is a quasi-isomorphism: its kernel is the cone of \(\id_X\text{.}\) Replace the cone by \(Z\) in its triangle.
    The cohomological bounds in the definition concern the object of the derived category, not each chosen complex representing it. A bounded object can have unbounded injective or projective resolutions. We also distinguish \(\D^b(\mathcal B)\) from the full subcategory of \(\D^b(\mathcal A)\) whose cohomology lies in an abelian subcategory \(\mathcal B\text{:}\) their equivalence requires additional hypotheses.

    Proposition 9.4.

    The functor \(\mathcal A\to\D(\mathcal A)\) placing objects in degree zero is fully faithful. For \(A,B\in\mathcal A\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \Hom_{\D(\mathcal A)}(A,B[n])=0\quad(n<0). \end{equation*}
    If \(\mathcal A\) has enough injectives or enough projectives, the nonnegative groups are naturally \(\Ext^n_{\mathcal A}(A,B)\text{,}\) with composition corresponding to Yoneda product.

    Proof.
    For a roof from \(A\) to \(B\text{,}\) truncate its middle complex above degree zero. The target map then factors through its degree-zero cohomology; the quasi-isomorphism to \(A\) identifies that cohomology with \(A\text{.}\) This gives the full faithfulness assertion and its uniqueness by the roof equivalence. The same truncation gives the negative-degree vanishing. Under an injective hypothesis choose \(B\to I\) with \(I\) bounded below and injective. Then
    \begin{equation*} \Hom_{\D(\mathcal A)}(A,B[n])=H^n\underline{\Hom}^{\bullet}(A,I), \end{equation*}
    which is the classical definition of Ext. The comparison with extensions identifies the connecting morphism of a short exact sequence with its degree-one class; splicing and composition give the general product. The projective proof is dual. The replacement calculation is proved in Chapter 10; the quotient and truncation arguments do not depend on these existence hypotheses.

    Section 9.3 What derived morphisms retain

    Example 9.5.

    The exact complex \(0\to\Z\xrightarrow{2}\Z\to\Z/2\Z\to0\) is zero in \(\D(\Ab)\) but is not contractible. A contracting homotopy would split the surjection \(\Z\to\Z/2\Z\text{,}\) which has no section. Thus a quasi-isomorphism need not be a homotopy equivalence.

    Example 9.6.

    The short exact sequence \(0\to\Z\xrightarrow{2}\Z\to\Z/2\Z\to0\) gives a nonzero morphism \(\Z/2\Z\to\Z[1]\text{.}\) Its class generates \(\Ext^1_{\Z}(\Z/2\Z,\Z)\simeq\Z/2\Z\text{.}\) No chain map from the stalk complex \(\Z/2\Z\) to the stalk complex \(\Z[1]\) represents this nonzero morphism: such a chain map is zero. A roof or a resolution supplies the additional morphism.
    Over a field, every complex is isomorphic in the derived category to its graded cohomology with zero differential. Choose complements to boundaries inside cycles and to cycles inside each term; the remaining pieces form contractible two-term complexes. The splitting generally depends on choices. Over a general ring, a complex need not split into its cohomology objects: connecting maps between truncations retain extension data.
    For a ring \(R\text{,}\) the homotopy category of bounded complexes of finitely generated projective modules embeds fully faithfully in \(\D(R)\text{.}\) Its essential image is the perfect complexes. A bounded complex of finitely generated modules need not be perfect: over \(k[\epsilon]/(\epsilon^2)\text{,}\) the residue field has an infinite projective resolution with repeated multiplication by \(\epsilon\text{.}\) These distinctions will also separate boundedness from compactness.

    Section 9.4 Morphism calculations and resolution models

    The localization describes a morphism by a roof, but a roof is not always the best way to calculate it. A resolution replaces one variable by an object orthogonal to every acyclic complex. The orthogonal calculation of Chapter 8 then removes the denominators.

    Proposition 9.7.

    Assume the indicated replacements exist.
    1. If \(Y\to I\) is a quasi-isomorphism with K-injective target, then \(\Hom_{\D(\mathcal A)}(X,Y[n])\simeq\Hom_{\K(\mathcal A)}(X,I[n])\) for every \(X,n\text{.}\)
    2. If \(P\to X\) is a quasi-isomorphism with K-projective source, then \(\Hom_{\D(\mathcal A)}(X,Y[n])\simeq\Hom_{\K(\mathcal A)}(P,Y[n])\) for every \(Y,n\text{.}\)
    3. If \(\mathcal A\) has enough injectives, then \(\K^+(\operatorname{Inj}\mathcal A)\to\D^+(\mathcal A)\) is an equivalence. If it has enough projectives, then \(\K^-(\operatorname{Proj}\mathcal A)\to\D^-(\mathcal A)\) is an equivalence.

    Proof.
    The first two statements are the orthogonal calculation; their definitions and existence hypotheses are developed in Chapter 10. For the bounded below case, resolve a bounded below representative by a bounded below complex of injectives. Such complexes are K-injective, so the displayed functor is fully faithful as well as essentially surjective. The projective case is dual. This proof indicates the dependency: the construction of \(\D\) does not require resolutions, while a resolution model requires the relevant existence theorem.
    The numerator of a morphism into \(I\) is an actual chain map into \(I\text{,}\) up to chain homotopy. A morphism into the original \(Y\) need not be an actual chain map into \(Y\text{.}\) Similarly, a morphism between two chosen injective models lifts uniquely only as a homotopy class. This supplies a functor on homotopy categories; it is not a strictly unique map of complexes.
    Bounded cohomology does not mean a bounded injective resolution exists. An object of \(\D^b(\mathcal A)\) may be represented by a complex of injectives extending indefinitely to the right. Thus \(\K^b(\operatorname{Inj}\mathcal A)\) need not model all of \(\D^b(\mathcal A)\text{.}\) A finite injective dimension hypothesis would have to be supplied. These boundedness distinctions also matter for total derived functors.

    Proposition 9.8.

    If \(H^i(X)=0\) for \(i>a\) and \(H^i(Y)=0\) for \(i<b\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \Hom_{\D(\mathcal A)}(X,Y[n])=0\quad\text{for }n<b-a. \end{equation*}
    At the boundary there is a natural isomorphism
    \begin{equation*} \Hom_{\D(\mathcal A)}(X,Y[b-a])\simeq\Hom_{\mathcal A}(H^aX,H^bY). \end{equation*}
    No injective or projective hypothesis is required.

    Proof.
    Choose a representative of \(Y\) vanishing below \(b\text{.}\) For a roof from \(X\) to \(Y[n]\text{,}\) truncate its middle complex above \(a\text{;}\) the denominator remains a quasi-isomorphism. If \(n<b-a\text{,}\) the numerator has disjoint source and target degree supports, so is zero. For the boundary case, the truncation triangles remove cohomology of \(X\) below \(a\) and of \(Y\) above \(b\text{;}\) the just-proved vanishing shows that these removals do not change Hom. The remaining calculation is full faithfulness of stalk complexes.
    This is an orthogonality statement about cohomological ranges. It does not bound positive Ext groups between two stalk complexes. Such a bound is a homological-dimension assertion, which is additional information.

    Section 9.5 Cohomology layers and extension data

    Proposition 9.9.

    Smart truncations define natural triangles
    \begin{equation*} \tau_{\leq n}X\longrightarrow X\longrightarrow\tau_{\geq n+1}X\longrightarrow(\tau_{\leq n}X)[1] \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \tau_{\leq n-1}X\longrightarrow\tau_{\leq n}X\longrightarrow H^n(X)[-n]\longrightarrow(\tau_{\leq n-1}X)[1]. \end{equation*}
    If \(X\) has cohomology in \([a,b]\text{,}\) these triangles give a finite construction of \(X\) by extensions of its cohomology objects in their prescribed degrees.

    Proof.
    The cone of \(\tau_{\leq n}X\to X\) maps by a quasi-isomorphism to \(\tau_{\geq n+1}X\text{;}\) this follows either by the cone cohomology sequence or by the explicit smart-truncation complexes. The second triangle follows by comparing successive upper truncations: the cone has only \(H^n(X)\) in degree \(n\) and hence is quasi-isomorphic to its stalk there. These triangles are natural in the derived category by the upper/lower range orthogonality. Iteration between \(a\) and \(b\) gives the finite construction.
    The successive objects are called the cohomology layers, or the Postnikov layers, of \(X\text{.}\) Their connecting maps record information lost by the graded object \(H^*(X)\text{.}\)2

    Example 9.10.

    Suppose that \(X\) has just \(H^0(X)=A\) and \(H^1(X)=B\text{.}\) Its truncation triangle has the form
    \begin{equation*} A\longrightarrow X\longrightarrow B[-1]\xrightarrow{\kappa}A[1]. \end{equation*}
    Thus the interaction between the two cohomology objects is measured by \(\kappa\in\Hom_{\D(\mathcal A)}(B,A[2])\text{.}\) Conversely, any such morphism gives a triangle whose middle object has precisely these two cohomology objects. The split-triangle criterion says that \(\kappa=0\) precisely when this triangle splits, giving \(X\simeq A\oplus B[-1]\text{.}\) Nonzero degree-two extension classes therefore give complexes which are not isomorphic to their graded cohomology.
    For an unbounded complex there is an infinite truncation tower. The finite-layer argument by itself does not identify the complex with a homotopy limit or homotopy colimit of that tower. Such a conclusion requires the relevant limits, exactness, and convergence conditions. In particular, exact filtered colimits in a Grothendieck category give no automatic exactness of infinite products.

    Section 9.6 Yoneda extensions and full subcategories

    Proposition 9.11.

    For every abelian category \(\mathcal A\) and \(n\geq1\text{,}\) Yoneda \(n\)-extensions of \(B\) by \(A\) correspond naturally to morphisms \(B\to A[n]\) in \(\D(\mathcal A)\text{.}\) Equivalence of extensions corresponds to equality of morphisms, and splicing corresponds to shifted composition. This assertion is made at a universe scale where the relevant extension classes or derived morphisms are sets.

    Proof.
    For an exact sequence \(0\to A\to E_{n-1}\to\cdots\to E_0\to B\to0\text{,}\) form the complex with \(A\) in degree \(-n\text{,}\) \(E_0\) in degree zero, and no final term \(B\text{.}\) Its augmentation to \(B\) is a quasi-isomorphism, and its projection to \(A[n]\) gives a roof representing the extension. Conversely, a roof from \(B\) to \(A[n]\) can be truncated to these degrees; kernels and cokernels at the ends turn it into an exact sequence of this form. The fraction-equivalence relation gives precisely Yoneda equivalence. This construction, including the endpoint modification, is proved in [Stacks, Tag 06XP].
    Thus Ext can be defined by derived morphisms or by Yoneda extensions even when there are not enough injectives or projectives. Resolutions provide a calculation of these groups under additional hypotheses. The comparison in the earlier stalk-complex proposition concerns that calculation; its hypotheses do not restrict the Yoneda statement.

    Proposition 9.12.

    Let \(\mathcal B\subset\mathcal A\) be a full abelian subcategory whose inclusion is exact and whose image is a Serre subcategory. Assume that for all \(B,C\in\mathcal B\) and \(n\geq0\) the comparison \(\Ext^n_{\mathcal B}(B,C)\to\Ext^n_{\mathcal A}(B,C)\) is an isomorphism, using Yoneda Ext. Then
    \begin{equation*} \D^b(\mathcal B)\longrightarrow\D^b(\mathcal A) \end{equation*}
    is fully faithful, with essential image the objects whose cohomology lies in \(\mathcal B\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    The inclusion preserves quasi-isomorphisms and gives the displayed functor. By the Yoneda comparison it is fully faithful on all shifts of stalk objects. Use the finite cohomology-layer construction and the five lemma, first in one variable and then in the other, to obtain full faithfulness on bounded complexes. Its essential image is therefore triangulated: a map between two objects in the image lifts to \(\D^b(\mathcal B)\text{,}\) and its cone lifts as well. Every bounded object with cohomology in \(\mathcal B\) is an iterated extension of such stalks, proving essential surjectivity onto the asserted subcategory. The Serre condition ensures that this cohomological condition is stable under triangles.
    The Ext comparison is a genuine hypothesis. Exactness and full faithfulness of the original abelian inclusion only control degree zero. The criterion explains what must be checked before replacing the phrase derived complexes with cohomology in \(\mathcal B\) by the derived category of \(\mathcal B\).
