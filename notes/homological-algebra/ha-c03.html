    Chapter 3 Closed monoidal structures

    Tensor products and internal Hom objects belong to the structure of a category. Exactness and the existence of resolutions are additional questions. We first describe the ordinary structures that will be derived in Chapter 12. Our conventions follow [LiII, §§7.1–7.3 and 9.1] and [KS06, Chapter 4]. The term “tensor category” has stronger meanings in representation theory, which we distinguish below.

    Section 3.1 Monoidal data and linearity

    Definition 3.1 Monoidal and symmetric monoidal categories.

    1. A monoidal category has a bifunctor \(\otimes:\mathcal C\times\mathcal C\to\mathcal C\text{,}\) a unit object \(\mathbf1\text{,}\) and natural isomorphisms \(a_{X,Y,Z}:(X\otimes Y)\otimes Z\to X\otimes(Y\otimes Z)\text{,}\) \(\lambda_X:\mathbf1\otimes X\to X\text{,}\) and \(\rho_X:X\otimes\mathbf1\to X\text{.}\) They satisfy the pentagon and triangle identities.
    2. A braiding is a natural family of isomorphisms \(c_{X,Y}:X\otimes Y\to Y\otimes X\) satisfying the two hexagon identities. A symmetry is a braiding satisfying \(c_{Y,X}c_{X,Y}=\id\text{.}\) The corresponding structures are called braided monoidal and symmetric monoidal.
    3. If \(\mathcal C\) is additive, the tensor product is biadditive when it is additive separately in each variable. Over a commutative ring \(k\text{,}\) a \(k\)-linear monoidal category has \(k\)-module morphism groups, bilinear composition, and a tensor product that is \(k\)-linear in each variable.
    The pentagon identifies the two composites from \(((W\otimes X)\otimes Y)\otimes Z\) to \(W\otimes(X\otimes(Y\otimes Z))\text{.}\) The triangle is the equality \((\id_X\otimes\lambda_Y)a_{X,\mathbf1,Y}=\rho_X\otimes\id_Y\text{.}\) We suppress these associativity and unit maps in formulas. The coherence theorem justifies this convention: composites of associativity and unit constraints with the same source and target agree. With a symmetry, one must also keep track of the permutation of factors. See [EGNO15, §§2.8–2.9 and 8.1].
    With associators suppressed, the two hexagon equations are
    \begin{gather*} c_{X,Y\otimes Z}=(\id_Y\otimes c_{X,Z})(c_{X,Y}\otimes\id_Z),\\ c_{X\otimes Y,Z}=(c_{X,Z}\otimes\id_Y)(\id_X\otimes c_{Y,Z}). \end{gather*}
    They say that moving a factor past a tensor of two factors agrees with moving it past them successively. The further symmetry equation makes two opposite interchanges cancel. In a merely braided category, composites inducing the same permutation may differ. For example, over a field \(k\) fix \(u\in k^\times\text{.}\) On graded vector spaces the maps \(x\otimes y\mapsto u^{pq}y\otimes x\) for homogeneous degrees \(p,q\) define a braiding; the composite in the two directions is multiplication by \(u^{2pq}\text{.}\) It is generally not a symmetry.
    The unit constraints identify the two ways of removing the unit from \(\mathbf1\otimes\mathbf1\text{.}\) They also determine how a braiding interchanges a factor with the unit. These are consequences of coherence, rather than additional choices of maps. We use coherence only for expressions built from the stated constraints; it does not assert that arbitrary diagrams of morphisms commute.
    Biadditivity means separate additivity. It does not mean that \(\otimes\text{,}\) viewed as a functor from the additive product category, is additive: expanding \((f+f')\otimes(g+g')\) has four terms. It does imply preservation of finite biproducts in each variable, including \(X\otimes0\simeq0\text{.}\)1

    Example 3.2.

    For a commutative ring \(R\text{,}\) \(\Mod(R)\) is symmetric monoidal under \(\otimes_R\text{,}\) with unit \(R\) and symmetry \(x\otimes y\mapsto y\otimes x\text{.}\) For a noncommutative ring, two left modules have no such tensor product over that ring. Instead, an \(R\)–\(S\)-bimodule \(M\) and an \(S\)–\(T\)-bimodule \(N\) have an \(R\)–\(T\)-bimodule \(M\otimes_SN\text{.}\) The category of \(R\)–\(R\)-bimodules is monoidal with unit \(R\text{,}\) and is generally not symmetric.
    Finite biproduct is a different monoidal operation on an additive category, with unit zero. For fixed nonzero \(X\text{,}\) the functor \(-\oplus X\) is not additive and has no right adjoint: it does not preserve the initial object. The tensor products used in the additive homological theory have the separate additivity required above.

    Definition 3.3 Algebra and module objects.

    A monoid object, also called an algebra object, is an object \(A\) with multiplication \(\mu:A\otimes A\to A\) and unit \(\eta:\mathbf1\to A\text{,}\) satisfying associativity and the two unit equations using the constraints. In a braided monoidal category it is commutative if \(\mu c_{A,A}=\mu\text{.}\) A left \(A\)-module object has an action \(A\otimes M\to M\) satisfying the corresponding associativity and unit equations.
    For commutative \(R\text{,}\) algebra objects in \(\Mod(R)\) are associative unital \(R\)-algebras with central \(R\)-action; module objects are their usual modules. The unit object itself is an algebra object through its unit constraints. A monoidal structure, which multiplies objects of a category, and an algebra object, which is one object carrying a multiplication map, are different data. We will use both when differential graded algebras and their modules occur.

    Proposition 3.4.

    The monoid \(\End_{\mathcal C}(\mathbf1)\) under composition is commutative in every monoidal category. In an additive category with biadditive tensor product it is a commutative ring. Each element \(a\) acts naturally on every object by \(a_X=\lambda_X(a\otimes\id_X)\lambda_X^{-1}\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Transport tensor product to a second multiplication on \(\End(\mathbf1)\) using \(\mathbf1\otimes\mathbf1\simeq\mathbf1\text{.}\) Both multiplications have identity \(\id_{\mathbf1}\text{,}\) and functoriality of tensor gives the interchange equation. Substituting identities in alternate positions shows that the multiplications agree and commute. Additivity gives the ring laws. Naturality of the unit constraint proves naturality of its action on each object.
    This ring of unit endomorphisms is one source of scalars in a monoidal category.2 A specified linear structure over another commutative ring must act compatibly with composition and tensor product; it is additional data, not a choice of a name for the same ring.

    Section 3.2 Internal Hom and adjunction

    Definition 3.5 Closed structures.

    A monoidal category is right closed, in our convention, if for every \(X\) the functor \(-\otimes X\) has a right adjoint \([X,-]\text{.}\) Thus there are natural bijections
    \begin{equation*} \Hom_{\mathcal C}(T\otimes X,Y)\simeq\Hom_{\mathcal C}(T,[X,Y]). \end{equation*}
    The object \([X,Y]\) is the internal Hom. Left closedness means that \(X\otimes-\) has a right adjoint for every \(X\text{.}\) In a symmetric monoidal category, the symmetry converts either structure into the other; we then say closed symmetric monoidal.
    Internal Hom is an object of \(\mathcal C\text{;}\) \(\Hom_{\mathcal C}(X,Y)\) is a set, or an abelian group in the additive case. Our bracket notation keeps the two visible. The representing property determines internal Hom up to a unique isomorphism compatible with its adjunction, and gives a bifunctor \([-,-]:\mathcal C^\op\times\mathcal C\to\mathcal C\text{.}\)3
    This is the internal-Hom convention of [LiII, Definition 7.3.1]. Its ordinary Hom and currying identities are [LiII, Propositions 7.3.3–7.3.4]. We retain their representing-property proofs because the same argument will produce the internal adjunction formulas for exceptional operations.

    Proposition 3.6.

    In a closed symmetric monoidal category there are natural isomorphisms \([\mathbf1,Y]\simeq Y\) and
    \begin{equation*} [X\otimes Y,Z]\simeq[X,[Y,Z]]. \end{equation*}
    There is a natural bijection \(\Hom(\mathbf1,[X,Y])\simeq\Hom(X,Y)\text{.}\) The counit is the evaluation map \(\operatorname{ev}_{X,Y}:[X,Y]\otimes X\to Y\text{.}\) If small colimits exist, tensoring with any fixed object preserves them.

    Proof.
    Apply the defining adjunction to morphisms from an arbitrary \(T\text{.}\) For the second isomorphism, the required chain is \(\Hom(T,[X\otimes Y,Z])\simeq\Hom(T\otimes X\otimes Y,Z)\simeq\Hom(T,[X,[Y,Z]])\text{.}\) Yoneda gives the isomorphisms and their naturality. The last assertion holds because tensoring is a left adjoint.
    Evaluation also defines internal composition \([Y,Z]\otimes[X,Y]\to[X,Z]\text{:}\) its adjoint evaluates first at \(X\) and then at \(Y\text{.}\) The morphism \(\mathbf1\to[X,X]\) corresponding to \(\id_X\) is its unit. Associativity follows by evaluating both composites on \(X\text{.}\) In particular, \([X,X]\) is a monoid object, with multiplication given by composition.

    Proposition 3.7.

    In a closed symmetric monoidal category, for every small diagram for which the indicated limits and colimits exist, there are natural isomorphisms
    \begin{equation*} [X,\lim_iY_i]\simeq\lim_i[X,Y_i],\qquad[\colim_iX_i,Y]\simeq\lim_i[X_i,Y]. \end{equation*}
    In the second formula the limit is indexed by the opposite of the original indexing category. A morphism \(a:X'\to X\) acts on internal Hom by precomposition, and \(b:Y\to Y'\) acts by postcomposition. The evaluation maps characterize both operations.

    Proof.
    The first assertion is preservation of limits by the right adjoint \([X,-]\text{.}\) For the second, test against an arbitrary \(T\text{.}\) Tensor with \(T\) preserves colimits, so
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}\Hom(T,[\colim_iX_i,Y])&\simeq\Hom(\colim_i(T\otimes X_i),Y)\\&\simeq\lim_i\Hom(T,[X_i,Y]).\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Yoneda proves the statement. The same argument constructs the variance maps from their composites with evaluation.
    There is also a canonical morphism
    \begin{equation*} [X,Y]\otimes Z\longrightarrow[X,Y\otimes Z]. \end{equation*}
    Its adjoint interchanges \(X\) and \(Z\text{,}\) evaluates \([X,Y]\otimes X\text{,}\) and leaves \(Z\) as a tensor factor. This morphism need not be invertible. Dualizability of \(X\) will make it invertible for every \(Y,Z\text{.}\) Merely writing a tensor–Hom comparison does not supply that hypothesis.

    Example 3.8.

    For commutative \(R\text{,}\) the internal Hom is the \(R\)-module \([M,N]=\Hom_R(M,N)\text{,}\) with \((r\phi)(m)=r\phi(m)\text{.}\) For noncommutative rings, the variance is expressed by bimodules. If \(M\) is an \(R\)–\(S\)-bimodule, \(N\) a left \(S\)-module, and \(P\) a left \(R\)-module, then
    \begin{equation*} \Hom_R(M\otimes_SN,P)\simeq\Hom_S(N,\Hom_R(M,P)), \end{equation*}
    where \((s\phi)(m)=\phi(ms)\text{.}\) The inverse maps a homomorphism \(\psi\) to \(m\otimes n\mapsto\psi(n)(m)\text{;}\) the balance relation is exactly its \(S\)-linearity.

    Example 3.9.

    Let \(R\) be commutative. For \(\Z\)-graded modules, set \((M\otimes N)^n=\bigoplus_{p+q=n}M^p\otimes_RN^q\) and
    \begin{equation*} [M,N]^n=\prod_{p\in\Z}\Hom_R(M^p,N^{p+n}). \end{equation*}
    Morphisms of the category preserve degree. These formulas define a closed symmetric monoidal structure with symmetry \(x\otimes y\mapsto(-1)^{pq}y\otimes x\) on degrees \(p,q\text{.}\) The product in internal Hom allows a homogeneous map to have a component on every source degree. Adding differentials will impose the formulas in Chapter 4.
    The functor \(\Hom(\mathbf1,-)\) extracts ordinary morphisms from an internal Hom object. It need not detect all information in an arbitrary object. For graded modules the unit is \(R\) in degree zero, so \(\Hom(\mathbf1,M)=M^0\text{.}\) A nonzero object concentrated in another degree has no nonzero map from the unit. Thus knowing ordinary maps \(X\to Y\) is not in general enough to reconstruct \([X,Y]\text{.}\) A generator condition on the unit is an extra assumption.

    Section 3.3 Exactness and dualizable objects

    Definition 3.10 Flatness and duality.

    1. In an abelian symmetric monoidal category with biadditive tensor product, \(X\) is flat if \(-\otimes X\) is exact.
    2. In a symmetric monoidal category, a dual of \(X\) consists of \(X^\vee\) and maps \(\operatorname{ev}:X^\vee\otimes X\to\mathbf1\text{,}\) \(\operatorname{coev}:\mathbf1\to X\otimes X^\vee\) such that the composites obtained by inserting coevaluation and then evaluation on \(X\) and on \(X^\vee\) are their identities. An object having such a dual is dualizable. A category in which every object is dualizable is rigid.

    Proposition 3.11.

    Let \(\mathcal A\) be abelian, closed symmetric monoidal, with biadditive tensor product.
    1. Tensoring with a fixed object is right exact, and \([X,-]\) is left exact. For a short exact sequence \(0\to X'\to X\to X''\to0\text{,}\) the sequence \(0\to[X'',Y]\to[X,Y]\to[X',Y]\) is exact.
    2. If \(X\) is dualizable, then \([X,Y]\simeq X^\vee\otimes Y\) naturally in \(Y\text{,}\) and tensoring with \(X\) is exact.

    Proof.
    Left adjoints preserve cokernels and right adjoints preserve kernels. For the contravariant assertion, apply \(\Hom(T,-)\) and the tensor–Hom adjunction. The right exact sequence \(T\otimes X'\to T\otimes X\to T\otimes X''\to0\) becomes a left exact sequence after applying \(\Hom(-,Y)\text{.}\) This is the kernel criterion, tested by every \(T\text{.}\)
    A dual pair gives a bijection \(\Hom(T\otimes X,Y)\simeq\Hom(T,X^\vee\otimes Y)\text{:}\) insert coevaluation in one direction and contract evaluation in the other. The two identities of a dual pair show these maps are inverse. The right adjoint is therefore \(X^\vee\otimes-\text{.}\) Symmetry gives an adjunction in the other direction as well, so tensoring with \(X\) preserves kernels and cokernels.
    For a module over a commutative ring, dualizability is equivalent to being finitely generated projective. One direction follows from a finite dual basis. Conversely, a coevaluation is a finite sum \(\sum_{i=1}^nm_i\otimes\phi_i\text{.}\) The triangle identity says \(m=\sum_i\phi_i(m)m_i\text{,}\) which expresses the module as a direct summand of \(R^n\text{.}\) Thus the algebraic dual \(\Hom_R(M,R)\) exists for every module, but is a dual in the monoidal sense only under this finite projectivity condition. For instance, \(\Z/2\Z\) is not flat and has zero algebraic dual. The \(\Z\)-module \(\Q\) is flat, since tensoring with it is localization, but it is not dualizable because it is not finitely generated.

    Proposition 3.12.

    In an abelian closed symmetric monoidal category, an object is flat if and only if tensoring with it preserves monomorphisms. Finite biproducts and direct summands of flat objects are flat. If the category is Grothendieck, filtered colimits and arbitrary coproducts of flat objects are flat.

    Proof.
    Tensor is already right exact, so preserving monomorphisms gives exactness. A finite sum of exact additive functors is exact, and a direct summand of an exact functor is exact, by taking the corresponding direct summands of its kernel and cokernel sequences. For a filtered diagram of flat objects, tensor commutes with its colimit and AB5 preserves the short exact sequences obtained at each stage. Arbitrary coproducts are filtered colimits of finite partial sums.
    In a nonsymmetric monoidal abelian category, exactness of \(-\otimes X\) and of \(X\otimes-\) must be distinguished. For bimodules they test different module actions. The symmetry in our definition identifies the two tests. Flatness is also relative to the chosen tensor product: its definition says nothing about \(\Hom(X,-)\text{.}\) Projectivity concerns the exactness of that ordinary Hom functor and has a different role in resolutions.

    Proposition 3.13.

    In a closed symmetric monoidal category, let \(X^\vee=[X,\mathbf1]\) and use evaluation to form
    \begin{equation*} X^\vee\otimes Y\longrightarrow[X,Y]. \end{equation*}
    The object \(X\) is dualizable if and only if these maps are isomorphisms for all \(Y\text{.}\) Duals, when they exist, are unique up to the isomorphism respecting evaluation and coevaluation. The unit is dualizable, the dual of \(X\otimes Y\) is \(Y^\vee\otimes X^\vee\text{,}\) and a dualizable object has a natural bidual isomorphism \(X\simeq X^{\vee\vee}\text{.}\) In an additive category with biadditive tensor, finite biproducts of dualizable objects are dualizable. They are also closed under retracts.

    Proof.
    A dual pair gives the tensor–Hom isomorphisms proved above. Conversely, the assumed isomorphisms identify the right adjoint of \(-\otimes X\) with \(X^\vee\otimes-\text{,}\) compatibly with evaluation. The adjunction unit at \(\mathbf1\) gives coevaluation, with symmetry putting its factors in the specified order. The adjunction identities give the two dual-pair identities. Uniqueness is uniqueness of the right adjoint with its counit. Tensoring two evaluation and coevaluation pairs gives the reversed-order tensor dual; the two contractions cancel separately. Exchanging the roles of an object and its dual gives biduality.
    For a finite biproduct take the sum of the duals, with diagonal evaluations and coevaluations, so the two identities hold on each summand. For a retract of \(X\text{,}\) its tensor–Hom comparison is a retract of the comparison for \(X\text{,}\) by naturality in the first variable. It is therefore invertible, and the criterion proves dualizability.
    The resulting duality is contravariant on the subcategory of dualizable objects. For \(f:X\to Y\text{,}\) its dual \(f^\vee:Y^\vee\to X^\vee\) is characterized by
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{ev}_X(f^\vee\otimes\id_X)=\operatorname{ev}_Y(\id_{Y^\vee}\otimes f). \end{equation*}
    This equation gives \((gf)^\vee=f^\vee g^\vee\text{.}\) The existence of an object called an algebraic dual is weaker than these assertions. In a closed category the object \([X,\mathbf1]\) exists for every \(X\text{,}\) but its tensor–Hom comparison may fail to be an isomorphism. Biduality for a particular object is weaker than dualizability as well; the distinction will be used in Chapter 16.
    For the ordinary dual-pair calculus, see [LiII, §9.1] and [EGNO15, §2.10]. In a nonsymmetric category a left dual and a right dual are distinct notions, and having one does not by itself identify them. Symmetry supplies the interchange needed for the single convention used here.
    A Grothendieck category equipped with a closed symmetric monoidal structure satisfies both an existence condition on the category and adjunction conditions on its tensor product. Neither condition alone supplies enough flat objects. Even after choosing flat objects, an unbounded complex of them need not be K-flat; the latter condition tests tensor products with acyclic complexes and belongs to Chapter 10.

    Section 3.4 Tensor terminology and functors

    Definition 3.14 Monoidal functors.

    A lax monoidal functor \(F:\mathcal C\to\mathcal E\) has natural maps \(F(X)\otimes F(Y)\to F(X\otimes Y)\) and \(\mathbf1_{\mathcal E}\to F(\mathbf1_{\mathcal C})\) compatible with associativity and units. It is strong monoidal when these maps are invertible. Between symmetric monoidal categories, compatibility with the two symmetries is an additional requirement. Exactness and faithfulness are separate properties.
    Writing the structure maps as \(\phi_{X,Y}\) and \(\phi_0\text{,}\) the associativity condition equates the two maps from \((FX\otimes FY)\otimes FZ\) to \(F(X\otimes(Y\otimes Z))\text{.}\) The unit conditions equate the composite through \(\phi_0\) with the corresponding unit constraint. A monoidal natural transformation \(\alpha:F\to G\) satisfies
    \begin{equation*} \alpha_{X\otimes Y}\phi^F_{X,Y}=\phi^G_{X,Y}(\alpha_X\otimes\alpha_Y),\qquad \alpha_{\mathbf1}\phi^F_0=\phi^G_0. \end{equation*}
    A monoidal equivalence is a strong monoidal functor whose underlying functor is an equivalence; an inverse equivalence can be equipped with compatible strong monoidal structure. A strict monoidal functor preserves the tensor and unit with identity structure maps. Strictness concerns specified data and is stronger than being strong.
    A colax monoidal functor has structure maps in the opposite directions, \(F(X\otimes Y)\to FX\otimes FY\) and \(F\mathbf1\to\mathbf1\text{.}\) When these maps are invertible the directions can be reversed; without invertibility lax and colax are different structures.4

    Proposition 3.15.

    A lax monoidal functor sends algebra objects to algebra objects. A strong monoidal functor sends dual pairs to dual pairs, and therefore preserves dualizable objects. If \(F:\mathcal C\to\mathcal E\) is strong monoidal between closed symmetric monoidal categories, there is a natural comparison
    \begin{equation*} F[X,Y]\longrightarrow[FX,FY]. \end{equation*}
    It is an isomorphism when \(X\) is dualizable. It need not be an isomorphism for arbitrary \(X\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    The multiplication on \(FA\) is the composite \(FA\otimes FA\to F(A\otimes A)\to FA\text{,}\) with unit obtained from \(\phi_0\text{.}\) Functoriality and the monoidal equations give its associativity and units. For a dual pair apply \(F\) to evaluation and coevaluation and use the invertible structure maps to identify their sources and targets. The identities remain identities. The displayed comparison is adjoint to \(F[X,Y]\otimes FX\simeq F([X,Y]\otimes X)\to FY\text{.}\) When \(X\) is dualizable, both its source and target are identified with \(F(X^\vee)\otimes FY\text{.}\)
    For an adjunction \(F\dashv G\) with \(F\) strong monoidal, the right adjoint has a lax monoidal structure. Its tensor map is adjoint to
    \begin{equation*} F(GA\otimes GB)\simeq FGA\otimes FGB\xrightarrow{\epsilon_A\otimes\epsilon_B}A\otimes B, \end{equation*}
    and its unit map is adjoint to \(F\mathbf1\simeq\mathbf1\text{.}\) The adjunction identities give coherence. These maps need not be invertible. Extension and restriction of scalars provide a familiar example: extension along a map of commutative rings is strong monoidal, while restriction is generally only lax. Its unit structure map is the ring map itself. The adjunction is developed abstractly in Chapter 17.
    In [EGNO15, Definition 4.1.1], over an algebraically closed field \(k\text{,}\) a tensor category is a locally finite \(k\)-linear abelian rigid monoidal category with bilinear tensor product and \(\End(\mathbf1)\simeq k\text{.}\) Here local finiteness includes finite-dimensional morphism spaces and finite length of objects. Neither symmetry nor semisimplicity is part of that definition. Their tensor functors also carry exactness and faithfulness requirements; see [EGNO15, §4.2]. These are stronger conventions than the monoidal terminology of this chapter.
    For example, all \(R\)-modules form our closed symmetric monoidal category, but generally not a tensor category in that representation-theoretic sense. Finite-dimensional representations of a group over a field provide examples with duals, while arbitrary-dimensional vector spaces already include nondualizable objects. A reference to “tensor categories” must therefore specify which hypotheses are intended. In this note the assumptions “abelian,” “closed,” “symmetric,” and “exact in each variable” will be stated when used.

    Associativity isomorphisms are specified structure. Writing tensors without parentheses does not assert that the original category is strictly associative. A strictification is a monoidal equivalence, not an equality of categories.

    2

    The right unit constraint defines a second action. In a symmetric monoidal category the two actions agree. In a general monoidal category they can differ: on an \(R\)-bimodule a central element of \(R=\mathbf1\) can act differently on the left and on the right.

    3

    “Left closed” and “right closed” are interchanged in some sources, according to whether the named side describes the fixed factor or the location of its adjoint. The displayed adjunction fixes our convention.

    4

    Some sources use “monoidal” for strong monoidal and others for lax monoidal. We state the adjective when invertibility matters. “Tensor functor” has a further representation-theoretic convention described below.
