    Chapter 5 Intersection theory and cohomology

    Invariant cycles make intersection theory explicit, and the character decomposition makes sheaf cohomology into a family of finite complexes. We establish those calculations without identifying the theories they compute. Chow groups are algebraic cycle groups; coherent cohomology belongs to a sheaf; and, over \(\C\text{,}\) singular cohomology is a topological invariant. Smoothness, completeness, and the coefficient ring determine when comparisons between them hold.
    The supporting inputs are the standard operations on Chow groups and the acyclicity of quasi-coherent sheaves on affine schemes. We state the required toric presentation theorem explicitly, then prove the ring presentation from it. The main references are [Ful93, Chapter 5], [CLS11, Chapters 9 and 12–13], and [FS97]. Except for the last section on complex equivariant cohomology, the field remains the algebraically closed field of arbitrary characteristic fixed in Chapter 0.

    Section 5.1 Invariant cycles and Chow groups

    For a variety \(X\text{,}\) the group \(A_j(X)\) is generated by integral \(j\)-dimensional subvarieties, modulo divisors of rational functions on integral \((j+1)\)-dimensional subvarieties. Rational equivalence includes those divisors even when the subvariety is not complete. Proper pushforward, restriction to an open subset, and the localization sequence are the standard Chow operations used below. Their foundations are supplied by [Ful98, Chapters 1–2].

    Proposition 5.1 Orbit closures generate.

    The group \(A_j(X_\Sigma)\) is generated by the classes \([V(\sigma)]\) with \(\dim\sigma=n-j\text{.}\) Completeness and smoothness are not required.

    Proof.
    A split torus \(\Gm^d\) is an open subset of \(\A^d\text{.}\) The localization sequence and homotopy invariance give \(A_j(\Gm^d)=0\) for \(j\ne d\) and \(A_d(\Gm^d)=\Z[\Gm^d]\text{.}\) The equality in top dimension also follows directly because there is one irreducible component and no subvariety of larger dimension that could supply a relation.
    Filter \(X_\Sigma\) by the closed subsets consisting of orbits of dimension at most \(d\text{.}\) Each successive open stratum is a disjoint union of \(d\)-dimensional tori. The localization sequence at each step shows that its only possible new Chow generators are the closures of those tori in dimension \(d\text{.}\) Induction gives precisely the asserted orbit closures.
    Generation alone does not identify all relations. The following presentation is the additional toric input. For a cone \(\tau\text{,}\) put \(N_\tau=N\cap\operatorname{span}_{\R}\tau\) and \(M(\tau)=M\cap\tau^\perp\text{.}\) If \(\tau\preceq\sigma\) and \(\dim\sigma=\dim\tau+1\text{,}\) write \(u_{\sigma/\tau}\) for the primitive generator of the quotient ray in \(N/N_\tau\text{.}\)

    Theorem 5.2 The complete list of invariant cycle relations.

    For \(0\leq j\leq n\text{,}\) the generators in Proposition 5.1 present \(A_j(X_\Sigma)\) subject to the relations
    \begin{equation} \sum_{\sigma\supset\tau\atop\dim\sigma=n-j}\langle m,u_{\sigma/\tau}\rangle[V(\sigma)]=0,\qquad \dim\tau=n-j-1,\quad m\in M(\tau).\tag{5.1} \end{equation}
    In particular, the relations displayed generate all rational equivalences between invariant cycle classes.
    The completeness of this relation list is the external presentation theorem in [FS97, Section 1; Proposition 1.1 in the arXiv version]; see also [Tot14, Theorem 1] for its algebraic stratification setting over a field. The displayed relation itself is already explained by our divisor calculation: it is the principal divisor of \(\chi^m\) on the orbit closure \(V(\tau)=X_{\operatorname{Star}(\tau)}\text{,}\) pushed forward to \(X\text{.}\)
    The primitive vector is taken in the quotient lattice. For a singular cone it need not be the raw image of an original primitive ray generator. Replacing it by an arbitrary integral representative without tracking its index changes the relation.1

    Section 5.2 The Chow ring of a smooth toric variety

    For a smooth pure \(n\)-dimensional variety, the intersection product equips \(A^*(X)=A_{n-*}(X)\) with a graded ring structure. A line bundle acts by its first Chern class; for a Cartier divisor this is its usual divisor class. We use this smooth intersection theory as a supporting result. On a singular variety the groups \(A_{n-*}(X)\) do not acquire such a ring simply by changing their indices.

    Definition 5.3 The two relation ideals.

    Let \(R_\Sigma=\Z[x_\rho:\rho\in\Sigma(1)]\text{,}\) with \(\deg x_\rho=1\text{.}\) Define
    \begin{equation*} I_\Sigma=(x_{\rho_1}\cdots x_{\rho_s}:\text{the distinct rays }\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_s\text{ lie in no cone of }\Sigma), \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} J_\Sigma=\left(\sum_\rho\langle m,u_\rho\rangle x_\rho:m\in M\right). \end{equation*}
    The first is the Stanley–Reisner ideal of the ray incidence complex; the second records character-divisor relations.

    Theorem 5.4 An integral presentation.

    If every cone of \(\Sigma\) is smooth, then
    \begin{equation} A^*(X_\Sigma)\cong R_\Sigma/(I_\Sigma+J_\Sigma),\qquad x_\rho\longmapsto[D_\rho].\tag{5.2} \end{equation}
    This algebraic statement holds over \(k\) and does not require completeness.

    Proof.
    The character-divisor formula kills \(J_\Sigma\text{.}\) Invariant divisors for rays not lying in a common cone have empty intersection, by the orbit–cone correspondence, so their product is zero and \(I_\Sigma\) is killed as well. For the rays of a cone \(\sigma\text{,}\) smooth coordinates on each chart show that the corresponding divisors meet transversely with intersection \(V(\sigma)\text{,}\) of multiplicity one. Therefore the induced map from the quotient ring is surjective by orbit generation.
    Define a homomorphism of graded groups in the other direction by
    \begin{equation*} [V(\sigma)]\longmapsto x_\sigma:=\prod_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}x_\rho. \end{equation*}
    We check the complete relation list (5.1). Smoothness makes \(u_{\sigma/\tau}\) the primitive image of the one new ray of \(\sigma\) outside \(\tau\text{.}\) Since \(m\in M(\tau)\text{,}\) the proposed image of the relation equals
    \begin{equation*} x_\tau\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma(1)}\langle m,u_\rho\rangle x_\rho. \end{equation*}
    Terms on rays of \(\tau\) have coefficient zero. A remaining term vanishes modulo \(I_\Sigma\) unless the ray and \(\tau\) lie in a cone; when they do, their union generates the unique next-dimensional face of that smooth cone. Thus the expression is exactly the asserted relation and is zero modulo \(J_\Sigma\text{.}\) The inverse is well defined.
    Its composite with the first map is the identity on the generating orbit classes. In the other order it fixes every squarefree cone monomial \(x_\sigma\text{,}\) because the product calculation sends that monomial to its orbit class. These monomials span the quotient as a group: monomials with repeated factors reduce to linear combinations of the \(x_\sigma\) by choosing a character taking value \(1\) on one repeated ray and \(0\) on the other rays in its smooth supporting cone, and multiplying its linear relation by the remaining monomial. At each nonzero term the number of distinct ray factors increases. The reduction stops at squarefree cone monomials. Hence the two maps are inverse on every graded piece.
    The proof isolates why the statement is integral: the ray vectors on a smooth cone form part of a lattice basis. Simpliciality alone supplies a rational basis and can introduce denominators. The standard complete simplicial rational comparison is given in [CLS11, Theorem 12.5.3]; it must not be used as an integral presentation for singular varieties.

    Proposition 5.5 Evaluating top products.

    Suppose in addition that \(X_\Sigma\) is complete. Its degree map \(A^n(X)\to\Z\) sends the squarefree product of the rays of a full-dimensional cone to \(1\text{.}\) A product of distinct rays contained in no cone is zero. The ring relations then compute every top-degree divisor product.

    Proof.
    The first product is the class of the torus-fixed point \(V(\sigma)\text{,}\) which has residue field \(k\) and degree \(1\text{.}\) The empty-intersection relation gives the second assertion. Orbit generation and the reduction in the ring proof express an arbitrary top product in these terms; proper pushforward ensures that the resulting integer is independent of the reductions.
    Properness is needed for this degree operation. For example, on \(\A^1\) the origin is the principal divisor of its coordinate and is zero in \(A^1(\A^1)\text{.}\) Assigning it a global intersection degree of \(1\) would contradict rational equivalence.

    Section 5.3 Surface products and the volume comparison

    Example 5.6 Projective plane and a product of lines.

    For \(\P^2\text{,}\) the three ray classes equal \(H\text{,}\) and the only minimal nonface consists of all three rays. Hence
    \begin{equation*} A^*(\P^2)=\Z[H]/(H^3),\qquad\deg H^2=1. \end{equation*}
    For \(\P^1\times\P^1\text{,}\) opposite ray pairs do not span cones, and the character relations identify opposite divisor classes. Thus
    \begin{equation*} A^*(\P^1\times\P^1)=\Z[A,B]/(A^2,B^2),\qquad\deg AB=1. \end{equation*}
    In particular, a divisor \(aA+bB\) has self-intersection \(2ab\text{.}\)

    Example 5.7 The intersection form of a Hirzebruch surface.

    Use the rays and classes of Example 3.18. The nonadjacent pairs are \(13\) and \(24\text{.}\) Since \([D_1]=[D_3]=F\) and \([D_4]=S+aF\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} A^*(\mathbb F_a)=\Z[S,F]/(F^2,S(S+aF)),\qquad\deg SF=1. \end{equation*}
    It follows that
    \begin{equation*} F^2=0,\qquad S^2=-a,\qquad(\alpha S+\beta F)^2=2\alpha\beta-a\alpha^2. \end{equation*}
    Thus the class denoted by \(S\) is the negative section when \(a>0\text{.}\) Its square has been derived from the fan, rather than imported from the projective-bundle description.
    For the invariant representative \(D=\alpha D_2+\beta D_1\text{,}\) the inequalities are
    \begin{equation*} p\geq-\beta,\qquad q\geq-\alpha,\qquad p\leq aq,\qquad q\leq0. \end{equation*}
    If \(\alpha\geq0\) and \(\beta\geq a\alpha\text{,}\) the polytope has area \(\alpha\beta-a\alpha^2/2\) in the measure for \(M=\Z^2\text{.}\) Twice this area is precisely the displayed self-intersection. Degenerate segments and points have zero two-dimensional area, as required.

    Example 5.8 Integral torsion on a smooth open variety.

    Remove the closed orbit from the quadratic affine surface of Example 1.29. The remaining fan has the two rays \((1,0),(1,2)\) and the zero cone, with no two-dimensional cone. Its cones are smooth, and the ring theorem gives
    \begin{equation*} A^*(X)=\Z[t]/(2t,t^2). \end{equation*}
    Indeed the linear relations are \(x_1+x_2=0\) and \(2x_2=0\text{,}\) and the nonface relation is \(x_1x_2=0\text{.}\) Thus \(A^1(X)=\Pic(X)=\Z/2\Z\text{.}\) Smoothness alone does not make the Picard group torsion-free; completeness is absent here.
    For ample divisors on a projective toric variety, the leading term of the lattice-point count gives the degree–volume formula. We use the normalization of Section 0.7: for an \(n\)-dimensional polytope, the factor is \(n!\text{,}\) and the volume is measured in \(M\text{.}\) For a nef divisor \(D\text{,}\) choose an ample invariant divisor \(A\) and apply the ample formula to \(qD+A\text{.}\) Divide by \(q^n\) and let positive integers \(q\) tend to infinity. The intersection side tends to \(D^n\text{;}\) the polytope side uses \(P_{qD+A}=qP_D+P_A\) and continuity of volume to tend to \(n!\vol_M(P_D)\text{.}\) This gives the nef comparison over \(k\) as well. Compare [CLS11, Theorem 13.4.3].

    Theorem 5.9 Intersection numbers and mixed volumes.

    Let \(X\) be a smooth projective toric variety of dimension \(n\geq1\text{,}\) and let \(D_1,\ldots,D_n\) be nef Cartier divisors. Then
    \begin{equation*} \deg(D_1\cdots D_n)=n!\,V_M(P_{D_1},\ldots,P_{D_n}). \end{equation*}
    The mixed-volume normalization is specified by \(V_M(P,\ldots,P)=\vol_M(P)\text{;}\) equivalently, \(n!V_M(P_1,\ldots,P_n)\) is the coefficient of \(t_1\cdots t_n\) in \(\vol_M(t_1P_1+\cdots+t_nP_n)\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    For globally generated invariant Cartier divisors on a complete fan, addition of divisors corresponds to Minkowski addition of their polytopes. Their support functions are the minima of their linear pairings, so equality of support functions gives this assertion. Apply the degree–volume formula to \(t_1D_1+\cdots+t_nD_n\) for nonnegative integers \(t_i\text{.}\) Both sides are polynomial in the \(t_i\text{;}\) equality on these tuples implies equality of coefficients. The coefficient of \(t_1\cdots t_n\) in the self-intersection is \(n!\deg(D_1\cdots D_n)\text{,}\) and the coefficient on the volume side is \(n!\cdot n!V_M\text{.}\) Cancelling the common factor gives the claim.

    Section 5.4 Bernstein’s theorem for Laurent polynomial systems

    Mixed intersections solve a problem about arbitrary Laurent polynomials, whose zero sets need not be toric. The polytopes choose a compactification; a condition on initial forms keeps the intersection inside its open torus. This gives a count with multiplicities over our field. A separate characteristic-zero transversality input will turn the generic count into a count of distinct points.

    Definition 5.10 Newton polytopes and the condition at infinity.

    For a nonzero Laurent polynomial \(f=\sum c_m\chi^m\in k[M]\text{,}\) its Newton polytope is
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Newt}(f)=\operatorname{conv}\{m:c_m\ne0\}. \end{equation*}
    For \(w\in N_{\R}\text{,}\) its initial form in direction \(w\) is the sum of terms where \(\langle m,w\rangle\) is minimal:
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{in}_w(f)= \sum_{\langle m,w\rangle=\min_{\operatorname{Newt}(f)} \langle\,\cdot\,,w\rangle}c_m\chi^m. \end{equation*}
    A system \(f_1,\ldots,f_n\) is nondegenerate at infinity if for every nonzero \(w\in N_{\R}\) the initial forms \(\operatorname{in}_w(f_1),\ldots,\operatorname{in}_w(f_n)\) have no common zero in \(T_N\text{.}\)
    This is a condition on the simultaneous system. It does not require each polynomial, considered separately, to define a smooth hypersurface on every face. The zero direction is excluded: its initial system is the original system, whose common roots we want to count.

    Theorem 5.11 The mixed-volume count with scheme multiplicities.

    Let \(\rk M=n\geq1\) and let \(f_1,\ldots,f_n\in k[M]\) be nonzero. Put \(P_i=\operatorname{Newt}(f_i)\) and assume that \(P_1+\cdots+P_n\) is full-dimensional in \(M_{\R}\text{.}\) If the system is nondegenerate at infinity, its common-zero scheme \(Z\subset T_N\) is finite and
    \begin{equation} \operatorname{length}_k Z =n!\,V_M(P_1,\ldots,P_n).\tag{5.3} \end{equation}
    The equality holds in arbitrary characteristic and permits both sides to be zero.

    Proof.
    Take the normal fan of \(P=P_1+\cdots+P_n\text{.}\) The minimum pairing function of the sum is the sum of the minimum pairing functions of the summands, so its normal fan refines their normal decompositions. Choose a smooth projective subdivision as supplied by Subsection 7.2.3. The lattice-subdivision construction works over \(k\text{,}\) and projectivity of the resulting variety follows by composing its projective morphism to \(X_P\) with the projective structure of \(X_P\text{.}\) Write \(X\) for this smooth projective toric compactification.
    Each function \(v\mapsto\min_{m\in P_i}\langle m,v\rangle\) is integral linear on every cone of the chosen fan and is concave. It defines a nef Cartier divisor \(D_i\) with polytope \(P_i\text{.}\) The polynomial \(f_i\) is its global section. On a maximal chart, choose the Cartier point \(m_{i,\sigma}\in P_i\text{;}\) the coefficient in the local frame is
    \begin{equation*} g_{i,\sigma}=\chi^{-m_{i,\sigma}}f_i \in k[S_\sigma]. \end{equation*}
    We identify its restriction to a boundary orbit \(O(\tau)\text{,}\) with \(0\ne\tau\preceq\sigma\text{.}\) Choose \(w\) in the relative interior of \(\tau\text{.}\) Restricting a monomial of \(k[S_\sigma]\) to this orbit kills it unless its exponent annihilates \(\tau\text{.}\) Since all these exponents pair nonnegatively with \(\tau\text{,}\) pairing zero with \(w\) is equivalent to annihilating \(\tau\text{.}\) The surviving terms are therefore exactly
    \begin{equation*} \chi^{-m_{i,\sigma}}\operatorname{in}_w(f_i) \in k[M\cap\tau^\perp]. \end{equation*}
    This polynomial is the restriction of the section in its local frame.
    The torus map \(T_N\to O(\tau)\cong T_{N/N_\tau}\) is surjective: \(N_\tau\) is saturated, so the quotient lattice is free and its torus map is a split projection after choosing a complement. A common zero of the restricted sections on the orbit thus lifts to a common zero of all the initial forms in \(T_N\text{.}\) Conversely, any common zero of those initial forms maps to a common zero on the orbit, since the monomial frame factors are invertible on the torus. Every nonzero direction belongs to the relative interior of a nonzero cone of the complete fan. Thus nondegeneracy is equivalent to excluding all boundary common zeros.
    The common-zero scheme of the sections on \(X\) is consequently supported inside \(T_N\) and equals \(Z\text{.}\) It is proper over \(k\text{,}\) since it is closed in \(X\text{,}\) and affine, since it is a closed subscheme of the affine torus. A proper affine scheme of finite type over a field is finite, proving the asserted finiteness.
    At each point of this finite scheme, the \(n\) local equations form a system of parameters in a regular local ring of dimension \(n\text{.}\) Such a ring is Cohen–Macaulay, so these parameters form a regular sequence. The zero-scheme intersection formula for sections of the line bundles \(\mathcal O(D_i)\) identifies its zero-cycle with \(D_1\cdots D_n\cap[X]\text{,}\) with coefficient equal to each local length. Taking degrees and applying Theorem 5.9 proves the formula. If the common-zero scheme is empty, the same zero-scheme formula gives zero on both sides.
    The classical toric treatment is [Ful93, Section 5.5]. The proof here isolates its characteristic-free intersection argument and its boundary condition. The assumption on the sum allows individual \(P_i\) to have smaller dimension; it does not assert that the mixed volume is positive. For example, a nonzero monomial as one equation has no torus zeros, and a point as one polytope makes the mixed volume zero.

    Corollary 5.12 Generic distinct roots over \(\C\).

    Let finite nonempty sets \(A_i\subset M\) have convex hulls \(P_i\text{,}\) with full-dimensional sum. Over \(\C\text{,}\) there is a nonempty Zariski open set of coefficient tuples for which the polynomials with these supports have exactly \(n!V_M(P_1,\ldots,P_n)\) distinct common roots in the torus. All these roots are simple.

    Proof.
    First require the coefficients at all vertices to be nonzero, so the Newton polytopes are the specified \(P_i\text{.}\) Their monomial linear systems are basepoint free on the common compactification: a minimizing vertex on each maximal cone gives a section nonvanishing on its chart. On a boundary orbit of dimension \(d\lt n\text{,}\) prescribing a common zero imposes one independent linear condition on each of the \(n\) separate coefficient spaces. The corresponding incidence space has dimension at most the parameter-space dimension plus \(d-n\text{.}\) Its image is contained in a proper closed subset. There are finitely many boundary orbits, so general coefficients give no boundary common zero.
    For simplicity of roots we invoke characteristic-zero generic transversality, equivalently Bertini for these basepoint-free linear systems on the smooth torus. It says that general members meet smoothly in the expected dimension, or have empty intersection. This is the additional input for reducedness; it is not a consequence of the intersection-number calculation. Together with boundary avoidance, the intersection is finite and reduced. The preceding theorem then counts its distinct points. See the generic form in [Ful93, Section 5.5].

    Example 5.13 Length cannot always be replaced by the number of points.

    In characteristic \(p\gt0\text{,}\) let \(f=x^p-a\) with \(a\in k^\times\text{.}\) Its Newton interval has lattice length \(p\text{,}\) and every nonzero-direction initial form is a monomial, so the boundary condition holds. Over the algebraically closed field, \(x^p-a=(x-b)^p\) for a unique nonzero \(b\text{.}\) The torus zero scheme has length \(p\) but only one point. Even general coefficients with this fixed support need not give simple roots in positive characteristic.

    Example 5.14 Three roots from two rectangles.

    Work over \(\C\text{,}\) with
    \begin{gather*} f=1+x+y+2xy,\\ g=1+3x^2+4y+5x^2y. \end{gather*}
    Their Newton polytopes are \(P_1=[0,1]^2\) and \(P_2=[0,2]\times[0,1]\text{.}\) Since
    \begin{equation*} \vol_M(tP_1+sP_2)=(t+2s)(t+s) =t^2+3ts+2s^2, \end{equation*}
    the coefficient convention of Theorem 5.9 gives \(2!V_M(P_1,P_2)=3\text{.}\)
    For a direction with both coordinates nonzero, each initial form is a nonzero vertex monomial. The remaining directions give, after discarding invertible monomial factors, the four pairs
    \begin{gather*} 1+y,\ 1+4y;\qquad 1+2y,\ 3+5y;\\ 1+x,\ 1+3x^2;\qquad 1+2x,\ 4+5x^2. \end{gather*}
    None has a common torus zero. Substitution of the root of the first polynomial in each pair into the second gives respectively \(-3,1/2,4,21/4\text{.}\) Thus the fixed-coefficient theorem applies, not only the generic corollary.
    We can check the three simple roots by elimination. The first equation gives
    \begin{equation*} y=-\frac{1+x}{1+2x}, \end{equation*}
    and eliminating \(y\) from the second yields
    \begin{equation*} (1+3x^2)(1+2x)-(4+5x^2)(1+x) =x^3-2x^2-2x-3=:h(x). \end{equation*}
    Its discriminant is \(-507\text{,}\) so its three complex roots are distinct. Moreover,
    \begin{equation*} h(0)=-3,\qquad h(-1)=-4,\qquad h(-1/2)=-21/8. \end{equation*}
    No root has \(x=0\text{,}\) makes the numerator of \(y\) zero, or makes its denominator zero. Every root therefore gives a point of \((\C^\times)^2\text{,}\) and the common-zero algebra is \(\C[x]/(h)\text{.}\) This proves directly that the system has exactly three simple torus roots, agreeing with its mixed volume.
    The ambient compactification and its line bundles are toric; the two polynomials and their common-zero scheme are unrestricted objects of the stated supports. The result is consequently a concrete example of a toric calculation proving a theorem about general polynomial systems.

    Section 5.5 What changes for a singular complete variety

    For a singular variety, use \(A_j(X)\) for cycles and \(A^i_{\mathrm{op}}(X)\) for operational Chow cohomology. An operational class is a compatible system of operations on Chow groups of schemes mapping to \(X\text{;}\) its product is composition. The construction belongs to the supporting intersection theory. Smoothness identifies it with the usual Chow ring; singularity requires retaining the distinction.

    Definition 5.16 A balanced weight on a fan.

    For a complete \(n\)-dimensional fan, a codimension-\(i\) Minkowski weight is an integer-valued function \(c\) on its \((n-i)\)-dimensional cones satisfying, for every \((n-i-1)\)-dimensional cone \(\tau\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{\sigma\supset\tau\atop\dim\sigma=n-i}c(\sigma)u_{\sigma/\tau}=0\quad\text{in }(N/N_\tau)_{\R}. \end{equation*}
    At \(i=n\) there are no balancing conditions. The primitive vectors have the same quotient-lattice meaning as in the cycle relations.
    For a complete toric variety, operational Chow cohomology identifies with these weights; its product admits a combinatorial displacement rule. We use this as a reference theorem, with the ring structure supplied by the operational theory, rather than declaring a product on singular cycles. See [FS97, Sections 2–3; Theorems 2.1 and 3.2 in the arXiv version] and [Tot14, Theorem 2] for the proper linear-variety duality that underlies the algebraic comparison.
    The degree reversal is visible in a surface fan: a codimension-one weight assigns numbers to rays, and balancing at the zero cone says that their primitive-vector weighted sum is zero. In the fan of \(\P^2\text{,}\) the constant weight \(1\) satisfies this condition because \(u_1+u_2+u_3=0\text{.}\) It corresponds to the hyperplane class. This formulation will also make contact with balanced tropical cycles; that connection needs its own definitions and does not change which Chow theory is being used here.

    Section 5.6 An interface: the Chow ring of a matroid

    The same presentation leads to a result about arbitrary matroids, including ones that cannot be realized by vectors over any field. This section assumes the elementary language of matroid rank and flats. It isolates the toric construction and states the additional Hodge-theoretic input; a development of matroid theory belongs elsewhere.
    Let \(\mathcal M\) be a loopless matroid of rank \(d+1\) on a nonempty finite set \(E\text{.}\) In
    \begin{equation*} N=\Z^E/\Z\mathbf1,\qquad \mathbf1=\sum_{i\in E}e_i, \end{equation*}
    put \(e_F=\sum_{i\in F}e_i\text{.}\) The fine Bergman fan \(\Sigma_{\mathcal M}\) has a ray generated by \(e_F\) for each nonempty proper flat \(F\text{,}\) and its cones are generated by the rays in chains of such flats. Its maximal cones have dimension \(d\text{.}\) This is the fine fan, retaining every flat ray; coarser fan structures need not have this incidence presentation.

    Proposition 5.17 A toric realization of the matroid ring.

    The fan \(\Sigma_{\mathcal M}\) is smooth in \(N\text{.}\) Its associated toric variety has dimension \(|E|-1\text{,}\) and its integral Chow ring is
    \begin{equation*} A^*(\mathcal M):=\Z[x_F:\varnothing\ne F\ne E\text{ a flat}]/(I_{\mathcal M}+J_{\mathcal M}), \end{equation*}
    where \(I_{\mathcal M}\) is generated by \(x_Fx_G\) for incomparable flats, and
    \begin{equation*} J_{\mathcal M}=\left(\sum_{F\ni i}x_F-\sum_{F\ni j}x_F:i,j\in E\right). \end{equation*}
    Thus \(A^*(X_{\Sigma_{\mathcal M}})\cong A^*(\mathcal M)\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    A chain \(F_1\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq F_s\) partitions \(E\) into the nonempty blocks \(F_1,F_2\setminus F_1,\ldots,F_s\setminus F_{s-1},E\setminus F_s\text{.}\) In each block, its sum of coordinate vectors, together with all but one of those coordinate vectors, is an integral basis of its coordinate lattice. Replace the block sums successively by \(e_{F_1},\ldots,e_{F_s},\mathbf1\text{;}\) the change is triangular with determinant one. Modulo \(\mathbf1\) the chain vectors therefore form part of a basis of \(N\text{.}\) The chain cones form a subfan of the fan of all chains of proper subsets, the braid fan, so this also realizes them as a fan.
    Apply Theorem 5.4. A collection of flats lies in a cone exactly when it is a chain, so the minimal nonfaces are incomparable pairs. The dual lattice is \(\{(a_i)\in\Z^E:\sum a_i=0\}\text{,}\) generated by coordinate differences. Their pairings with \(e_F\) give exactly the displayed linear relations.
    The top nonzero degree of this ring is \(d\text{,}\) rather than the ambient toric dimension in general. There is a combinatorial isomorphism \(\deg:A^d(\mathcal M)\to\Z\) sending every full flag product \(x_{F_1}\cdots x_{F_d}\) to \(1\text{;}\) see [AHK18, Definition 5.9 and Proposition 5.10]. If \(d+1<|E|\text{,}\) the fan has smaller-dimensional support and the toric variety is not complete. This degree is then an additional combinatorial operation, not proper pushforward from that variety. For the free matroid, the fan is instead the complete permutohedral fan.
    Here is the additional theorem of Adiprasito–Huh–Katz, [AHK18, Theorem 1.4]. Work in \(A^*(\mathcal M)_\R\text{.}\) Choose numbers \(c_I\) on proper nonempty subsets, setting \(c_\varnothing=c_E=0\text{,}\) such that
    \begin{equation*} c_I+c_J>c_{I\cap J}+c_{I\cup J} \end{equation*}
    whenever \(I,J\) are incomparable. For \(\ell=\sum_Fc_Fx_F\) and \(0\leq q\leq d/2\text{,}\) multiplication by \(\ell^{d-2q}\) is an isomorphism \(A^q\to A^{d-q}\text{,}\) and
    \begin{equation*} (a,b)\longmapsto(-1)^q\deg(a\ell^{d-2q}b) \end{equation*}
    is positive definite on \(\ker(\ell^{d-2q+1}:A^q\to A^{d-q+1})\text{.}\) These are hard Lefschetz and the Hodge–Riemann relations for the matroid ring. They require a new theorem: projective Hodge theory cannot simply be applied to a smooth toric variety with this generally noncomplete fan.
    For any \(j\in E\text{,}\) the classes
    \begin{equation*} \alpha=\sum_{F\ni j}x_F,\qquad\beta=\sum_{F\not\ni j}x_F \end{equation*}
    are independent of \(j\) and are limits of the strictly submodular classes. Write the reduced characteristic polynomial as
    \begin{equation*} \overline\chi_{\mathcal M}(t)=\chi_{\mathcal M}(t)/(t-1)=\sum_{i=0}^d(-1)^i\mu^it^{d-i}. \end{equation*}
    The identity \(\mu^i=\deg(\alpha^{d-i}\beta^i)\text{,}\) together with Hodge–Riemann and its limiting inequalities, proves \((\mu^i)^2\geq\mu^{i-1}\mu^{i+1}\text{;}\) see [AHK18, Definition 5.7, Proposition 9.5, and Section 9.2]. This is a concrete passage from a toric presentation to a theorem for all loopless matroids.

    Example 5.18 Rank three on four elements.

    For the uniform matroid \(U_{3,4}\text{,}\) the proper nonempty flats are the four singletons and the six pairs. Write their variables as \(a_i,b_{ij}\text{.}\) A full flag is \(\{i\}\subset\{i,j\}\text{,}\) so \(\deg(a_ib_{ij})=1\text{.}\) Multiplying the linear relations by \(a_i\) and \(b_{ij}\) gives
    \begin{equation*} \deg(a_i^2)=-2,\qquad\deg(b_{ij}^2)=-1. \end{equation*}
    All other products vanish unless the flats are nested. Taking \(j=1\) in the preceding definitions now yields
    \begin{equation*} \deg\alpha^2=1,\qquad\deg\alpha\beta=3,\qquad\deg\beta^2=3. \end{equation*}
    These recover \(\overline\chi_{U_{3,4}}(t)=t^2-3t+3\) and its inequality \(3^2\geq1\cdot3\text{.}\) The fan is two-dimensional in a rank-three lattice, and its toric variety is a smooth noncomplete threefold. The ring and its degree have the dimension of the matroid fan.

    Section 5.7 A finite complex for each character

    Let \(D=\sum a_\rho D_\rho\) be an invariant Weil divisor. Cover \(X_\Sigma\) by the charts of its maximal cones \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_s\text{.}\) Every nonempty finite intersection is the affine chart of their cone intersection. The Čech complex for this cover computes the cohomology of the quasi-coherent sheaf \(\mathcal O(D)\text{,}\) because these intersections are affine and have no higher quasi-coherent cohomology. This is the affine-cover acyclicity input; see [CLS11, Section 9.1, equations (9.1.1)–(9.1.2)].
    Throughout this character complex, a subscript \(m\) records the coefficient exponent of \(\chi^m\text{.}\) For the natural action on rational sections, \((t\cdot s)(p)=s(t^{-1}p)\text{,}\) its action weight is \(-m\text{.}\) Thus the displayed grading is the opposite of the section-action weight; compare Section 9.1.

    Proposition 5.19 The character complex.

    Fix \(m\in M\text{.}\) For \(I=\{i_0<\cdots<i_p\}\text{,}\) put \(\sigma_I=\bigcap_{i\in I}\sigma_i\text{.}\) Then the coefficient-exponent-\(m\) Čech complex has terms
    \begin{equation} C^p(D)_m=\bigoplus_{|I|=p+1\atop\langle m,u_\rho\rangle\geq-a_\rho\ \mathrm{for\ all}\ \rho\in\sigma_I(1)} k.\tag{5.4} \end{equation}
    Its differentials are alternating restriction maps: each nonzero restriction is the identity on the monomial \(\chi^m\text{.}\) Its cohomology is \(H^p(X,\mathcal O(D))_m\text{,}\) and the full cohomology is the direct sum over \(m\in M\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    The local character-basis formula (3.3) makes the coefficient-exponent component on each intersection either \(0\) or \(k\chi^m\text{,}\) with the stated inequality test. Restriction preserves the rational function \(\chi^m\text{;}\) weakening cone inequalities cannot invalidate a section already present. Thus the Čech maps preserve coefficient exponents and have precisely the displayed components. The finite Čech complex splits as a direct sum of character complexes. Direct sums of vector spaces are exact, so their cohomology splits as well.
    The complex is finite for each character, although the set of characters is infinite. A complete variety has finite-dimensional coherent cohomology, so only finitely many character components survive. For a nonproper variety no such conclusion follows merely from finiteness of each individual complex.

    Example 5.20 All line-bundle cohomology on the projective line.

    For \(D=a_+D_++a_-D_-\text{,}\) a coefficient exponent \(m\in\Z\) occurs on the positive chart if \(m\geq-a_+\text{,}\) on the negative chart if \(m\leq a_-\text{,}\) and on their intersection for every \(m\text{.}\) The character complex is
    \begin{equation*} (k\ \mathrm{if}\ m\geq-a_+)\ \oplus\ (k\ \mathrm{if}\ m\leq a_-)\longrightarrow k, \end{equation*}
    with differential the difference of restrictions. A zero-th cohomology class occurs exactly when both inequalities hold; a first cohomology class occurs exactly when neither holds. If \(d=a_++a_-\text{,}\) counting these integer intervals gives
    \begin{equation*} h^0(\P^1,\mathcal O(D))=\max(d+1,0),\qquad h^1(\P^1,\mathcal O(D))=\max(-d-1,0). \end{equation*}
    The calculation includes \(d=-1\text{,}\) when both groups vanish, and uses no characteristic-zero argument.

    Example 5.21 The same calculation on the projective plane.

    Use \(D=dD_3\) and a coefficient exponent \(m=(p,q)\text{.}\) Write the three ray inequalities as
    \begin{equation*} A:\ p\geq0,\qquad B:\ q\geq0,\qquad C:\ p+q\leq d. \end{equation*}
    The three maximal charts test the pairs \(AB,BC,CA\text{;}\) the pairwise intersections test \(A,B,C\text{;}\) the triple intersection tests none. If exactly \(r\) of the three inequalities hold, the dimensions of the character complex are
    \begin{equation*} \binom r2\longrightarrow r\longrightarrow1. \end{equation*}
    For \(r=1\) the last map is an isomorphism; for \(r=2\) the sequence \(k\to k^2\to k\) is exact; for \(r=3\) it is the simplex complex with only \(H^0=k\text{.}\) For \(r=0\) it has only \(H^2=k\text{.}\) The alternating signs give these assertions over every field, including characteristic two.
    Thus \(H^1(\P^2,\mathcal O(d))=0\) for all integers \(d\text{.}\) The zero-th group counts \(p,q\geq0\) with \(p+q\leq d\text{.}\) The second group counts \(p,q<0\) with \(p+q>d\text{.}\) Consequently
    \begin{align*} h^0(\P^2,\mathcal O(d))=\begin{cases}\binom{d+2}{2}&d\geq0,\\0&d<0,\end{cases}\\ h^2(\P^2,\mathcal O(d))=\begin{cases}\binom{-d-1}{2}&d\leq-3,\\0&d\geq-2.\end{cases} \end{align*}
    The character complex explains these formulas directly and exposes which inequalities account for each surviving class.

    Section 5.8 Convexity and Demazure vanishing

    There is a useful topological interpretation of the character complex for a Cartier divisor. For each \(m\in M\text{,}\) put
    \begin{equation*} V_{D,m}=\{u\in|\Sigma|:\langle m,u\rangle<\psi_D(u)\}. \end{equation*}
    The character complex can be compared with a relative cochain complex on the fan, giving
    \begin{equation*} H^p(X,\mathcal O(D))_m\cong\widetilde H^{p-1}(V_{D,m};k). \end{equation*}
    Here the right side is reduced singular cohomology of a subset of the real fan space, with coefficients in \(k\text{,}\) using \(\widetilde H^{-1}(\varnothing;k)=k\text{.}\) It is an auxiliary combinatorial-topological model for algebraic coherent cohomology; it does not mean that \(X(k)\) has been given a complex topology. We use the comparison as stated in [CLS11, Theorem 9.1.3(b)]. Its proof compares the same finite character complexes and works over the coefficient field \(k\text{.}\)

    Theorem 5.22 Vanishing for a nef toric line bundle.

    Let \(X_\Sigma\) be complete and \(D\) a nef Cartier divisor. Then \(H^p(X,\mathcal O(D))=0\) for every \(p>0\text{.}\) Smoothness and characteristic zero are not required.

    Proof.
    The nef criterion in the positivity chapter makes \(D\) globally generated. Its support function is concave in our sign convention, and
    \begin{equation*} \psi_D(u)=\min_{\sigma\ \mathrm{maximal}}\langle m_\sigma,u\rangle\qquad(u\in N_\R). \end{equation*}
    Thus \(V_{D,m}\) is the intersection of the finitely many open half-spaces
    \begin{equation*} \langle m-m_\sigma,u\rangle<0. \end{equation*}
    It is convex, and is either empty or contractible. Its reduced cohomology in every nonnegative degree is zero. The preceding comparison therefore makes every weight space of every positive cohomology group zero. The direct-sum decomposition proves the claim.
    Compare [CLS11, Theorem 9.2.3] and the characteristic-free vanishing result in [Fuj07, Corollary 1.7]. The proof explains why this statement survives in positive characteristic: it uses a character decomposition and convexity. It also explains why the nef hypothesis is substantive: the negative line bundles in the projective-line example have nonzero first cohomology.
    Chapter 17 computes Euler characteristics from this intersection ring and relates Serre duality to interior lattice points. Its adjoint vanishing argument specifies the additional step needed for \(\mathcal O(K_X+D)\text{.}\)

    Section 5.9 Complex topology and fixed-point calculations

    In this section only, let \(k=\C\text{.}\) For a smooth complete toric variety, the cycle map identifies \(A^i(X)\) with \(H^{2i}(X(\C),\Z)\text{,}\) and odd singular cohomology vanishes. This is the integral complete smooth comparison, not a statement about arbitrary toric varieties; see [CLS11, Theorem 12.4.4 and equation (12.5.9)]. In particular, the cycle degree \(i\) corresponds to topological degree \(2i\text{.}\)
    For a smooth projective toric variety, the fixed points are the maximal cones. Write \(S_T=H_T^*(\mathrm{pt},\Q)=\operatorname{Sym}_{\Q}(M\otimes\Q)\text{,}\) assigning a character topological degree \(2\text{.}\) Equivariant localization computes pushforward after nonzero character classes have been inverted:
    \begin{equation} \int_X\alpha=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma(n)}\frac{\alpha|_{x_\sigma}}{e_T(T_{x_\sigma}X)}.\tag{5.5} \end{equation}
    We invoke the localization theorem, including its pushforward and coefficient localization, rather than deriving it from ordinary Chow theory. An introduction with these constructions is [And12, Theorem 2.10].

    Proposition 5.23 The local weights and the sign of a line bundle.

    If \(\sigma\) has ray generators forming a basis of \(N\text{,}\) and dual basis \(m_1,\ldots,m_n\text{,}\) then its tangent weights are \(m_1,\ldots,m_n\) and
    \begin{equation*} e_T(T_{x_\sigma}X)=\prod_{j=1}^nm_j. \end{equation*}
    For the invariant divisor \(D\) with local frame \(\chi^{m_\sigma}\text{,}\) the natural linearization has fiber weight \(-m_\sigma\) at that fixed point.

    Proof.
    The chart coordinates are \(z_j=\chi^{m_j}\text{,}\) and the action on points satisfies \(z_j(t\cdot p)=\chi^{m_j}(t)z_j(p)\text{.}\) Its derivative at the origin has these weights. For the divisorial sheaf, the induced action on rational sections is \((t\cdot s)(p)=s(t^{-1}p)\text{.}\) Hence the frame transforms by \(\chi^{-m_\sigma}(t)\text{,}\) giving the opposite sign on the fiber. The Euler class is the product of the tangent weights.

    Example 5.24 Two checks on signs and denominators.

    For \(\P^1\text{,}\) let \(u\) denote its standard character. The tangent weights at the positive and negative cones are \(u,-u\text{.}\) For \(D=D_-\text{,}\) the local support data are \(0,1\text{,}\) so the fiber weights are \(0,-u\text{.}\) Localization gives
    \begin{equation*} \int_{\P^1}c_1^T(\mathcal O(D))=\frac0u+\frac{-u}{-u}=1. \end{equation*}
    In contrast, \(\int_{\P^1}1=1/u+1/(-u)=0\text{,}\) consistent with its negative output degree.
    On \(\P^2\text{,}\) use characters \(u,v\) and \(D=D_3\text{.}\) The three tangent Euler classes are \(uv\text{,}\) \((-u)(v-u)\text{,}\) and \((-v)(u-v)\text{,}\) and the fiber weights are \(0,-u,-v\text{.}\) Thus
    \begin{equation*} \int_{\P^2}(c_1^T(\mathcal O(D)))^2=0+\frac{u^2}{(-u)(v-u)}+\frac{v^2}{(-v)(u-v)}=1. \end{equation*}
    Individual fractions depend on equivariant parameters; their sum recovers the ordinary degree already obtained from the Chow ring.
    For a nonproper target, ordinary pushforward to a point is not supplied by this formula. An equivariant theory may instead define a localized expression when its fixed loci are proper, but that definition and any nonequivariant limit need separate justification. In Gromov–Witten theory the relevant integrals occur on moduli spaces with virtual classes; the tangent-weight calculation is an input to that theory, not a replacement for it.
