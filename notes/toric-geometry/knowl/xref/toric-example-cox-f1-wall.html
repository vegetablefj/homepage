Example 6.12 Crossing a wall from the first Hirzebruch surface.

Set \(a=1\) in the previous example. The four weights are
\begin{equation*} w_1=w_3=(1,0),\qquad w_2=(0,1),\qquad w_4=(1,1). \end{equation*}
The effective quadrant has two full-dimensional chambers separated by the diagonal ray. Choose characters
\begin{equation*} \theta_+=(2,1),\qquad\theta_0=(1,1),\qquad\theta_-=(1,2). \end{equation*}
The plus sign names the chamber of the Hirzebruch surface; it is not a convention about a positive or negative divisor.
🔗
The orbit-cone test gives
\begin{equation*} U_{\theta_+}=\{(x_1,x_3)\ne(0,0),\ (x_2,x_4)\ne(0,0)\}=\widehat{\mathbb F_1}. \end{equation*}
Hence \(Y_{\theta_+}=\mathbb F_1\text{.}\) On the other side it gives
\begin{equation*} U_{\theta_-}=\{x_2\ne0,\ (x_1,x_3,x_4)\ne(0,0,0)\}. \end{equation*}
Normalize \(x_2=1\) using the second scaling. The remaining scaling acts with weight one on \(x_1,x_3,x_4/x_2\text{,}\) so its quotient is \(\P^2\text{.}\) This normalization is an isomorphism of quotient constructions, not a division by a group order.
🔗
At the wall,
\begin{equation*} U_{\theta_0}=D(x_4)\cup D(x_1x_2)\cup D(x_3x_2). \end{equation*}
The entire section ring is
\begin{equation*} R_{\theta_0}=k[x_1x_2,x_3x_2,x_4], \end{equation*}
a polynomial ring with its three displayed generators in summand degree one. Indeed, a monomial of class \((\ell,\ell)\) satisfies \(b_1+b_3+b_4=\ell=b_2+b_4\text{,}\) so \(b_2=b_1+b_3\text{.}\) It is uniquely a product of the three displayed monomials. Thus \(Y_{\theta_0}=\P^2\text{.}\)
🔗
The inclusions of the two chamber opens into the wall open induce
\begin{equation*} \mathbb F_1\longrightarrow\P^2\longleftarrow\P^2. \end{equation*}
On the first Cox open, the left map is
\begin{equation*} (x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)\longmapsto[x_1x_2:x_3x_2:x_4]. \end{equation*}
These three expressions have the same class \(F+S\text{,}\) and they never vanish simultaneously there. The divisor \(D_2\text{,}\) given by \(x_2=0\text{,}\) is contracted to \([0:0:1]\text{.}\) Away from that divisor, \(x_2\) is invertible and the expression agrees with the quotient coordinates on the other chamber. The right-hand arrow is an isomorphism.
🔗
The fan confirms the modification. Removing the ray \(u_2=(0,1)\) leaves the fan of \(\P^2\text{,}\) and \(u_2=u_1+u_3\) subdivides its smooth cone \(\Cone(u_1,u_3)\text{.}\) By the affine blowup calculation in Example 2.23, the left map is the blowup of that fixed point. This is a divisorial wall crossing, not a flip. The graded ring stayed fixed; the semistable set and quotient changed.
