Proposition 14.4 Integration by parts at lattice distance one.

If \(f\) is continuously differentiable on a neighborhood of \(P\text{,}\) then
\begin{equation} \int_{\partial P}f\,d\sigma =\int_P\bigl(nf+\langle x,\nabla f\rangle\bigr)\,dx.\tag{14.1} \end{equation}
The same identity holds for continuous piecewise affine \(f\text{,}\) using its almost-everywhere gradient. In particular,
\begin{equation*} \int_{\partial P}d\sigma=nV_P. \end{equation*}

🔗

Proof.
The outward unit normal to \(F_\rho\) is \(\nu_\rho=-u_\rho/\|u_\rho\|\text{.}\) Since \(\langle x,u_\rho\rangle=-1\) there, the vector field \(x\) satisfies \(\langle x,\nu_\rho\rangle=1/\|u_\rho\|\text{.}\) Apply the Euclidean divergence theorem to \(f(x)x\text{.}\) Its boundary flux is exactly \(f\,dA/\|u_\rho\|=f\,d\sigma\text{,}\) while its divergence is \(nf+\langle x,\nabla f\rangle\text{.}\) The coordinate volume is \(dx\) by our lattice normalization.
🔗
For a continuous piecewise affine function, subdivide \(P\) into finitely many polyhedral cells on which it is affine. Apply the same identity cellwise using the vector field \(f(x)x\text{.}\) The two fluxes on every internal wall cancel, because the normals are opposite and the vector field is continuous. The remaining walls have measure zero in the volume integral, so the resulting interior term uses the almost-everywhere gradient. Taking \(f=1\) proves the last identity.
