    Chapter 0 Scope, conventions, and reading routes

    Toric geometry gives a precise passage between algebraic geometry and finite combinatorial data. A cone determines a semigroup algebra; a fan organizes affine charts; divisors become piecewise linear functions and inequalities. The purpose of these notes is to establish this dictionary, explain why its translations hold, and make it usable in calculations. We also ask how toric constructions enter problems about varieties that are not themselves toric.
    The opening chapters develop the affine, fan, and divisor dictionaries before their uses in positivity, intersection theory, and quotients. Each chapter states its scope and identifies any substantial external input. A proposed interface with another subject is a reading route, rather than an assertion that the relevant theory has already been developed here.

    Section 0.1 Questions, background, and dependencies

    Our basic questions are concrete. How does a cone specify a variety? Which geometric properties can be read from its lattice points? How do local constructions glue? How are sections, intersections, and birational modifications computed from finite data? A useful answer must specify the lattice, the hypotheses on the variety, and the direction of every translation.
    We assume familiarity with commutative rings, ideals, localization, polynomial and Laurent polynomial rings, and elementary linear algebra. Readers should know affine varieties or affine schemes and the correspondence between ring homomorphisms and morphisms of affine schemes. The affine construction explains the semigroup algebras and convex geometry it uses. Gluing requires schemes and open immersions; divisors require normal varieties, codimension-one points, and invertible sheaves. Sheaf cohomology and intersection theory enter only when the corresponding chapters use them.
    These dependencies are not all prerequisites for beginning the notes. A reader can learn the affine dictionary first and supply the scheme-theoretic background when gluing becomes necessary. Longer supporting theories have their own sources: for example, a use of Chow groups will specify the operation and its hypotheses without interrupting the toric argument with a separate development of intersection theory.
    Examples are part of the argument. Affine spaces and projective spaces calibrate the notation; a simple singular affine surface separates smoothness from weaker combinatorial conditions; Hirzebruch surfaces connect fans, divisors, positivity, and contractions. Noncomplete examples keep the distinction between a variety and a compactification visible. Whenever a calculation is offered as evidence for a general statement, the statement still requires a proof.

    Section 0.2 The ground field and the objects under study

    Unless a different hypothesis is stated, \(k\) is an algebraically closed field of arbitrary characteristic. A variety over \(k\) is an integral separated scheme of finite type over \(k\text{.}\) In particular, it is nonempty, reduced, and irreducible. All morphisms are morphisms over \(k\text{.}\) Rings are commutative with identity, ring homomorphisms preserve the identity, and \(\N=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\text{.}\)
    A toric variety in these notes is normal. Neither smoothness nor completeness nor projectivity is part of the definition. Normality is imposed on the objects, rather than inferred from the existence of a dense torus. When a nonnormal example is discussed, it is explicitly called a nonnormal toric variety and serves as a comparison with our main category.1
    The fan classification of normal toric varieties follows from the affine construction, gluing, and the invariant affine-neighborhood theorem specified in the fan chapter. That last theorem is a substantial external input. In particular, the use of normality in the classification must be distinguished from the elementary construction of an algebra generated by monomials.
    The algebraic foundations do not assume \(k=\C\text{.}\) A passage to complex topology, a symplectic form, or a Gromov–Witten theory will state its additional assumptions at the point of use. Likewise, statements involving quotient singularities or finite group schemes require their own characteristic hypotheses. A lattice calculation over \(\Z\) does not by itself remove these issues.

    Section 0.3 The chosen torus and its morphisms

    Definition 0.1 Toric variety.

    A toric variety consists of a normal \(k\)-variety \(X\text{,}\) a split algebraic torus \(T\cong\Gm^n\text{,}\) a dense open immersion \(j:T\hookrightarrow X\text{,}\) and an algebraic action \(\alpha:T\times X\to X\) whose restriction to \(T\times T\) is multiplication in \(T\text{.}\) We identify \(T\) with its image under \(j\text{,}\) while retaining its group structure and its role as the specified open torus.
    The chosen torus is part of the data. The assertion that an underlying variety admits some toric structure is weaker than the specification of the structure being used. A basis identifying \(T\) with \(\Gm^n\) is not part of the data. Rank zero is allowed: \(\Gm^0=\Spec k\text{,}\) so a point is a toric variety.

    Definition 0.2 Toric morphism.

    For toric varieties \((X,T_X)\) and \((Y,T_Y)\text{,}\) a toric morphism is a morphism \(f:X\to Y\) whose restriction to \(T_X\) maps into \(T_Y\) and is a homomorphism of algebraic groups \(\varphi:T_X\to T_Y\text{,}\) with respect to which \(f\) is equivariant. Thus
    \begin{equation*} f(t\cdot x)=\varphi(t)\cdot f(x). \end{equation*}
    This identity denotes equality of morphisms \(T_X\times X\to Y\text{,}\) not merely an identity on a selected set of points.
    For our varieties the equivariance identity also follows from the condition on the restriction to the dense torus: the two morphisms agree on \(T_X\times T_X\text{,}\) and the target is separated. We include equivariance in the definition to display the compatibility that the combinatorial description must preserve. This convention agrees with [CLS11, Definition 3.3.3 and Theorem 3.3.4].
    An equivariant map need not be toric in this sense. If \(a\in T(k)\text{,}\) translation \(t\mapsto at\) is equivariant for the identity homomorphism \(T\to T\text{;}\) when \(a\ne1\text{,}\) its restriction to the torus is not a group homomorphism. More generally, equivariance alone does not require the dense torus of the source to map into the dense torus of the target: an equivariant constant map can have its value at a fixed boundary point.2
    A toric isomorphism is an isomorphism in this category, including the chosen torus data. An isomorphism of underlying varieties is not automatically a toric isomorphism. Ordinary morphisms of varieties remain available, but they will not be identified with maps of fans unless the required toric compatibility has been checked.

    Section 0.4 Characters, one-parameter subgroups, and variance

    A lattice is a free abelian group of finite rank. For a lattice \(N\text{,}\) its dual lattice is \(M=\Hom_{\Z}(N,\Z)\text{.}\) We write \(N_{\R}=N\otimes_{\Z}\R\) and \(N_{\Q}=N\otimes_{\Z}\Q\text{,}\) and similarly for \(M\text{.}\) The pairing is written in the order
    \begin{equation*} \langle\ ,\ \rangle:M\times N\longrightarrow\Z,\qquad (m,u)\longmapsto\langle m,u\rangle, \end{equation*}
    and extended to real or rational vector spaces as required.
    For a split torus \(T\text{,}\) the character lattice is \(M=\Hom(T,\Gm)\) and the lattice of one-parameter subgroups is \(N=\Hom(\Gm,T)\text{,}\) where both hom-sets mean homomorphisms of algebraic groups over \(k\text{.}\) Their group laws come from multiplication in the tori, but we write the lattice operations additively. The character associated to \(m\in M\) is denoted by \(\chi^m\text{,}\) and the one-parameter subgroup associated to \(u\in N\) by \(\lambda_u\text{.}\) The pairing is characterized by
    \begin{equation*} \chi^m\circ\lambda_u:\Gm\longrightarrow\Gm,\qquad t\longmapsto t^{\langle m,u\rangle}. \end{equation*}
    These lattices are dual, and \(\dim T=\rk M=\rk N\text{;}\) see [CLS11, Section 1.1].
    The torus associated to \(N\) is denoted by \(T_N=\Spec k[M]\text{.}\) Here \(k[M]\) is the group algebra with basis elements \(\chi^m\) and multiplication \(\chi^m\chi^{m'}=\chi^{m+m'}\text{.}\) A basis of \(M\) identifies this algebra with a Laurent polynomial ring. This identification supplies coordinates, while the group algebra itself is independent of the basis.
    A homomorphism \(\varphi:T_{N_1}\to T_{N_2}\) induces a map \(\varphi_N:N_1\to N_2\) by composition of one-parameter subgroups, and a map \(\varphi_M:M_2\to M_1\) by pullback of characters. Their directions and compatibility are
    \begin{equation*} \varphi^*(\chi^{m_2})=\chi^{\varphi_M(m_2)},\qquad \langle\varphi_M(m_2),u_1\rangle=\langle m_2,\varphi_N(u_1)\rangle. \end{equation*}
    Fan maps will follow the direction of \(\varphi_N\text{;}\) coordinate-ring maps follow the opposite direction. We keep these maps distinct even when a choice of bases represents them by a matrix and its transpose.

    Section 0.5 Cones, fans, and their ambient lattices

    For \(u_1,\ldots,u_s\in N_{\R}\text{,}\) write
    \begin{equation*} \Cone(u_1,\ldots,u_s)=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^s a_i u_i:a_i\in\R_{\geq0}\right\}. \end{equation*}
    The cone generated by the empty list is \(\{0\}\text{.}\) A cone is rational with respect to \(N\) if it has a finite generating set in \(N\text{,}\) and strongly convex if \(\sigma\cap(-\sigma)=\{0\}\text{.}\) Unless explicitly stated otherwise, a cone used to build a toric variety is rational, polyhedral, and strongly convex. General polyhedral cones also occur in duality arguments and are named as such.
    We do not require \(\sigma\) to span \(N_{\R}\text{.}\) Its dimension means the dimension of its real linear span, while the ambient lattice retains its specified rank. The same drawn cone in ambient lattices of different rank is different toric input. In particular, the zero cone in a positive-rank lattice must not be confused with the zero cone in the rank-zero lattice.
    The dual cone and annihilator are
    \begin{equation*} \sigma^\vee=\{m\in M_{\R}:\langle m,u\rangle\geq0\text{ for every }u\in\sigma\},\qquad \sigma^\perp=\{m\in M_{\R}:\langle m,u\rangle=0\text{ for every }u\in\sigma\}. \end{equation*}
    A face of \(\sigma\) is a set \(\sigma\cap m^\perp\) for some \(m\in\sigma^\vee\text{.}\) We write \(\tau\preceq\sigma\) for this relation and include \(\sigma\) itself as a face. A ray is a one-dimensional strongly convex cone. Its primitive generator \(u_\rho\in N\) is the unique generator for which \(\rho\cap N=\N u_\rho\text{.}\) The lattice, rather than a Euclidean choice of length, determines primitiveness.
    A fan \(\Sigma\) in \(N_{\R}\) is a finite collection of rational strongly convex polyhedral cones that contains every face of each of its cones and for which the intersection of any two cones is a face of each. We write \(\Sigma(d)\) for its set of \(d\)-dimensional cones and
    \begin{equation*} |\Sigma|=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\sigma \end{equation*}
    for its support. A fan is called complete when \(|\Sigma|=N_{\R}\text{.}\) The relation between this condition and properness is a geometric theorem stated and explained in the fan chapter, with a reference to its valuative proof.
    All fans representing toric varieties here are nonempty, hence contain the zero cone. We occasionally permit the empty collection as a formal empty fan; its associated scheme is the empty scheme, which is not a variety under our convention. The fan \(\{\{0\}\}\) is different: it gives the torus \(T_N\text{.}\) Finiteness of a fan is part of the convention and will correspond to the finite-type setting.
    A cone and its ambient lattice are recorded together. When passing to a subspace or a quotient, we will name the new lattice and check any torsion or saturation issue. A real-linear picture alone does not specify the integral structure needed for the corresponding variety.

    Section 0.6 Divisor signs and polyhedral inequalities

    This section fixes the notation for the later divisor dictionary; it does not replace its construction and proof. For a toric variety built from a fan \(\Sigma\text{,}\) the invariant prime divisor corresponding to a ray \(\rho\in\Sigma(1)\) will be denoted by \(D_\rho\text{.}\) Our character and valuation conventions give
    \begin{equation} \divisor(\chi^m)=\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma(1)}\langle m,u_\rho\rangle D_\rho.\tag{0.1} \end{equation}
    The coefficient formula will be proved in the divisor chapter; it is the sign to use when comparing our later formulas with another source. See [CLS11, Proposition 4.1.2].
    For an invariant Weil divisor \(D=\sum_\rho a_\rho D_\rho\) with integral coefficients, define its associated polyhedron by
    \begin{equation} P_D=\{m\in M_{\R}:\langle m,u_\rho\rangle\geq-a_\rho\text{ for every }\rho\in\Sigma(1)\}.\tag{0.2} \end{equation}
    This definition does not assert that \(P_D\) is nonempty or bounded. In particular, the word polytope will be used only when boundedness has been specified. Rational or real divisor coefficients give the same system of inequalities over the corresponding coefficient field.
    If \(D\) is Cartier, our support function is denoted by \(\psi_D:|\Sigma|\to\R\) and uses the normalization
    \begin{equation} \psi_D(u_\rho)=-a_\rho.\tag{0.3} \end{equation}
    On a cone \(\sigma\text{,}\) we use a lattice element \(m_\sigma\in M\) with \(\psi_D(u)=\langle m_\sigma,u\rangle\) for \(u\in\sigma\text{;}\) the corresponding local equation for \(D\) is \(\chi^{-m_\sigma}\text{.}\) The Cartier criterion establishing the existence of this data will be proved later. If \(\sigma\) is not full dimensional, \(m_\sigma\) need not be unique, but its restriction to the span of \(\sigma\) is fixed. For a \(\Q\)-Cartier divisor we instead allow \(m_\sigma\in M_{\Q}\text{.}\)
    An arbitrary invariant Weil divisor is not assigned a piecewise linear support function by definition. Its polyhedron \(P_D\) still makes sense, but cone-wise linearity of the prescribed ray values is an additional condition. We will state positivity criteria by explicit inequalities as well as by the chosen convexity terminology.3
    As a consistency check, replacing \(D\) by \(D+\divisor(\chi^q)\) changes the inequalities to those defining \(P_D-q\text{.}\) When a support function is defined, it changes to \(\psi_D-\langle q,\ \rangle\text{.}\) Thus linearly equivalent invariant representatives can give translated polyhedra. The representative divisor, the associated line bundle, and a chosen polyhedron should not be silently identified.

    Section 0.7 Lattice volume and lattice-point counting

    For a rank-\(d\) lattice \(L\text{,}\) \(\vol_L\) denotes the translation-invariant measure on \(L_{\R}\) for which a fundamental parallelepiped of \(L\) has volume \(1\text{.}\) The normalized lattice volume is
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Vol}_L(P)=d!\,\vol_L(P). \end{equation*}
    Thus the simplex spanned by \(0\) and a basis of \(L\) has \(\vol_L=1/d!\) and \(\operatorname{Vol}_L=1\text{.}\) The two normalizations will retain distinct notation. In dimension zero a point has volume \(1\text{,}\) with \(0!=1\text{.}\)
    For a nonempty lattice polytope \(P\subset M_{\R}\) of dimension \(d\text{,}\) intrinsic volume uses
    \begin{equation*} L_P=M\cap\operatorname{span}_{\R}(P-P). \end{equation*}
    Translating the affine span of \(P\) by any lattice vertex identifies its affine lattice with \(L_P\text{.}\) We use this rank-\(d\) lattice, rather than the ambient rank-\(n\) measure that would give a lower-dimensional polytope volume zero. A polytope is called a lattice polytope when all its vertices lie in the stated lattice, and rational when all vertices lie in its rational vector space.
    We write
    \begin{equation*} E_P(q)=\#(qP\cap M),\qquad q\in\N, \end{equation*}
    for the lattice-point counting function. The lattice-point chapter will distinguish the polynomial statement for lattice polytopes from the quasipolynomial statement for rational polytopes. With our notation, the volume appearing as the leading coefficient in the lattice-polytope version of Ehrhart’s theorem is \(\vol_{L_P}(P)\text{,}\) not \(\operatorname{Vol}_{L_P}(P)\text{.}\) Factors of \(d!\) will therefore be displayed explicitly when volume is compared with degree or an intersection number.
    These normalizations depend on the lattice. Passing to a finite-index sublattice changes covolumes and can change the count of lattice points. A linear change of real coordinates is not a lattice isomorphism unless its integral compatibility is checked.

    Section 0.8 Reading routes and the use of sources

    The main route starts with Chapter 1, proceeds to Chapter 2, and then develops the divisor and polytope dictionaries in Chapter 3 and Chapter 4. The affine construction is the place to see why lattice points become regular functions. The fan construction explains how these functions fit together on charts. Divisors and polytopes then explain why the same data can control sections and positivity.
    A reader interested in calculations should work through the recurring examples along this route, recording both an input and an output: generators of a semigroup and equations of an affine chart; rays and invariant divisors; divisor coefficients and lattice-point inequalities. Intersection calculations in Chapter 5, homogeneous coordinates and quotients in Chapter 6, and birational modifications in Chapter 7 depend on different parts of this dictionary. They need not be forced into one uninterrupted prerequisite chain.
    Chapter 17 connects the intersection ring, line-bundle cohomology, and lattice-point polynomial. It computes the Todd class from the toric Euler sequence, derives the surface formula, and interprets Ehrhart reciprocity through Serre duality. It can be read after the divisor and intersection chapters; its comparison with the combinatorial proof and Pick’s formula also uses Chapter 16. For a calculation involving several constructions at once, Section 7.4 follows the conifold from its invariant ring through the two GIT quotients to their fans and the Atiyah flop, then compares these resolutions with its smoothing.
    Further branches extend constructions already encountered. Chapter 9 replaces the single local weight of a line bundle by compatible filtrations of a vector space; it needs the affine and divisor dictionaries. Chapter 10 uses initial ideals and orbit closures to choose compactifications of subvarieties of a torus. Its boundary calculations continue the initial-form argument in the Bernstein unit, while its general existence theorems are stated as external inputs. Chapter 11 constructs flat families from weighted equations, relates monomial initial ideals to lattice triangulations, and gives a Grassmannian with a normal toric special fiber. It can be read after the affine and polytope chapters, independently of the curve-counting theories.
    Chapter 12 combines monomial blowups and discrepancies into the shifted-interior formula for multiplier ideals. Its setting is complex affine space, where a global toric log resolution of a monomial ideal is available. The general birational definition and its resolution independence are supporting inputs; the Newton-polyhedron calculation is proved here. Chapter 13 follows the intersection–volume dictionary in another direction: the surface Hodge index theorem yields mixed inequalities on a common toric model, and convex approximation then proves inequalities for arbitrary real convex bodies. Chapter 14 uses the preferred anticanonical polytope to compute a barycenter obstruction; its metric existence conclusion is the external Wang–Zhu theorem, with the compact real torus and analytic normalizations specified there. Finally, Chapter 15 turns a lattice Minkowski decomposition into an explicit flat family of affine toric singularities. It proves the central-fiber identification and flatness directly, then distinguishes a smoothing from a resolution and from a versal deformation. For exact lattice-point counts, Chapter 16 returns to the cone construction: its graded semigroup ring yields the Hilbert series, an elementary half-open decomposition proves Ehrhart reciprocity, and the Reeve example separates the ambient lattice from the lattice generated in degree one.
    Cox–Little–Schenck is the main working reference for the systematic dictionary, explicit constructions, and calculations. Its Sections 1.1–1.3 and 3.1–3.3 support our opening material; Chapters 4–7 support the divisor, coordinate, and positivity dictionaries. Fulton provides a concise geometric route through cones, orbits, divisors, and intersection theory. Oda provides a further reference for the relation with convex geometry and for comparisons of classical formulations. Danilov’s survey gives a classical overview of the algebraic and combinatorial structure, useful for seeing these topics together. These are complementary roles, rather than a requirement to compare every result in all four sources. See [CLS11], [Ful93], [Oda88], and [Dan78].
    Our ground-field and normality conventions are stated here because they must be checked when importing a result. In particular, a proof written over \(\C\) will be used over arbitrary \(k\) only after its argument and hypotheses have been checked. Citations at the point of use identify the definition, statement, or proof being used; a general bibliography entry does not certify an extension of that statement.
    The interfaces are organized around mechanisms. Finite generation and toric degenerations appear in Section 4.6; the Bergman fan and the matroid Hodge theorem in Section 5.6; toroidal local models and weak factorization in Section 7.5; and enumerative and mirror constructions in Chapter 8. A degeneration asks which information survives the special fiber. A fan model asks which geometric data it retains. A curve-counting construction asks what additional moduli, compactness, or virtual theory is required. In each case, the route is
    \begin{equation*} \text{general problem}\ \longrightarrow\ \text{toric data}\ \longrightarrow\ \text{calculation or theorem}\ \longrightarrow\ \text{a justified conclusion}. \end{equation*}
    The last arrow needs its own hypotheses and proof. Success in a toric example alone does not supply it.
    These interfaces guide the selection of examples and results while the subject of the notes remains toric geometry. Logarithmic geometry, valuation theory, combinatorial Hodge theory, Gromov–Witten theory, and mirror symmetry each have definitions and substantial supporting arguments of their own. When one of them is used, the relevant chapter will specify the required result and where its full proof belongs, rather than treating its name as a substitute for an explanation.
