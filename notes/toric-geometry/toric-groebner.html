    Chapter 11 Gröbner degenerations and toric special fibers

    A toric variety can occur as the special fiber of a family whose other fibers have a different geometric description. Weighted initial ideals give a concrete way to construct such families. The construction itself guarantees flatness; whether the special fiber is integral, reduced, or normal remains a separate calculation. This chapter proves the family construction, explains the triangulations attached to monomial initial ideals of toric ideals, and produces an explicit degeneration of \(\operatorname{Gr}(2,4)\) to a normal projective toric fourfold.
    We use the semigroup and toric-ideal results of Chapter 1 and the projective polytope dictionary of Chapter 4. The main reference for initial ideals and triangulations is [Stu96, Chapters 1 and 8]. All algebraic constructions and examples below work over the fixed algebraically closed field in arbitrary characteristic. The triangulation theorem is identified as an external combinatorial input.

    Section 11.1 Weights and the flat family

    Definition 11.1 Minimum-weight initial forms.

    Let \(S=k[x_1,\ldots,x_r]\text{,}\) with \(r\geq1\text{,}\) let \(\omega\in\Z^r\text{,}\) and write \(x^\alpha=\prod_i x_i^{\alpha_i}\text{.}\) For \(0\ne f=\sum_\alpha c_\alpha x^\alpha\text{,}\) put
    \begin{equation*} m_\omega(f)=\min_{c_\alpha\ne0}\langle\omega,\alpha\rangle, \qquad \operatorname{in}_\omega(f)= \sum_{\langle\omega,\alpha\rangle=m_\omega(f)}c_\alpha x^\alpha. \end{equation*}
    For an ideal \(I\subseteq S\text{,}\) its initial ideal is generated by \(\operatorname{in}_\omega(f)\) for all nonzero \(f\in I\text{.}\) Initial forms can have several terms. Our convention selects the minimum weight.
    The corresponding weighted homogenization is
    \begin{equation} f^\omega(x,t)=t^{-m_\omega(f)} f(t^{\omega_1}x_1,\ldots,t^{\omega_r}x_r).\tag{11.1} \end{equation}
    Despite possibly negative entries of \(\omega\text{,}\) this is a polynomial: its \(x^\alpha\)-coefficient has exponent \(\langle\omega,\alpha\rangle-m_\omega(f)\geq0\) in \(t\text{.}\) At \(t=0\) it becomes the initial form. Define
    \begin{equation*} J_\omega=(f^\omega:0\ne f\in I)\subseteq S[t], \qquad B=S[t]/J_\omega. \end{equation*}
    The definition uses all elements of the ideal, not merely an arbitrary list of its generators.

    Theorem 11.2 The weighted initial family is flat.

    The algebra \(B\) is finitely generated and flat over \(k[t]\text{,}\) with
    \begin{equation*} B/tB\cong S/\operatorname{in}_\omega(I),\qquad B[t^{-1}]\cong(S/I)[t,t^{-1}]. \end{equation*}
    Under the second isomorphism the coordinate \(x_i\) maps to \(t^{-\omega_i}\overline x_i\text{.}\) In particular, every fiber at \(t=c\ne0\) is isomorphic to \(\Spec(S/I)\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Consider the homomorphism
    \begin{equation*} \varphi:S[t]\longrightarrow(S/I)[t,t^{-1}], \qquad x_i\longmapsto t^{-\omega_i}\overline x_i, \quad t\longmapsto t. \end{equation*}
    Every \(f^\omega\) maps to zero. To prove that these generate the kernel, group a polynomial \(F\in\ker\varphi\) by the integer \(q=b-\langle\omega,\alpha\rangle\) of a term \(x^\alpha t^b\text{.}\) Its group of index \(q\) is
    \begin{equation*} F_q=t^q f_q(t^{\omega_1}x_1,\ldots,t^{\omega_r}x_r). \end{equation*}
    The Laurent powers of \(t\) are independent over \(S/I\text{,}\) so \(f_q\in I\text{.}\) For a nonzero group, the smallest actual \(t\)-exponent in it is \(q+m_\omega(f_q)\geq0\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} F_q=t^{q+m_\omega(f_q)}f_q^\omega\in J_\omega. \end{equation*}
    Summing the groups proves \(\ker\varphi=J_\omega\text{.}\)
    Thus \(B\) is the image algebra, generated by \(t\) and the finitely many \(t^{-\omega_i}\overline x_i\text{.}\) As a \(k[t]\)-module it embeds in \((S/I)[t,t^{-1}]\text{.}\) The latter is torsion-free: choose a \(k\)-basis of \(S/I\) and regard it as a direct sum of copies of \(k[t,t^{-1}]\text{.}\) Consequently \(B\) is torsion-free over the PID \(k[t]\text{,}\) and is flat. Indeed a torsion-free module over a PID is the directed union of its finitely generated submodules, each of which is free; a directed colimit of flat modules is flat. No finite generation as a module is required.
    Reducing the generators of \(J_\omega\) modulo \(t\) gives precisely \(\operatorname{in}_\omega(I)\text{.}\) After inverting \(t\text{,}\) all \(\overline x_i\) belong to the image by multiplication with \(t^{\omega_i}\text{,}\) proving the second isomorphism and the assertions about fibers.
    Flatness here uses torsion-freeness with respect to every nonzero polynomial in \(k[t]\text{.}\) Merely knowing that multiplication by \(t\) is injective would not suffice. For a nonhomogeneous ideal the special fiber can even be empty: \(I=(x-1)\text{,}\) \(\omega=1\) gives \(J_\omega=(tx-1)\text{,}\) so \(B=k[t,t^{-1}]\) and \(\operatorname{in}_\omega(I)=(1)\text{.}\)

    Proposition 11.3 Homogeneous families preserve the Hilbert function.

    If \(I\) is homogeneous in the standard grading, give \(x_i\) degree one and \(t\) degree zero. The algebra \(B\) is graded, and each \(B_d\) is a finite free \(k[t]\)-module of rank \(\dim_k(S/I)_d\text{.}\) Thus all fibers have the same graded Hilbert function. Moreover \(\Proj B\to\A^1\) is a flat projective family with the corresponding projective fibers.

    Proof.
    Write \(f\in I\) as the sum of its nonzero homogeneous parts \(f_d\in I\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} f^\omega=\sum_d t^{m_\omega(f_d)-m_\omega(f)}f_d^\omega. \end{equation*}
    Its exponents in \(t\) are nonnegative, so homogeneous elements suffice in the definition of \(J_\omega\text{.}\) That ideal is therefore homogeneous in the indicated grading. Each \(B_d\) is a quotient of the finite free module \(S_d\otimes k[t]\text{,}\) hence finitely generated. It is torsion-free by the embedding in the theorem, and is therefore free over the PID. Its rank is computed after inverting \(t\text{,}\) giving the stated number.
    The relative Proj is closed in \(\P^{r-1}\times\A^1\) because \(B\) is generated in degree one over \(k[t]\text{.}\) Its standard affine charts have rings \((B[x_i^{-1}])_0\text{.}\) Localization preserves flatness, and the degree-zero part is a \(k[t]\)-module direct summand of the localized graded module. These rings are therefore flat. They cover the relative Proj, proving flatness of the projective family.
    In particular its Hilbert polynomial, dimension, and projective degree remain fixed. Individual cohomology groups have not been identified by this argument; the assertion concerns the homogeneous coordinate rings and their Hilbert functions.

    Section 11.2 Computing the family and identifying its special fiber

    Suppose \(I=(g_1,\ldots,g_s)\) and \(K=(g_1^\omega,\ldots,g_s^\omega)\text{.}\) Inverting \(t\) makes \(K\) and \(J_\omega\) equal, but they need not agree before that localization. The kernel description shows that \(J_\omega\) is \(t\)-saturated. Consequently
    \begin{equation} J_\omega=K:t^\infty =\{F\in S[t]:t^qF\in K\text{ for some }q\geq0\}.\tag{11.2} \end{equation}
    Indeed both sides are the contraction of their common localization from \(S[t,t^{-1}]\text{.}\) This gives a precise way to correct arbitrary homogenized generators. Gröbner-basis algorithms compute this saturation or provide generators adapted to the homogenization.

    Example 11.4 A missing equation caused by a cancellation.

    Take \(I=(x+y,x)=(x,y)\) and weights \(\omega_x=0\text{,}\) \(\omega_y=1\text{.}\) Homogenizing the displayed generators gives \(K=(x+ty,x)=(x,ty)\text{.}\) Its fiber at zero has ideal \((x)\text{,}\) which misses the equation \(y=0\text{.}\) In its quotient, the nonzero class of \(y\) is killed by \(t\text{,}\) so this proposed family is not flat. Saturation gives \(K:t^\infty=(x,y)=J_\omega\text{.}\) Equivalently the polynomial \(y=(x+y)-x\in I\) contributes its own homogenization. This works in every characteristic.
    After computing the correct initial ideal, one must identify the resulting algebra. An initial ideal is not automatically prime. A monomial initial ideal often defines a union of coordinate subspaces, with possible nilpotents. Even a prime binomial ideal need not give a normal algebra: the cusp algebra \(k[s^2,s^3]\) in Example 1.36 is a domain but its semigroup is not saturated. Our convention for a toric variety requires integrality and normality.
    A sufficient test used below is explicit: exhibit the special algebra as \(k[\Gamma]\) for a finitely generated submonoid of a lattice, and prove that \(\Gamma\) is saturated in its group of differences. The semigroup normalization theorem then gives normality. For a homogeneous algebra, the grading must also be specified before passing to Proj. This retains the lattice information that an equation alone can conceal.

    Section 11.3 Regular triangulations and multiplicities

    Let \(A=(a_1,\ldots,a_r)\) be distinct elements of a lattice \(L\text{,}\) generating \(L\) as a group, and suppose that an integral height function \(h:L\to\Z\) satisfies \(h(a_i)=1\text{.}\) The toric ideal
    \begin{equation*} I_A=\ker\bigl(k[x_1,\ldots,x_r]\longrightarrow k[L],\ x_i\longmapsto\chi^{a_i}\bigr) \end{equation*}
    is homogeneous. Its monomials of a fixed \(A\)-degree correspond to different expressions for one lattice point as a nonnegative sum of the columns.

    Definition 11.5 Lower lifts and their simplicial complex.

    Lift \(a_i\) to \((a_i,-\omega_i)\) and project the lower faces of their convex hull back to \(\conv(A)\text{.}\) A lower face is one minimizing a linear functional whose last-coordinate coefficient is positive. For generic weights these projected cells are simplices, defining a regular triangulation \(\Delta_{-\omega}\text{.}\) Regard it as a simplicial complex on the labelled index set \(\{1,\ldots,r\}\text{;}\) an index need not occur as a vertex of any cell. Its Stanley–Reisner ideal is
    \begin{equation*} I_{\Delta_{-\omega}}= \left(\prod_{i\in F}x_i:F\notin\Delta_{-\omega}\right). \end{equation*}
    A full-dimensional simplex with index set \(F\) is unimodular when its columns form a basis of \(L\text{.}\) Equivalently, its normalized volume in the affine lattice of \(h=1\) is one.

    Theorem 11.6 Initial ideals and regular triangulations.

    For generic integral weights, so that \(\operatorname{in}_\omega(I_A)\) is monomial, one has
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{\operatorname{in}_\omega(I_A)}=I_{\Delta_{-\omega}}. \end{equation*}
    The initial ideal is squarefree, equivalently radical, exactly when every maximal simplex of \(\Delta_{-\omega}\) is unimodular in \(L\text{.}\) More generally the multiplicity of a component corresponding to a maximal simplex is that simplex’s normalized lattice volume.
    This is the external triangulation theorem of [Stu96, Theorem 8.3, Corollary 8.4, Theorem 8.8, and Corollary 8.9]. Its statements and proofs are combinatorial and apply over an arbitrary field. The lattice is the intrinsic lattice \(L=\gp(A)\text{,}\) not an arbitrarily larger lattice containing the columns. An unused index gives a singleton nonface and hence a variable in the Stanley–Reisner ideal; it must not silently be deleted from the polynomial ring.1

    Example 11.7 A reduced pair of lines and a double line.

    Take \(A=((0,1),(1,1),(2,1))\) in \(L=\Z^2\text{,}\) with height the second coordinate. Its toric ideal is
    \begin{equation*} I_A=(x_0x_2-x_1^2). \end{equation*}
    This is the homogeneous coordinate ring of the conic parametrized by \([u:v]\mapsto[u^2:uv:v^2]\text{.}\) For \(\omega=(0,1,0)\text{,}\) the family is
    \begin{equation*} x_0x_2-t^2x_1^2=0. \end{equation*}
    Its special fiber consists of the two reduced coordinate lines \(x_0=0\) and \(x_2=0\text{,}\) meeting at \([0:1:0]\text{.}\) The lower lift has heights \((0,-1,0)\text{,}\) and its two lower segments have endpoints \(\{0,1\}\) and \(\{1,2\}\text{.}\) Both have lattice length one. Their sole minimal nonface is \(\{0,2\}\text{,}\) giving the squarefree initial ideal \((x_0x_2)\text{.}\)
    For \(\omega=(0,-1,0)\text{,}\) the family instead is
    \begin{equation*} t^2x_0x_2-x_1^2=0. \end{equation*}
    Its special fiber has ideal \((x_1^2)\text{:}\) it is a double line with underlying line \(x_1=0\text{.}\) The lower heights are \((0,1,0)\text{;}\) the only maximal lower segment joins endpoints \(0,2\text{,}\) has lattice length two, and leaves index \(1\) unused. The radical ideal is \((x_1)\text{,}\) and the component multiplicity is two, exactly as predicted. These descriptions remain valid in characteristic two.
    Both special fibers have Hilbert series
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1-s^2}{(1-s)^3}=\frac{1+s}{(1-s)^2}, \end{equation*}
    hence Hilbert function \(2q+1\) in degree \(q\geq0\text{.}\) Here \(s\) is the Hilbert-series variable, distinct from the family parameter. Equal Hilbert functions do not distinguish a reduced pair of components from a nonreduced component. Neither special fiber is an integral toric variety under our standing convention, although its reduced components are projective toric lines.
    Thus a monomial degeneration of a toric ideal naturally organizes coordinate components by a triangulation. To obtain an integral toric special fiber of another variety, one instead looks for an appropriate binomial initial algebra and verifies its semigroup. The next example makes that change of purpose explicit.

    Section 11.4 A Grassmannian with a normal toric special fiber

    Subsection 11.4.1 The Plücker equation and its charts

    The Grassmannian \(\operatorname{Gr}(2,4)\) parametrizes two-dimensional subspaces of \(k^4\text{.}\) Use its Plücker coordinates
    \begin{equation*} (a,b,c,d,e,f)=(p_{12},p_{13},p_{14},p_{23},p_{24},p_{34}). \end{equation*}
    They identify it with the quadric
    \begin{equation*} af-be+cd=0\quad\text{in }\P^5. \end{equation*}
    This description can be checked on charts. On the open set \(a\ne0\text{,}\) a two-plane has a unique row-space representative
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix}1&0&u&v\\0&1&w&z\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Its normalized Plücker coordinates are
    \begin{equation*} (a,b,c,d,e,f)=(1,w,z,-u,-v,uz-vw). \end{equation*}
    Conversely a point of the quadric with \(a=1\) has \(u=-d\text{,}\) \(v=-e\text{,}\) \(w=b\text{,}\) \(z=c\text{,}\) and the relation forces the displayed value of \(f\text{.}\) These are mutually inverse polynomial maps. Permuting the four coordinate directions gives the same verification on each of the six Plücker opens, which cover both schemes. Thus the identification, including its scheme structure, holds in every characteristic.
    Choose weights \(\omega=(0,0,1,1,0,0)\) in the indicated variable order. The weighted family is
    \begin{equation} \mathcal Y= \{af-be+t^2cd=0\}\subseteq\P^5\times\A^1.\tag{11.3} \end{equation}
    For a principal ideal the initial form of a product is the product of initial forms, so this one homogenized equation generates the full weighted ideal. The flat-family theorem therefore applies. For \(t\ne0\text{,}\) scaling \(c,d\) by \(t\) identifies the equation with the Plücker quadric. At zero the homogeneous algebra is
    \begin{equation*} R_0=k[a,b,c,d,e,f]/(af-be). \end{equation*}

    Subsection 11.4.2 The special algebra and its intrinsic lattice

    In \(L=\Z^5\text{,}\) assign the following exponent vectors to the six variables:
    \begin{gather*} a=(1,0,0,0,0),\quad f=(-1,0,1,0,0),\\ b=(0,1,0,0,0),\quad e=(0,-1,1,0,0),\\ c=(0,0,0,1,0),\quad d=(0,0,0,0,1). \end{gather*}
    They generate \(L\) as a group: the first, second, fourth, and fifth coordinate directions are present, and \(a+f\) is the third. Their group of relations has rank one and is generated by \(a+f=b+e\text{.}\) For any binomial relation, cancel common factors; its remaining exponent difference is a multiple of this relation, so the remaining binomial is a difference of equal powers of \(af\) and \(be\text{,}\) divisible by \(af-be\text{.}\) The toric-ideal theorem proves that the full kernel is \((af-be)\text{.}\) Thus \(R_0=k[\Gamma]\) for the monoid generated by these six vectors.
    There is also a direct normality test. In coordinates \((\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon)\text{,}\) the real cone generated by the vectors is
    \begin{equation*} \delta,\epsilon\geq0,\qquad \gamma\geq\max(0,-\alpha)+\max(0,-\beta). \end{equation*}
    Necessity follows by counting the coefficients of \(f,e\text{.}\) For sufficiency, use \(a\) or \(f\) to supply the sign of \(\alpha\text{,}\) and \(b\) or \(e\) to supply the sign of \(\beta\text{.}\) The remaining nonnegative third coordinate is supplied by \(a+f\text{,}\) while \(c,d\) supply the last two coordinates. This works with real coefficients for the cone, and with integer coefficients for every lattice point in it. Consequently
    \begin{equation*} \Gamma=\Cone(\Gamma)\cap L. \end{equation*}
    The monoid is saturated in its full difference lattice, so \(R_0\) is a normal domain. This is the affine conifold algebra with two polynomial variables adjoined; compare Example 8.13. The lattice argument gives normality directly, including in positive characteristic.
    The standard grading is induced by the integral function
    \begin{equation*} h(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon) =\alpha+\beta+2\gamma+\delta+\epsilon. \end{equation*}
    Every generator has height one. If \(P\) is their convex hull in \(h=1\text{,}\) saturation and degree-one generation give the integer decomposition property for \(P\text{.}\) Its affine dimension is four, and its intrinsic difference lattice is \(\ker h\text{.}\) The normal projective variety \(Y_0=\Proj R_0\) is therefore the toric variety of this polarized polytope, with dense torus
    \begin{equation*} \Spec k[\ker h]. \end{equation*}
    One sees this torus by inverting all six monomials and taking degree zero. Normality also follows on each projective affine chart from the degree-zero subring of the corresponding normal homogeneous localization.

    Subsection 11.4.3 Degree is preserved while smoothness changes

    The special hypersurface has singular locus
    \begin{equation*} a=b=e=f=0,\qquad [c:d]\in\P^1. \end{equation*}
    Indeed the partial derivatives of \(af-be\) generate \((a,b,e,f)\text{.}\) On the other hand, for \(t\ne0\) the derivatives of \(af-be+t^2cd\) can vanish simultaneously only if every projective coordinate vanishes. Thus the general fiber is smooth and the special fiber is singular along this projective line, in every characteristic.
    Every fiber is cut out by a nonzero quadratic nonzerodivisor in a polynomial ring with six degree-one variables. Its Hilbert series is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1-s^2}{(1-s)^6}=\frac{1+s}{(1-s)^5}. \end{equation*}
    The projective dimension is four and the degree is two. Equivalently the degree-\(q\) part has dimension
    \begin{equation*} \binom{q+4}{4}+\binom{q+3}{4}, \end{equation*}
    with the second term zero when \(q=0\text{,}\) and leading term \(2q^4/4!\text{.}\) By the volume formula of Theorem 4.19, the polytope \(P\text{,}\) translated by any vertex into \((\ker h)_\R\text{,}\) has normalized lattice volume two. The lattice and the factorial normalization are both fixed by that comparison.

    Section 11.5 The scope of the transfer

    The Grassmannian example separates three steps. Weighted homogenization constructs the flat family. The exponent calculation identifies its special algebra. Saturation in the stated lattice makes that algebra normal, allowing the fan and polytope tools to apply to the special fiber. None of those steps can be replaced by the instruction to discard higher-weight terms of a chosen presentation.
    The valuation construction in Theorem 4.22 has a related structure: finite generation produces a degeneration algebra, while saturation controls the normality of the resulting semigroup algebra. The inputs differ. Here one begins with equations and weights and proves flatness directly; there one begins with a valuation and its actual value semigroup. A convex body or a visually toric-looking equation alone supplies neither complete construction.
    In the homogeneous families proved here, Hilbert functions and degrees transfer. Smoothness demonstrably does not. Identifying individual cohomology groups, Gromov–Witten invariants, or mirror constructions across a degeneration would require additional comparison results and their hypotheses. The useful conclusion already established is concrete: an explicitly specified general variety can be connected to a normal toric special fiber whose coordinate algebra, lattice, degree, and singularities are all available for calculation.
