    Chapter 2 Polarizations and variation of the fibres

    A smooth fibre of a Lagrangian fibration is an abelian variety, although it has no preferred origin. Its polarization records integral information that is absent from its dimension alone. The fibres also vary in a moduli space of polarized abelian varieties. We first determine which polarization is attached to the fibration, then study its period map and the restrictions imposed by the symplectic total space.

    Section 2.1 Polarizations on an abelian variety

    Let \(A=V/\Gamma\) be an abelian variety of dimension \(n\text{,}\) where \(V\) is a complex vector space and \(\Gamma\subset V\) is a lattice of real rank \(2n\text{.}\) A polarization is the first Chern class \(\theta=c_1(M)\) of an ample line bundle. Tensoring \(M\) by a line bundle in \(\Pic^0(A)\) does not change the polarization. Using
    \begin{equation*} H^2(A,\Z)=\Lambda^2\Hom(\Gamma,\Z), \end{equation*}
    regard \(\theta\) as an integral alternating form \(E_\theta\) on \(\Gamma\text{.}\) Its real extension satisfies \(E_\theta(Iv,Iw)=E_\theta(v,w)\) and \(E_\theta(v,Iv)>0\) for \(v\ne0\text{.}\) These are the Riemann bilinear relations.

    Definition 2.1.

    The type of a polarization \(\theta\) on \(A\) is the unique tuple \((d_1,\ldots,d_n)\) of positive integers with \(d_i\mid d_{i+1}\) for which there is an integral basis \(\gamma_1,\ldots,\gamma_n,\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_n\) of \(\Gamma\) satisfying
    \begin{equation*} E_\theta(\gamma_i,\gamma_j)=E_\theta(\delta_i,\delta_j)=0,\qquad E_\theta(\gamma_i,\delta_j)=d_i\delta_{ij}. \end{equation*}
    The polarization is principal if every \(d_i=1\text{.}\)
    Existence and uniqueness follow from the elementary-divisor theorem for a nondegenerate alternating form over \(\Z\text{.}\) Equivalently, its Gram matrix is integrally congruent to \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&D\\-D&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\text{,}\) where \(D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n)\text{.}\) Wieneck uses this description to define the type of a Lagrangian fibration [Wie16, Section 4].

    Proposition 2.2.

    Let \(\theta=c_1(M)\) have type \((d_1,\ldots,d_n)\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \frac1{n!}\int_A\theta^n=h^0(A,M)=\prod_i d_i. \end{equation*}
    The polarization homomorphism \(\phi_M:A\to A^\vee=\Pic^0(A)\text{,}\) defined by \(a\mapsto t_a^*M\otimes M^{-1}\text{,}\) is an isogeny with
    \begin{equation*} \ker\phi_M\simeq\bigoplus_i(\Z/d_i\Z)^2. \end{equation*}
    The class \(\theta\) is primitive in \(H^2(A,\Z)\) exactly when \(d_1=1\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Let \(x_i,y_i\) be the integral cohomology basis dual to \(\gamma_i,\delta_i\text{.}\) Then \(\theta=\sum_i d_i x_i\wedge y_i\text{.}\) Its \(n\)th power contains exactly the \(n!\) permutations of the product of these \(n\) summands, so its integral divided by \(n!\) is \(\prod_i d_i\text{.}\) The orientation agrees with the complex orientation because \(\theta\) is positive. The tangent bundle of \(A\) is trivial, so Riemann–Roch gives \(\chi(A,M)=\int_A\theta^n/n!\text{.}\) Kodaira vanishing, with \(K_A\simeq\cO_A\text{,}\) gives \(H^i(A,M)=0\) for \(i>0\text{.}\)
    On lattices, the polarization homomorphism is induced by \(v\mapsto E_\theta(v,-)\text{.}\) Its kernel on the quotient torus is
    \begin{equation*} \Gamma^\sharp/\Gamma,\qquad \Gamma^\sharp=\{v\in\Gamma_\R:E_\theta(v,\Gamma)\subseteq\Z\}. \end{equation*}
    In the chosen basis, \(\Gamma^\sharp\) is generated by \(\gamma_i/d_i,\delta_i/d_i\text{.}\) This gives the asserted kernel, of order \((\prod_i d_i)^2\text{.}\) The lattice map is nonsingular, so the homomorphism is an isogeny. Finally, divisibility of \(\theta\) in \(H^2(A,\Z)\) is the gcd of its coefficients in an integral exterior basis. That gcd is \(d_1\text{.}\)
    Multiplication of the class by an integer \(k>0\) multiplies every elementary divisor by \(k\text{.}\) A primitive polarization need not be principal: for elliptic curves \(E_1,E_2\text{,}\) the product class
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{pr}_1^*[0]+3\operatorname{pr}_2^*[0] \end{equation*}
    on \(E_1\times E_2\) is primitive of type \((1,3)\text{.}\) Its square is six, its line bundle has three sections, and its polarization kernel is \((\Z/3\Z)^2\text{.}\) A principal polarization instead gives an isomorphism \(A\simeq A^\vee\text{.}\)
    Translations act trivially on integral cohomology, so the type is defined for an abelian torsor without choosing an origin. Once an origin has been chosen, the homomorphism \(\phi_M\) depends only on the class \(\theta\text{.}\) The corresponding construction for a family, including its finite kernel and its action on a torsor, is treated in Chapter 5.

    Section 2.2 The primitive polarization of a fibre

    Fix a Lagrangian fibration \(f:X\to B\) from an IHS manifold of dimension \(2n\text{,}\) and let \(i:F\hookrightarrow X\) be a smooth fibre. Choose an ample bundle \(H_B\) on the projective base and write \(h=c_1(f^*H_B)\text{.}\) The class \(h\) restricts to zero on \(F\text{.}\) The fibre polarization instead comes from restricting Kähler classes on \(X\text{.}\)

    Proposition 2.3.

    The restriction map satisfies
    \begin{equation*} \ker\bigl(i^*:H^2(X,\R)\to H^2(F,\R)\bigr)=h^\perp. \end{equation*}
    Its image is one-dimensional. The restrictions of Kähler classes form its positive ray, which has a unique primitive integral generator \(\theta_F\in H^2(F,\Z)\text{.}\) This generator is an ample class.

    Proof.
    Intersecting \(n\) general divisors in high powers of \(H_B\) gives
    \begin{equation*} h^n=\left(\int_Bc_1(H_B)^n\right)[F]. \end{equation*}
    All smooth fibres have the same homology class. Since \(q_X(h)=0\text{,}\) the polarized Fujiki formula implies that, for a positive constant \(C\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \int_F i^*\alpha_1\cdots i^*\alpha_n=C\prod_{j=1}^nq_X(h,\alpha_j). \end{equation*}
    In each nonzero pairing of the \(n\) copies of \(h\) with the other \(n\) classes, every copy of \(h\) must be paired with one of the latter. Positivity of \(C\) follows by taking all \(\alpha_j\) Kähler.
    Choose a Kähler class \(\omega\) on \(X\) and let \(\kappa=i^*\omega\text{.}\) One has \(q_X(h,\omega)>0\text{.}\) For a real \((1,1)\) class \(\alpha\) with \(q_X(h,\alpha)=0\text{,}\) the mixed formula gives \(\int_F i^*\alpha\,\kappa^{n-1}=0\text{.}\) If \(n\ge2\text{,}\) it also gives \(\int_F(i^*\alpha)^2\kappa^{n-2}=0\text{.}\) The Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations are negative definite on the real primitive \((1,1)\) classes, so \(i^*\alpha=0\text{.}\) If \(n=1\text{,}\) vanishing of the integral already gives that conclusion.
    The classes in \(H^{2,0}(X)\oplus H^{0,2}(X)\) restrict to zero because \(F\) is Lagrangian, and they are orthogonal to \(h\text{.}\) The preceding argument therefore applies to every real class orthogonal to \(h\text{.}\) Conversely, if \(i^*\alpha=0\text{,}\) the mixed formula with \(\omega\) in the remaining slots gives \(q_X(h,\alpha)=0\text{.}\) This proves the kernel assertion and the rank-one image.
    The kernel is defined over \(\Q\text{,}\) so the image line is rational. Its intersection with \(H^2(F,\Z)\) has rank one. The positive generator \(\theta_F\) is a positive real multiple of \(\kappa\text{,}\) hence is an integral Kähler class. It is therefore the first Chern class of an ample line bundle.
    This is the restriction theorem used by Voisin and Wieneck; compare [Wie16, Proposition 4.3 and Lemma 4.4] and [Kim25, Lemma 3.6 and Corollary 3.7]. The proof above gives the kernel as well as its dimension.
    The integral image of \(i^*\) may be a proper subgroup of \(\Z\theta_F\text{.}\) The class \(\theta_F\) generates the saturation of that image in its real span. It need not be the restriction of an integral class on \(X\text{.}\) A positive rescaling of a Kähler class on \(X\) always restricts to \(\theta_F\text{,}\) but the rescaled class on \(X\) need not be integral.1

    Definition 2.4.

    The polarization type of \(f\), denoted \(\operatorname{pol}(f)\text{,}\) is the type of the primitive ample class \(\theta_F\) on a smooth fibre.
    This is independent of the smooth fibre. The restriction of every fixed class on \(X\) is parallel under smooth transport of fibres. These restrictions define a rank-one sub-local system of \(R^2f_*\R\text{.}\) Its primitive positive integral generators are preserved by transport, since transport preserves both the integral lattice and the Kähler ray. The alternating forms \(E_{\theta_F}\) consequently have constant elementary divisors.

    Theorem 2.5 (Wieneck).

    The polarization type is constant in a connected family of Lagrangian fibrations. In particular, deformation equivalent fibrations have the same polarization type.
    This is [Wie16, Theorem 4.9]. Locally in the parameter space, choose a smoothly varying regular fibre. Smooth proper transport identifies the two integral cohomology groups and their restriction maps. Their rational image line, its saturation, and its positive generator are then constant. The elementary divisors of the resulting integral form are constant. This proves local constancy, and connectedness gives the assertion. The family in the theorem is a family of morphisms, including their bases; deformation equivalence of the total spaces alone is weaker.

    Section 2.3 Known types and the integral restriction map

    The primitive polarization can be principal even when a global integral class restricts to a multiple of it. Two different integers must be distinguished. A class is primitive if it is indivisible in the underlying abelian group. For a nonzero integral class \(h\text{,}\) its BBF divisibility is
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{div}(h)=\gcd\{q_X(h,\alpha):\alpha\in H^2(X,\Z)\}. \end{equation*}
    A primitive vector in a nonunimodular lattice can have divisibility greater than one. For example, either basis vector of the lattice with Gram matrix \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&r\\r&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) is primitive of divisibility \(r\text{.}\)
    For the next statements assume \(B=\PP^n\) and take the specific normalization \(h=c_1(f^*\cO_{\PP^n}(1))\text{.}\) The class \(h\) is primitive by Theorem 6.8. In the notation \(\lambda=m e\) of the specified isotropic bundle, \(h=e\) when \(f\) is its connected fibration and the positive ray is fixed. Its BBF divisibility is a separate invariant.
    Wieneck computed the Hilbert and generalized Kummer cases [Wie16, Theorem 6.1]; [Wie18, Theorem 1.1]. The two O’Grady cases, due to Mongardi–Rapagnetta and Mongardi–Onorati, are included with the same primitive normalization in [Kim25, Theorem 3.16].

    Theorem 2.6.

    Let \(f:X\to\PP^n\) be a Lagrangian fibration, and set \(r=\operatorname{div}(c_1(f^*\cO(1)))\text{.}\) Its primitive fibre polarization has type
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{pol}(f)=\begin{cases}(1,\ldots,1),&X\text{ of }\mathrm{K3}^{[n]}\text{ type},\\(1,\ldots,1,r,(n+1)/r),&X\text{ of }\mathrm{Kum}_n\text{ type},\\(1,2,2),&X\text{ of }\mathrm{OG}_6\text{ type},\\(1,1,1,1,1),&X\text{ of }\mathrm{OG}_{10}\text{ type}.\end{cases} \end{equation*}
    In the generalized Kummer case \(n\ge2\text{,}\) there are \(n-2\) initial entries equal to one, \(r^2\mid n+1\text{,}\) and every positive integer satisfying this condition occurs for a fibration of that type.
    The computations use deformation of the fibration to moduli spaces with explicit fibres and polarizations. Constancy of type then returns the result to the original fibration. For the Hilbert type, the comparison fibres are Jacobians with their principal theta classes. In the Kummer case one obtains abelian subvarieties of Jacobians, whose polarization need not be principal. These moduli constructions are external here; the restrictions they give on the lattice and its intersections can be checked directly.

    Proposition 2.7.

    Let \(f:X\to\PP^n\) be a Lagrangian fibration and \(F\) a smooth fibre. Set \(h=c_1(f^*\cO(1))\) and \(r=\operatorname{div}(h)\text{.}\) Let \(a_f\) be the positive integer determined by
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{im}\bigl(H^2(X,\Z)\to H^2(F,\Z)\bigr)=\Z(a_f\theta_F). \end{equation*}
    If \(\operatorname{pol}(f)=(d_1,\ldots,d_n)\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation} a_f^n\prod_i d_i=\frac{2^n n!}{(2n)!}\,c_X r^n.\tag{2.1} \end{equation}
    For every \(\alpha\in H^2(X,\Z)\text{,}\) one has \(i^*\alpha=(a_f/r)q_X(h,\alpha)\theta_F\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    The two homomorphisms \(i^*\) and \(q_X(h,-)\) have the same real kernel by Proposition 2.3. Choose an integral class \(\alpha_0\) with \(q_X(h,\alpha_0)=r\text{.}\) Since the values of this pairing are exactly \(r\Z\text{,}\) its restriction generates the integral image. Its sign is positive: its intersection with \(\kappa^{n-1}\) is positive by the mixed Fujiki formula. Thus \(i^*\alpha_0=a_f\theta_F\text{,}\) and proportionality gives the last assertion.
    The hyperplane normalization gives \([F]=h^n\text{.}\) Comparing coefficients of \(t^n\) in the Fujiki relation for \(\alpha_0+th\) gives
    \begin{equation*} \binom{2n}{n}\int_Xh^n\alpha_0^n=c_X\,2^nr^n. \end{equation*}
    Divide by \(n!\binom{2n}{n}\) and use \(\int_F\theta_F^n/n!=\prod_i d_i\text{.}\) Since \(1/(n!\binom{2n}{n})=n!/(2n)!\text{,}\) this is the displayed formula.
    The identity is the normalization of [Kim25, Proposition 3.17] appropriate to our Fujiki convention. In that source the coefficient attached to a complete pairing is the small Fujiki constant. Here \(c_X\) is defined by \(\int_X\alpha^{2n}=c_Xq_X(\alpha)^n\text{,}\) so the factor \(2^n n!/(2n)!\) must be retained. The index identity itself uses the hyperplane class and the restriction theorem, independently of the separate primitivity theorem for \(h\text{.}\)

    Example 2.8.

    For \(\mathrm{K3}^{[n]}\) type, \(c_X=(2n)!/(2^nn!)\) and the fibre type is principal. Formula (2.1) gives \(a_f=r\text{.}\) For generalized Kummer type, \(c_X=(n+1)(2n)!/(2^nn!)\) and \(\prod_i d_i=n+1\text{,}\) so again \(a_f=r\text{.}\) These Fujiki constants use the primitive BBF form, as in the examples in General HK.
    In dimension six, generalized Kummer fibrations have two possible types. Divisibility one gives \((1,1,4)\text{,}\) with polarization kernel \((\Z/4\Z)^2\) and integral restriction image \(\Z\theta_F\text{.}\) Divisibility two gives \((1,2,2)\text{,}\) with kernel \((\Z/2\Z)^4\) and restriction image \(2\Z\theta_F\text{.}\) Both primitive polarizations have top intersection \(\int_F\theta_F^3=24\text{.}\) The common unpolarized Fujiki constant is \(60\text{,}\) and the coefficient \(2^3 3!/6!=1/15\) gives \(a_f^3\cdot4=4r^3\text{.}\)
    The two sixfold fibrations belong to the same deformation type of total spaces but cannot be deformation equivalent as fibrations, because their polarization types differ. This also explains why the type is not determined by dimension, or even by deformation type of \(X\text{,}\) alone.

    Example 2.9.

    Let \(S\to\PP^1\) be an elliptic K3 surface with a section. The induced map \(S^{[n]}\to\PP^n\) has, over \(n\) distinct regular values, fibre \(F=E_1\times\cdots\times E_n\text{.}\) A section divisor on \(S\) has degree one on every \(E_i\text{.}\) Its associated divisor on the Hilbert scheme restricts to \(\sum_i\operatorname{pr}_i^*[0]\) on \(F\text{,}\) after choosing the origins supplied by that section. This is the product principal polarization. Its top self-intersection is \(n!\text{,}\) as follows by expanding the product.

    Section 2.4 The period map and Matsushita’s alternative

    Let \(U\subset B\) be the full open set of regular values. The local system \(\mathbb H_\Z=R^1(f|_{X_U})_*\Z\) has rank \(2n\text{.}\) Its flat holomorphic bundle \(\mathcal H=\mathbb H_\Z\otimes\cO_U\) has the Gauss–Manin connection and the holomorphic subbundle
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal F^1=f_*\Omega^1_{X_U/U},\qquad \mathcal F^1_b=H^{1,0}(X_b). \end{equation*}
    Fibrewise, \(\mathcal H_b=\mathcal F^1_b\oplus\overline{\mathcal F^1_b}\text{.}\) These are the weight-one Hodge structures of the fibres. The classes \(\theta_{X_b}\) are parallel and polarize them.1 Thus \(\mathbb H_\Z\) underlies a polarizable variation of Hodge structure.
    Locally choose a marking of the cycle lattice compatible with its polarization. The Hodge subspaces are described by a holomorphic matrix \(\tau\) in Siegel upper half-space. A change of marking acts by the corresponding arithmetic symplectic group. Quotienting gives the period map
    \begin{equation*} \varphi:U\to\mathcal A_{\operatorname{pol}(f)} \end{equation*}
    to the moduli space of polarized abelian varieties of that type. The origin is chosen on the associated abelian variety, not on the original fibre torsor. A finite étale cover imposing a sufficiently high level structure replaces the coarse moduli space by a fine one when needed.

    Definition 2.10.

    The fibration is isotrivial if \(\varphi\) is constant. It has maximal variation if \(\varphi\) is generically finite onto its image, or equivalently if \(\dim\varphi(U)=n\text{.}\)
    For a compact IHS fibration, isotriviality is equivalent to finiteness of the monodromy on \(H^1\) [Bak25, introduction]. One implication has a direct explanation. For constant periods, monodromy preserves a fixed complex structure and its positive polarization metric. It lies in a compact unitary group and also preserves the integral lattice. The intersection is finite, because a bounded set contains only finitely many integral matrices. For the converse, kill the finite monodromy by a finite étale cover of \(U\text{.}\) The fixed-part theorem for this geometric polarized variation makes its Hodge filtration constant.
    The last implication uses the geometric variation over the algebraic open set \(U\text{,}\) with its compactification. It is not a statement about arbitrary local families: on a small simply connected disk, a holomorphic nonconstant map to Siegel space gives a family with trivial monodromy and varying periods. Trivial monodromy on one coordinate neighbourhood is only a choice of marking.
    The symplectic form identifies \(T_bU\) with \(H^0(X_b,\Omega^1_{X_b})\) by contraction with a lift of a base vector. Under the polarization, the derivative of the period map is a symmetric bilinear form in the fibre directions. In special coordinates its entries are \(\partial_k\tau_{ij}\text{,}\) symmetric in all three indices. The prepotential and its Hessian are derived in Section 3.2. At a point of maximal rank, a tangent vector \(v=\sum_kv_k\partial_k\) satisfies \(\sum_kv_k\partial_k\tau_{ij}=0\) for every \(i,j\) only if \(v=0\text{.}\)

    Theorem 2.11 (Bakker).

    Every Lagrangian fibration from a compact IHS Kähler manifold is either isotrivial or of maximal variation.
    This is [Bak25, Theorem 1], quoted here in its compact Kähler form. A restriction on the variation that enters its proof follows directly from the rank-one restriction map.

    Lemma 2.12.

    The real variation \(\mathbb H_\R\) is irreducible as a polarizable variation of Hodge structure.

    Proof.
    The global invariant-cycle theorem identifies
    \begin{equation*} H^0(U,R^2f_*\R)=\operatorname{im}\bigl(H^2(X,\R)\to H^2(F,\R)\bigr)=\R\theta_F. \end{equation*}
    We use the compact Kähler form of that external theorem, as in the proof of [Bak25, Theorem 1]. Suppose that \(\mathbb H_\R=\mathbb V\oplus\mathbb W\) is a nontrivial decomposition into polarizable real subvariations. The polarization on each summand has an inverse alternating tensor. Extending these tensors by zero on the other summand gives two independent flat sections of
    \begin{equation*} \Lambda^2\mathbb H_\R=R^2f_*\R. \end{equation*}
    Their restrictions to \(\Lambda^2\mathbb V\) and \(\Lambda^2\mathbb W\) distinguish them, so they cannot be proportional. This contradicts the one-dimensional invariant subspace.
    Irreducibility here concerns subvariations, including their Hodge structures. It does not assert that a single fibre is a simple abelian variety. In the Hilbert-scheme example, the fibre is a product of elliptic curves, while monodromy permutes its factors.
    The remainder of Bakker’s proof is external here [Bak25, Lemma 5 and proof of Theorem 1]. In the nonisotrivial case, every irreducible complex factor of the pulled-back variation after finite étale Galois base change has level one, meaning that its first Hodge indices occupy two consecutive degrees.2 If the period map has positive-dimensional fibres, the symplectic form produces a nonzero subvariation of level at most one in \(\mathbb H_\R\otimes T^\vee\text{,}\) where \(T=\R\Re[\sigma]\oplus\R\Im[\sigma]\) has types \((2,0),(0,2)\text{.}\) Semisimplicity and the Hodge degrees of the complex factors exclude such a subvariation. These steps use the fixed-part theorem and the mixed Hodge structures associated with the fibres of the period map.
    Both alternatives have geometric examples. An elliptic K3 surface with nonconstant \(j\)-function induces a maximally varying fibration on \(S^{[n]}\text{:}\) on the cover ordering \(n\) distinct base points, the period map is the product of the \(n\) elliptic period maps and has rank \(n\) at general points. If the elliptic K3 fibration is isotrivial, its induced Hilbert-scheme fibration is isotrivial. For another maximally varying example, the Beauville–Mukai system associated with a base point free ample class on a K3 surface has maximal variation [Bak25, Corollary 2].

    Section 2.5 The structure of isotrivial fibrations

    For this section assume that \(X\) is projective and \(f:X\to B\) is isotrivial. This is the setting of Kim–Laza–Martin [KLM26]. Write \(G\) for the finite monodromy image on \(H^1(F,\Z)\text{,}\) and let \(\widetilde U\to U\) be the connected Galois cover corresponding to its kernel. The associated abelian scheme becomes constant on \(\widetilde U\text{.}\) The pulled-back family \(X_U\times_U\widetilde U\) is a torsor under that constant scheme and need not yet be a product. Its translation cocycle is a different datum from the action on \(H^1\text{;}\) see Proposition 5.2.
    The following are external structure and classification theorems [KLM26, Theorems 1.3, 1.4 and 1.6].

    Theorem 2.13 (Kim–Laza–Martin).

    Let \(f:X\to B\) be an isotrivial Lagrangian fibration from a projective IHS manifold of dimension \(2n\text{.}\) Its smooth fibre \(F\) is isogenous to \(E^n\) for an elliptic curve \(E\text{.}\) For the monodromy-killing cover \(\widetilde U\text{,}\) exactly one of the following holds:
    1. \(\widetilde U\) compactifies to an abelian variety isogenous to a power of an elliptic curve.
    2. \(\widetilde U\) has a smooth projective compactification of general type.
    These are called type A and type B. If the fibration is of type A and has a rational section, it is birational, as a fibration, to one induced from an isotrivial elliptic K3 surface on its Hilbert scheme, or to one induced from a homomorphism of an abelian surface to an elliptic curve on its generalized Kummer variety.
    The isogeny assertion concerns the unpolarized abelian variety; it does not identify its lattice or its polarization with the product principal one. The two types concern the ordinary Kodaira dimension of a smooth projective compactification of \(\widetilde U\text{,}\) which is zero or \(n\text{.}\)3 The condition on a rational section remains part of the birational classification theorem.

    Example 2.14.

    Let \(E,C\) be elliptic curves. Resolve the sixteen ordinary double points of \((E\times C)/\langle(-1,-1)\rangle\) to obtain the Kummer K3 surface \(S\text{.}\) The projection induces \(S\to C/\{\pm1\}\simeq\PP^1\text{,}\) with general fibre \(E\text{.}\) Its four singular fibres are of type \(I_0^*\text{.}\) Monodromy around a branch value acts by \(-1\) on \(H^1(E,\Z)\text{.}\) The cover killing this monodromy is the elliptic double cover \(C\to\PP^1\text{,}\) restricted away from its four branch points. Its compactification is elliptic, so this is type A. The induced fibration on \(S^{[n]}\) is also of type A [KLM26, Section 2.4 and Remark 3.3].

    Example 2.15.

    Let \(p(t)\) have degree twelve with twelve distinct roots. The minimal elliptic surface with Weierstrass equation \(y^2=x^3+p(t)\) is a K3 surface with constant \(j=0\) and twelve fibres of Kodaira type II. This is one of the classical isotrivial K3 constructions described in [KLM26, Appendix A]. On the cyclic cover \(u^6=p(t)\text{,}\) the substitution \(x=u^2X\text{,}\) \(y=u^3Y\) makes the equation \(Y^2=X^3+1\text{.}\) The deck action has order six on \(H^1\text{,}\) so this is the monodromy-killing cover.
    Let \(\bar C\) be the smooth compactification of that cover. There are twelve ramification points of index six, and infinity is unramified because the pole order twelve is divisible by six. Riemann–Hurwitz gives
    \begin{equation*} 2g(\bar C)-2=6(-2)+12(6-1)=48. \end{equation*}
    Thus \(g(\bar C)=25\text{,}\) and the fibration is of type B. The associated Hilbert-scheme fibrations give type B examples in every dimension \(2n\) [KLM26, Remark 3.3]. Constant elliptic periods therefore allow distinct global geometries of the covering base.
    The results on codimension-one multiplicity remove an assumption from a further consequence of the isotrivial structure theorem. Let \(\operatorname{End}_{\Q}(E)=\operatorname{End}(E)\otimes\Q\text{.}\)

    Corollary 2.16.

    Let \(f:X\to\PP^n\) be an isotrivial Lagrangian fibration from a projective IHS manifold. Let \(E\) be an elliptic curve such that the smooth fibre \(F\) is isogenous to \(E^n\text{.}\) If
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{End}_{\Q}(E)\notin\{\Q(i),\Q(\sqrt{-3})\}, \end{equation*}
    then \(f\) is of type A and its reduced discriminant has degree \(2(n+1)\text{.}\) If \(f\) also has a rational section, it has one of the two birational descriptions in Theorem 2.13.

    Proof.
    Kim–Oguiso–Shinder prove that \(f^*D\) is not divisible by an integer greater than one for every prime divisor \(D\subset\PP^n\) [KOS26, Theorem 3.4]. Write \(f^*D=\sum_j a_jE_j\text{.}\) Equidimensionality forces every \(E_j\) to dominate \(D\text{.}\) For general \(b\in D\text{,}\) the fibre \((E_j)_b\) of each reduced divisor is generically reduced, since the ground field has characteristic zero. Its irreducible components occur in \(f^{-1}(b)\) with coefficient \(a_j\text{.}\) Thus the gcd of the fibre multiplicities is \(\gcd_j a_j=1\text{.}\) This is the non-multiple condition in [KLM26, Theorem 1.7]. That theorem applies to the projective isotrivial fibration and the stated endomorphism field, giving type A and the degree formula. [KLM26, Theorem 1.6] gives the assertion with a rational section.
    In this case the local monodromy around a general discriminant point has order two, and the singular fibre has characteristic type \(I_0^*\) [KLM26, Corollary 4.12 and Section 4.3]. Let \(D\) be the reduced discriminant. The canonical bundle formula gives \(K_{\PP^n}+\frac12D\sim_{\Q}0\text{,}\) so \(\deg D=2(n+1)\text{.}\) The weights attached to other discriminant constructions and the geometry of its fibres are discussed in Chapter 4.
    Isotriviality is a closed condition in families of Lagrangian fibrations [KLM26, Proposition 6.1]. When \(b_2(X)\ge7\text{,}\) the given fibration has a deformation which is not isotrivial [KLM26, Theorem 1.11]. By Theorem 2.11, that deformation has maximal variation. Thus an isotrivial fibration and a maximally varying one can occur in the same deformation family.
