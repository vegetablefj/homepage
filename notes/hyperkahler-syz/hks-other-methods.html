    Chapter 11 Effective divisors and finiteness of deformation types

    The proof through a degeneration begins with a nef isotropic class and constructs a torus. A different geometric argument begins with an effective divisor and finds Lagrangian subvarieties inside it. In dimension four, Höring–Lazić–Lehn prove semiampleness under a nonvanishing hypothesis by deforming these subvarieties or extending sections from the divisor. Their smoothing theorem applies in every even dimension.
    The last section proves a consequence of hyperkähler SYZ which concerns all deformation types in a fixed dimension with \(b_2\ge5\text{.}\) Its geometric part is the finiteness theorem of Engel–Filipazzi–Greer–Mauri–Svaldi for manifolds admitting a Lagrangian fibration. Its lattice-theoretic part constructs a deformation with a nef isotropic line bundle. We give that reduction in detail.

    Section 11.1 From an effective divisor to a Lagrangian subvariety

    For a line bundle \(L\text{,}\) nonvanishing means \(H^0(X,L^{\otimes m})\ne0\) for some \(m>0\text{,}\) or equivalently \(\kappa(X,L)\ge0\text{.}\) For a nontrivial nef isotropic bundle on a manifold of dimension \(2n\text{,}\) Proposition 1.3 gives \(\nu(L)=n\text{.}\) Nonvanishing is therefore weaker than the equality \(\kappa(X,L)=\nu(L)\) required by abundance. Even the existence of one effective multiple is a separate assertion about sections.
    The four-dimensional theorem of [HLL26, Theorem 1.1] is as follows.

    Theorem 11.1.

    Let \(X\) be a hyperkähler fourfold with \(\Pic(X)=\Z L\text{,}\) where \(L\) is nef and \(q_X(c_1(L))=0\text{.}\) If \(\kappa(X,L)\ge0\text{,}\) then \(L\) is semiample.
    The generator \(L\) is nontrivial. The Picard group contains no class of positive square, so \(X\) is nonprojective by the projectivity criterion in General HK. This hypothesis restricts the divisors on \(X\) and is used throughout the proof.
    Choose an effective divisor in \(|L^{\otimes m}|\) and a prime component \(D\text{.}\) Since the Picard group is generated by \(L\text{,}\) the class of \(D\) is a positive integral multiple of \(c_1(L)\text{.}\) Positivity of the multiple follows by pairing with the cube of a Kähler class. Thus \(D\) is nef and isotropic. If a fibration were already known, a divisor pulled back from a curve in its surface base would have a fibration by abelian surfaces, except where that curve lies in the discriminant. The proof establishes the needed geometry of \(D\) without assuming that a fibration on \(X\) exists.
    If \(\kappa(X,L)>0\text{,}\) take an effective \(D_0\in|L^{\otimes m}|\text{.}\) For sufficiently small rational \(\varepsilon>0\text{,}\) the pair \((X,\varepsilon D_0)\) is klt and
    \begin{equation*} K_X+\varepsilon D_0\sim_\Q\varepsilon mL \end{equation*}
    is nef with positive Kodaira dimension. The four-dimensional case of [HLL25, Theorem 4.1] makes this adjoint divisor semiample. It follows that a positive power of \(L\) is globally generated. The new argument excludes \(\kappa(X,L)=0\text{.}\) In that case the prime divisor is unique. The proof may pass to a very general deformation of the pair in the Hodge locus of \(L\text{;}\) the extension and deformation arguments needed to justify this passage are included in [HLL26, Theorem 7.3 and its proof].
    The analysis of the normalization \(\nu_D:D^\nu\to D\) produces a fibration
    \begin{equation*} \varphi:D^\nu\longrightarrow\Gamma \end{equation*}
    over a curve. If \(D\) is not uniruled, the corresponding fibration on \(D\) has general fibre an abelian or bielliptic surface; see [HLL26, Proposition 4.3]. In the abelian case this is an embedded Lagrangian torus, so Theorem 10.3 gives a fibration on \(X\text{.}\) In the bielliptic case, the finite étale abelian cover is deformed as a map to \(X\) by Theorem 11.2, giving an embedded abelian surface. The possibility of a bielliptic fibre is why deforming maps, rather than only embedded smooth subvarieties, occurs in the proof.
    If \(D\) is uniruled, the general fibre of \(\varphi\) is a \(\PP^1\)-bundle over an elliptic curve by [HLL26, Proposition 5.1]. Its image meets the singularities of \(D\text{.}\) When the relevant singularities are double normal crossings, the fibration on \(D^\nu\) descends to \(D\text{.}\) For singularities of Kodaira types \(II\text{,}\) \(III\text{,}\) and \(IV\text{,}\) the source uses special resolutions, extension of pluricanonical sections, and algebraic approximation to exclude the case \(\kappa(X,L)=0\text{.}\) These singularities are not all normal crossings, so the smoothing theorem in the next section is not applied to them. The resolution and extension arguments are external results from [HLL26, §§5–7].
    The conclusion of that divisor analysis, under the assumption \(\kappa(X,L)=0\) and after the permitted deformation, is a fibration \(\phi:D\to U\) over a curve whose general fibre \(Y\) is a normal crossing Lagrangian surface of Kodaira type \(I\text{.}\) The dualizing sheaf \(\omega_Y\) is torsion by adjunction. The normalization has nef anticanonical divisor. The one-parameter locally trivial family of fibres gives
    \begin{equation*} h^0(Y,\widetilde\Omega_Y^1)\ge1, \end{equation*}
    by the identification of locally trivial normal deformations with torsion-free differentials. The structure theorem for the index-one cover gives the reverse bound. These checks are [HLL26, Lemma 11.2, Corollaries 2.57 and 8.17, and the proof of Theorem 1.1]. Thus \(Y\) satisfies precisely the three hypotheses of Theorem 11.2.
    That theorem gives a Lagrangian fibration on \(X\text{.}\) The pullback of an ample bundle from its base is a positive multiple of \(L\text{,}\) since \(\Pic(X)=\Z L\) and both classes have positive pairing with a Kähler class. Its Kodaira dimension is two. This contradicts \(\kappa(X,L)=0\text{.}\) The deformation theorem for the prescribed class transfers semiampleness back to the original pair. These are the final deductions once the divisor analysis and smoothing theorems have been established.
    The distinction between these cases is geometric. The ruled surface in \(D^\nu\) need not itself be the subvariety that is deformed. One must descend through the normalization, determine the image and its singularities, and check its dualizing sheaf and normal sheaf. Those checks precede the smoothing. Merely finding a ruled surface in a divisor would not establish the existence of a torus in \(X\text{.}\)1
    There is a version for a prescribed bundle without the rank-one assumption. If a general deformation of the pair \((X,L)\) has nonnegative Kodaira dimension, then \(L\) is semiample; see [HLL26, Corollary 1.3]. The deformation keeps the integral class of \(L\text{.}\) As in Theorem 10.5, semiampleness of an unrelated class on a deformation would not give this conclusion for \(L\text{.}\)

    Section 11.2 Normal crossing Lagrangian subvarieties

    A normal crossing variety of dimension \(n\) is locally a hypersurface \(z_1\cdots z_k=0\) in a smooth space of dimension \(n+1\text{.}\) In the theorem below it is embedded in \(X\text{,}\) and its smooth locus is Lagrangian. The dualizing sheaf \(\omega_Y\) is a line bundle because a normal crossing variety is Gorenstein. Let \(\widetilde\Omega_Y^1\) denote the sheaf of Kähler differentials modulo torsion, and let \(Y^\nu\) be the normalization. These are different from reflexive differentials on a normal space.
    The smoothing and fibration theorems in [HLL26, Theorems 10.2 and 1.2] have the following consequences.

    Theorem 11.2.

    Let \(Y\subset X\) be a closed normal crossing Lagrangian subvariety of a hyperkähler manifold of dimension \(2n\text{.}\) Assume that
    \begin{equation*} h^0(Y,\widetilde\Omega_Y^1)=n-1,\qquad \omega_Y\text{ is torsion},\qquad -K_{Y^\nu}\text{ is nef}. \end{equation*}
    Let \(p:Y'\to Y\) be the index-one cover of \(\omega_Y\text{,}\) and let \(f:Y'\to X\) be its composition with the inclusion.
    1. The universal deformation of \(f\) with target \(X\) fixed has a smooth base of dimension \(n\text{.}\) Its evaluation map is generically étale, and a general deformed map is an isomorphism onto a Lagrangian abelian variety in \(X\text{.}\)
    2. There is a holomorphic Lagrangian fibration \(g:X\to B\) such that \(Y\) is the reduced subspace underlying a fibre of \(g\text{.}\)
    The index-one cover can be described without assuming that \(Y\) is normal. If \(r\) is the order of \(\omega_Y\text{,}\) choose an isomorphism \(\omega_Y^{\otimes r}\simeq\cO_Y\) and form
    \begin{equation*} Y'=\operatorname{Spec}_Y \left(\bigoplus_{j=0}^{r-1}\omega_Y^{-j}\right). \end{equation*}
    Multiplication uses the chosen isomorphism. Locally this is the equation \(u^r=a\) with \(a\) a unit. Since the characteristic is zero, \(ru^{r-1}\) is invertible on the cover. Thus \(p\) is finite étale and \(\omega_{Y'}\) is trivial. Deforming \(f\) allows this cover to become an embedded abelian variety, even when the original map \(f\) has degree greater than one onto its image.
    For a smooth Lagrangian submanifold \(F\subset X\text{,}\) contraction with the symplectic form identifies \(N_{F/X}\) with \(\Omega_F^1\text{.}\) For normal crossing \(Y\text{,}\) the corresponding sheaf is a sheaf of logarithmic differentials. The source proves the necessary identification on the index-one cover and the unobstructedness of the fixed-target deformation problem in [HLL26, Theorem 10.1]. Under the hypotheses of Theorem 11.2, its structure theorem gives a trivial logarithmic cotangent bundle of rank \(n\) on \(Y'\text{.}\) This explains both the dimension \(n\) of the deformation space and the rank of its evaluation map.
    At a smooth point \(y'\in Y'\text{,}\) the differential of evaluation contains the map
    \begin{equation*} H^0(Y',p^*N_{Y/X})\longrightarrow(p^*N_{Y/X})_{y'}. \end{equation*}
    Triviality of the logarithmic cotangent bundle makes this map surjective. The tangent directions along \(Y'\) and these \(n\) normal directions span the \(2n\)-dimensional tangent space of \(X\text{.}\) The evaluation map is therefore étale near smooth points of the central source. The global assertion that a general source becomes an abelian variety, and the identification of \(Y\) as a reduced fibre, are included in the quoted theorem.

    Example 11.3.

    Let \(C\) be an irreducible rational curve with one node and arithmetic genus one, and let \(A\) be an abelian variety of dimension \(n-1\text{.}\) The normal crossing variety \(Y=C\times A\) satisfies
    \begin{equation*} h^0(Y,\widetilde\Omega_Y^1)=n-1,\qquad \omega_Y\simeq\cO_Y,\qquad -K_{Y^\nu}\text{ is nef}. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    The normalization is \(\PP^1\times A\text{.}\) A torsion-free differential on \(C\) pulls back to a regular differential on \(\PP^1\text{,}\) so it has no nonzero global sections. The differentials from \(A\) give all global sections of \(\widetilde\Omega_Y^1\text{,}\) of dimension \(n-1\text{.}\) The dualizing sheaf of the nodal genus-one curve is trivial, as is \(\omega_A\text{.}\) Hence \(\omega_Y\) is trivial. On the normalization, \(-K_{Y^\nu}\) is the pullback of \(\cO_{\PP^1}(2)\text{,}\) which is nef.
    This example describes the simplest singular variety allowed by the three numerical conditions. To apply Theorem 11.2, one must in addition have a Lagrangian embedding of \(Y\) in a hyperkähler manifold. The theorem does not construct that embedding from the abstract variety \(C\times A\text{.}\)
    The condition \(h^0(\widetilde\Omega_Y^1)=n-1\) concerns ordinary torsion-free differentials on \(Y\text{.}\) The deformation space in the theorem has dimension \(n\) because it is controlled by logarithmic differentials on the cover. The additional direction smooths the singularity. These two dimensions refer to different sheaves and different deformation problems.2

    Section 11.3 Nonvanishing and positive currents

    A nef class on a compact Kähler manifold has a closed positive current representative. One way to see this is to choose smooth representatives bounded below by \(-\varepsilon\omega\text{,}\) add \(\varepsilon\omega\text{,}\) and take a weak limit as \(\varepsilon\to0\text{.}\) Positivity bounds the mass on the compact manifold, and the limit is closed, positive, and in the prescribed class. The current may be singular.
    The Lelong number \(\nu(T,x)\) measures the logarithmic singularity of a local plurisubharmonic potential of a positive \((1,1)\)-current \(T\text{.}\) Its normalization gives \(\nu([D],x)=\operatorname{mult}_xD\) for an effective divisor. For \(c>0\text{,}\) Siu’s theorem makes
    \begin{equation*} E_c(T)=\{x\in X:\nu(T,x)\ge c\} \end{equation*}
    an analytic subset. A Lelong component is an irreducible component of every \(E_c(T)\) for all sufficiently small positive \(c\text{.}\) A maximal Lelong component means one of maximal dimension among these components, as in [HLL25, Definition 8.1].
    A subvariety \(Z\subset X\) is coisotropic if
    \begin{equation*} (T_zZ)^{\perp_{\sigma}}\subset T_zZ \end{equation*}
    at every smooth point \(z\text{.}\) This forces \(\dim Z\ge n\text{.}\) If \(\dim Z=n\text{,}\) the inclusion is equality and \(Z\) is Lagrangian. The definition is on the smooth locus and does not assume that \(Z\) is nonsingular.
    The geometry of maximal Lelong components is constrained by isotropy. We use [HLL25, Theorem D and Theorem 8.2] in the following form.

    Theorem 11.4.

    Let \(L\) be a nontrivial nef isotropic line bundle on \(X\text{.}\) If \(\kappa(X,L)<0\text{,}\) then, for every closed positive current \(T\in c_1(L)\) and every maximal Lelong component \(Z\) of \(T\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} n\le\dim Z\le2n-2,\qquad c_1(L|_Z)^{\dim Z-n+1}=0. \end{equation*}
    The subvariety \(Z\) is coisotropic. If \(\dim Z=n\text{,}\) it is Lagrangian and \(c_1(L|_Z)=0\text{.}\)
    For a fourfold, any such \(Z\) has dimension two and is Lagrangian. This conclusion gives a subvariety on which the nef class vanishes. It is weaker than the smoothing hypotheses in Theorem 11.2: the current theorem does not assert that \(Z\) has normal crossings or torsion dualizing sheaf.
    There is a direct semiampleness theorem when no logarithmic singularities occur; see [HLL25, Corollary E].

    Theorem 11.5.

    Let \(X\) be a hyperkähler fourfold and let \(L\) be nef with \(q_X(c_1(L))=0\text{.}\) If \(c_1(L)\) contains a closed positive current all of whose Lelong numbers vanish, then \(L\) is semiample.
    The hypothesis allows singular potentials. A smooth semipositive representative is a stronger condition. Conversely, semiampleness produces such a smooth representative: if \(L^{\otimes m}\) is globally generated, the associated morphism to projective space pulls back the Fubini–Study form to a smooth semipositive representative of \(m c_1(L)\text{.}\) Dividing by \(m\) gives one for \(c_1(L)\text{.}\) Thus, for a nef isotropic bundle on a hyperkähler fourfold, Theorem 11.5 makes the existence of a positive representative with zero Lelong numbers equivalent to semiampleness.
    The current arguments use regularization, intersection inequalities, and multiplier ideals. They concern singularities of a positive representative of one prescribed class. The metric argument in Chapter 9 instead studies the Ricci-flat metrics in a varying family of complex structures. Both arguments lead to Lagrangian geometry, but they impose different conditions at their starting point.3

    Section 11.4 Finitely many deformation types in a fixed dimension

    Two compact hyperkähler manifolds are deformation equivalent if they can be joined by a chain of smooth proper families over connected complex bases. The total spaces of the Lagrangian fibrations may vary through nonprojective manifolds. Neither a polarization nor a particular fibration is fixed in this equivalence relation.
    The smooth consequence of [EFGMS25, Theorem B and Corollary A.3] that we need is:

    Theorem 11.6.

    For fixed \(n\text{,}\) compact hyperkähler manifolds of dimension \(2n\) which admit a holomorphic Lagrangian fibration belong to finitely many smooth deformation equivalence classes.
    The cited theorem is formulated more generally for primitive symplectic varieties and locally trivial deformations. Its corollary deforms a Lagrangian fibration to a projective one. Local triviality means that every point has an analytic neighbourhood whose deformation is a product. Starting from a smooth space, such deformations preserve smoothness. The resulting smooth proper families preserve the fundamental group by Ehresmann’s theorem, so a family starting from an IHS manifold remains simply connected. The conclusion therefore specializes to the smooth deformation equivalence just defined. The finiteness is that of deformation classes of total spaces.
    We next construct a nef isotropic bundle on a deformation. This reduction is used in [OAI26a, §6] and goes back to [Saw03, Proposition 4.3].

    Proposition 11.7.

    Let \(X\) be a compact hyperkähler manifold with \(b_2(X)\ge5\text{.}\) There is a deformation \(X_0\) of \(X\) and a nontrivial nef isotropic line bundle \(L_0\) such that \(\Pic(X_0)=\Z L_0\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Let \(\Lambda=H^2(X,\Z)\text{,}\) with its integral BBF form. Write \(r=\operatorname{rk}\Lambda=b_2(X)\text{.}\) The rational quadratic space \(\Lambda_{\Q}\) has signature \((3,r-3)\text{.}\) It is indefinite and has rank at least five. Meyer’s theorem gives a nonzero rational isotropic vector; see [Ser73, Chapter IV, §3.2, Corollary 2]. Clearing denominators and dividing by the greatest common divisor of its integral coordinates gives a primitive vector \(e\in\Lambda\) with \(q_X(e)=0\text{.}\)
    Consider the period domain inside \(\PP(\Lambda_{\C})\text{,}\) and impose \(q_X(\sigma,e)=0\text{.}\) The quotient
    \begin{equation*} e^\perp/\R e \end{equation*}
    has signature \((2,r-4)\text{.}\) It contains oriented positive two-planes, so the imposed period locus is nonempty. For each \(a\in\Lambda\setminus\Q e\text{,}\) the additional equation \(q_X(\sigma,a)=0\) cuts out a proper analytic subset of that locus. Indeed, a functional vanishing on all of \(e^\perp\) would be a multiple of \(q_X(e,-)\text{,}\) which would force \(a\in\R e\text{,}\) hence \(a\in\Q e\) since \(a\) is integral and \(e\) is primitive.
    There are countably many integral vectors \(a\text{.}\) The Baire category theorem permits a period outside these proper subsets, within the relevant period component. Period surjectivity gives a marked deformation \(X_0\) of \(X\) with this period. The integral classes of type \((1,1)\) are exactly
    \begin{equation*} \NS(X_0)=\Z e. \end{equation*}
    Here primitivity of \(e\) is what turns the rational line into the displayed integral lattice. The exponential sequence and \(H^1(X_0,\cO_{X_0})=0\) identify \(\Pic(X_0)\) with this lattice. The period and Picard assertions are reviewed in the deformation theory in General HK.
    No integral \((1,1)\)-class on \(X_0\) has negative square, because every such class is a multiple of \(e\text{.}\) The Kähler cone theorem therefore identifies the Kähler cone with the component of the positive cone containing a Kähler class \(\omega\text{.}\) In the real space \(H^{1,1}(X_0,\R)\text{,}\) the BBF form has signature \((1,r-3)\text{.}\) A nonzero isotropic vector has nonzero pairing with \(\omega\text{,}\) since \(\omega^\perp\) is negative definite. Replace \(e\) by \(-e\) if necessary so that \(q_X(e,\omega)>0\text{.}\)
    The Lorentzian description of the positive cone places this \(e\) in its closure. More explicitly, \(e+\varepsilon\omega\) has positive square and positive pairing with \(\omega\) for every \(\varepsilon>0\text{.}\) It lies in the chosen positive cone and hence in the Kähler cone. Letting \(\varepsilon\to0\) proves that the line bundle \(L_0\) with \(c_1(L_0)=e\) is nef. It is nontrivial and isotropic, and it generates \(\Pic(X_0)\text{.}\)
    The only use of the rank hypothesis in this proof is Meyer’s theorem. A specified isotropic class does not require that hypothesis. This is why the hyperkähler SYZ theorem itself has no assumption \(b_2\ge5\text{.}\)

    Corollary 11.8.

    For each \(n\text{,}\) compact hyperkähler manifolds of dimension \(2n\) with \(b_2\ge5\) belong to finitely many smooth deformation equivalence classes.

    Proof.
    For every such manifold, Proposition 11.7 gives a deformation carrying a nontrivial nef isotropic line bundle. By Theorem 10.6, that bundle is semiample. The associated map is a holomorphic Lagrangian fibration by Proposition 1.7. Every deformation class under consideration therefore contains a manifold to which Theorem 11.6 applies. That theorem leaves finitely many classes.
    Finiteness of deformation types gives a non-effective bound on the second Betti number in fixed dimension. Take the maximum of \(4\) and the second Betti numbers of representatives of the finite list in Corollary 11.8. This bounds \(b_2\) for every hyperkähler manifold of that dimension, including any with \(b_2\le4\text{.}\) The argument does not determine the finite list or the value of its maximum.
    Finiteness of deformation types is also different from boundedness by one algebraic family of finite type. Even unpolarized K3 surfaces illustrate the distinction: they form one smooth deformation class, whereas projective K3 surfaces of all polarization degrees do not belong to one bounded algebraic family. The theorem used here asserts the finite number of deformation classes. No uniform polarization degree or finite-type parameter space for all the manifolds is asserted.
