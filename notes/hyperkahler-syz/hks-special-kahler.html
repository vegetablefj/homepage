    Chapter 8 Special Kähler geometry and semi-flat metrics

    Over its regular values, a holomorphic Lagrangian fibration is a family of complex tori. Its holomorphic symplectic form turns fibre cycles into closed one-forms on the base. A polarization then gives a Kähler metric. We derive the resulting affine structures and local potentials before constructing the associated semi-flat hyperkähler metric. These constructions concern the smooth part of a given fibration.

    Section 8.1 The period lattice

    Let \(f:Z\to U\) be a proper holomorphic submersion with connected Lagrangian fibres of dimension \(n\text{,}\) where \((Z,\sigma)\) is holomorphic symplectic and \(U\) is smooth of dimension \(n\text{.}\) Assume a locally constant family of integral polarizations on the fibres. The restriction of a relatively ample line bundle supplies such a family. This is the smooth part of an algebraic completely integrable system in the terminology of [Fre99, Definition 3.1].1
    A base covector \(\alpha\in T_u^*U\) defines a vertical vector field on \(Z_u\) by \(\iota_{V_\alpha}\sigma=-f^*\alpha\text{.}\) The Lagrangian condition and nondegeneracy of \(\sigma\) make this an isomorphism from base covectors to invariant fibre vector fields. For a local function \(h\text{,}\) the field \(V_{dh}\) is Hamiltonian. Hamiltonians pulled back from the base Poisson commute, so these vertical fields commute. Their flows give a transitive action of \(T_u^*U\) on the compact fibre. Its kernel is a full real lattice \(\Lambda_u\) of rank \(2n\text{,}\) and \(Z_u\) is a torsor under \(T_u^*U/\Lambda_u\text{.}\)

    Proposition 8.1.

    After shrinking \(U\) about any point, there is a holomorphic Lagrangian section of \(f\) and a holomorphic symplectic isomorphism
    \begin{equation*} Z\simeq T^*U/\Lambda \end{equation*}
    over \(U\text{.}\) Each local section of \(\Lambda\subset T^*U\) is a closed holomorphic one-form. The holomorphic symplectic form on the quotient is induced by \(\sigma_{\rm can}=-d\vartheta\text{,}\) where \(\vartheta\) is the tautological one-form.

    Proof.
    Choose a local holomorphic section \(s\) of the submersion. Exponentiating the vertical fields gives a local covering \(\Phi:T^*U\to Z\) whose kernel in each fibre is \(\Lambda\text{.}\) The contraction identity for \(V_\alpha\) gives
    \begin{equation*} \Phi^*\sigma=\sigma_{\rm can}+\pi^*(s^*\sigma), \end{equation*}
    where \(\pi:T^*U\to U\) is the projection. Both sides have the same contraction with every vertical vector, so their difference is pulled back from the base; restriction to the zero section determines that difference.
    Translation by a local lattice section \(\alpha\) does not change \(\Phi\text{.}\) On the cotangent bundle, that translation sends \(\sigma_{\rm can}\) to \(\sigma_{\rm can}-\pi^*d\alpha\text{.}\) Thus \(d\alpha=0\text{.}\) The closed holomorphic two-form \(s^*\sigma\) is locally \(d\gamma\) by the holomorphic Poincaré lemma. Replacing \(s\) by its translate by \(\gamma\) makes its pullback of \(\sigma\) zero. The covering therefore descends to the asserted symplectic isomorphism.
    The lattice is the local system of fibre one-cycles, embedded in \(T^*U\) by integration of \(\sigma\text{.}\) Explicitly, for a locally transported cycle \(\gamma\) and \(v\in T_uU\text{,}\) its period covector is \(\int_{\gamma_u}\iota_{\widetilde v}\sigma\text{,}\) up to the common sign determined by the Hamiltonian convention. Here \(\widetilde v\) is any lift of \(v\text{.}\) Two lifts differ by a vertical field, and \(\sigma\) vanishes on two vertical vectors, so the covector is well-defined. Closedness also follows from fibre integration and \(d\sigma=0\text{.}\) These are the period forms used below [Fre99, Section 3].
    The quotient \(J=T^*U/\Lambda\) has a zero section and is the associated family of abelian varieties. The original \(Z\) is locally isomorphic to \(J\) after choosing a Lagrangian section. The lattice and its periods make sense without a global section.

    Section 8.2 Special coordinates and the prepotential

    The polarization gives a flat nondegenerate alternating form on \(\Lambda_\R\text{.}\) Choose a real symplectic frame of this local system, with period forms denoted \(dz_1,\ldots,dz_n,dw_1,\ldots,dw_n\text{.}\) They have holomorphic primitives by Proposition 8.1. The Riemann bilinear relations imply that the \(z_i\) are holomorphic coordinates and
    \begin{equation*} dw_i=\sum_j\tau_{ij}\,dz_j,\qquad \tau^t=\tau,\qquad Y:=\Im\tau>0. \end{equation*}
    Thus \(\tau\) takes values in Siegel upper half-space. It is the period matrix in this frame.
    A real symplectic frame need not be an integral basis. If the polarization has type \((d_1,\ldots,d_n)\text{,}\) choose integral cycles \(\gamma_i,\delta_i\) with pairing \(\langle\gamma_i,\delta_j\rangle=d_i\delta_{ij}\text{.}\) The normalized frame \(\gamma_i/d_i,\delta_i\) is real symplectic, and the actual period lattice is generated by \(d_i dz_i,dw_i\text{.}\) The formulas in normalized coordinates hold for every polarization type. In the principally polarized case all \(d_i=1\text{,}\) and this normalized frame is integral.2
    Since \(\tau\) is symmetric, the holomorphic one-form \(\sum_iw_i dz_i\) is closed. There is a local holomorphic function \(F\text{,}\) called a prepotential, such that
    \begin{equation*} w_i=\frac{\partial F}{\partial z_i},\qquad \tau_{ij}=\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial z_i\partial z_j}. \end{equation*}
    The closedness of the period forms makes \(\partial_k\tau_{ij}\) symmetric in all three indices. This is the local cubic condition on the period map. It is an extra condition on a family of polarized abelian varieties: a general such family, even with base dimension \(n\text{,}\) need not arise from a holomorphic Lagrangian system [Fre99, Remark 3.6].
    Write \(z=x+iu\) and \(w=p+iv\text{,}\) and set \(y=-p\text{.}\) All four symbols denote real \(n\)-tuples. Taking real parts in \(dw=\tau dz\) gives
    \begin{equation*} dy=-(\Re\tau)\,dx+Y\,du. \end{equation*}
    Since \(Y\) is positive definite, \((x,y)\) are local real coordinates. Define
    \begin{equation*} \omega_B=\sum_i dx_i\wedge dy_i=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{i,j}Y_{ij}\,dz_i\wedge d\overline z_j. \end{equation*}
    The associated Riemannian metric is
    \begin{equation*} g_B=\sum_{i,j}Y_{ij}(dx_i\otimes dx_j+du_i\otimes du_j). \end{equation*}
    These formulas agree on overlaps because the normalized period frames differ by constant real symplectic transformations. They give a global Kähler structure once the polarization and \(\sigma\) have been fixed [Fre99, Theorem 3.4].
    A Kähler potential is \(K=\frac12\Im\sum_i\overline z_iw_i\text{:}\) differentiation gives \(\omega_B=i\partial\bar\partial K\text{.}\) Changes in the primitives or in the symplectic frame can change this potential while preserving the form. The potential \(F\) and its coordinates are local; the period lattice is the globally defined object.

    Section 8.3 Two integral affine structures and a Hessian metric

    Definition 8.2.

    An integral affine structure on a real \(r\)-manifold is an atlas whose coordinate changes are \(q\mapsto Aq+b\text{,}\) with \(A\in\mathrm{GL}(r,\Z)\) and \(b\in\R^r\text{.}\) Equivalently, it is a full lattice in the real cotangent bundle locally generated by closed one-forms.
    Taking real parts of lattice period forms gives a lattice in \(T_\R^*U\text{.}\) Taking imaginary parts gives another. Both are locally generated by closed forms, so they define two integral affine structures. In normalized coordinates, the first connection makes \(dx_i,dy_i\) parallel, and the second makes \(du_i,dv_i\) parallel. For polarization type \((d_i)\text{,}\) integral affine coordinates can be taken as \((d_ix_i,y_i)\) and \((d_iu_i,v_i)\text{,}\) with harmless sign changes in the chosen basis. The normalized coordinates give the same flat connections.
    The first flat connection has an intrinsic compatibility with the Kähler structure. Denote the complex structure on \(U\) by \(I_B\text{.}\)

    Definition 8.3.

    An affine special Kähler structure consists of a Kähler manifold \((U,I_B,g_B,\omega_B)\) and a real connection \(\nabla\) on \(T_\R U\) such that \(\nabla\) is flat and torsion-free, \(\nabla\omega_B=0\text{,}\) and
    \begin{equation*} d^\nabla I_B=0. \end{equation*}
    The last condition means \((\nabla_aI_B)b=(\nabla_bI_B)a\) for real vector fields \(a,b\text{.}\)
    This is Freed’s special Kähler structure [Fre99, Definition 1.1]. The adjective “affine” distinguishes it from projective special Kähler geometry, a different notion. The base itself can be an open set in a projective variety; that does not change which definition applies.

    Proposition 8.4.

    The connection with parallel coframe \((dx,dy)\) makes \((U,I_B,g_B,\omega_B)\) affine special Kähler. In these affine coordinates the metric is the Hessian of
    \begin{equation*} H(x,y)=\Im F(z)+\sum_i u_i y_i. \end{equation*}
    The connection with parallel coframe \((dv,du)\) is dual to \(\nabla\) with respect to \(g_B\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    The closed coordinate coframe gives flatness and zero torsion. The expression \(\omega_B=\sum_i dx_i\wedge dy_i\) gives \(\nabla\omega_B=0\text{.}\) Holomorphicity of \(z,w\) implies
    \begin{equation*} du_i=-dx_i\circ I_B,\qquad dv_i=dy_i\circ I_B. \end{equation*}
    Both right-hand sides are closed. In a parallel frame this is exactly \(d^\nabla I_B=0\text{.}\)
    Let \(A=\Re\tau\text{.}\) Since \(dF=\sum_iw_i dz_i\text{,}\) one has
    \begin{equation*} dH=\sum_i(v_i dx_i+u_i dy_i). \end{equation*}
    The period relation gives \(du=Y^{-1}(dy+A\,dx)\) and \(dv=Y\,dx+A\,du\text{.}\) Consequently, in the order \(q=(x,y)\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Hess}_q H=\begin{pmatrix}Y+AY^{-1}A&AY^{-1}\\Y^{-1}A&Y^{-1}\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Substitution in the formula for \(g_B\) gives exactly this matrix. It is positive definite. Its determinant is one, by taking the Schur complement of \(Y^{-1}\text{.}\)
    For a Hessian metric \(g_{ab}=\partial_a\partial_bH\text{,}\) define the dual connection \(\nabla^*\) by
    \begin{equation*} a\bigl(g_B(b,c)\bigr)=g_B(\nabla_a b,c)+g_B(b,\nabla_a^*c). \end{equation*}
    Its parallel coordinates are the gradient coordinates \(r_a=\partial H/\partial q_a\text{,}\) since \(dr_a=\sum_b g_{ab}dq_b\) and the displayed identity makes these forms parallel. Here \(r=(v,u)\text{.}\) This proves the assertion about the second connection.
    The Legendre transform \(H^*(r)=\sum_a q_ar_a-H(q)\) has \(q=\nabla_rH^*\) and \(\operatorname{Hess}_rH^*=(\operatorname{Hess}_qH)^{-1}\text{.}\) Thus the two real affine structures are related by the metric and Legendre duality. The equation \(\det\operatorname{Hess}_qH=1\) is a real Monge–Ampère equation in normalized symplectic affine coordinates. An integral affine change for a nonprincipal polarization changes its constant by a fixed positive factor.
    The dual connection can also be written \(\nabla_a^*b=I_B^{-1}\nabla_a(I_Bb)\text{.}\) Indeed, use \(g_B(b,c)=\omega_B(b,I_Bc)\) and \(\nabla\omega_B=0\) in its defining identity. The condition \(d^\nabla I_B=0\) gives zero torsion for \(\nabla^*\text{.}\) It does not say \(\nabla I_B=0\text{;}\) that stronger condition would force \(g_B\) to be flat.

    Section 8.4 Variation and curvature

    The third derivatives of \(F\) measure both the variation of the fibres and the curvature of the base metric. Put \(C_{ijk}=\partial_i\partial_j\partial_kF\text{.}\) With the curvature convention for the Hermitian matrix \(Y\)
    \begin{equation*} R_{i\overline j k\overline\ell}=-\partial_i\partial_{\overline j}Y_{k\ell}+\sum_{p,q}Y^{pq}(\partial_iY_{kq})(\partial_{\overline j}Y_{p\ell}), \end{equation*}
    the holomorphicity of \(\tau\) gives
    \begin{equation*} R_{i\overline j k\overline\ell}=\frac14\sum_{p,q}Y^{pq}C_{ikq}\overline{C_{j\ell p}}. \end{equation*}
    Here \(Y^{pq}\) denotes the inverse matrix. To verify the factor, use \(\partial_iY_{kq}=C_{ikq}/(2i)\) and \(\partial_{\overline j}Y_{p\ell}=-\overline{C_{jp\ell}}/(2i)\text{.}\) The mixed second derivative of \(Y\) vanishes.
    For tangent vectors \(a,b\text{,}\) the bisectional curvature is a positive multiple of the squared \(Y^{-1}\)-norm of \(C(a,b,\cdot)\text{,}\) hence is nonnegative. The metric is flat exactly when \(C=0\text{,}\) or equivalently when \(\tau\) is locally constant. Its Ricci curvature in direction \(a\) vanishes exactly when \(d\tau(a)=0\text{.}\) In particular, an immersive period map gives positive Ricci curvature. These are the curvature relations used by Li–Tosatti in their proof of the smooth-base theorem [LT24, Section 2.3].3
    The flat connection \(\nabla\) is different from the Levi-Civita connection of \(g_B\text{.}\) Flatness of the integral affine structure is compatible with a curved Kähler metric because the Hessian \(\operatorname{Hess}H\) varies in its affine coordinates. The logarithmic example below exhibits this distinction in dimension one.

    Section 8.5 The semi-flat hyperkähler metric

    The connection \(\nabla\) splits the tangent bundle of the real cotangent bundle into horizontal and vertical summands. Write a tangent vector in this splitting as \((a,\alpha)\in T_bU\oplus T_b^*U\text{.}\) Define
    \begin{equation*} g_{\rm sf}\bigl((a,\alpha),(b,\beta)\bigr)=g_B(a,b)+g_B^{-1}(\alpha,\beta). \end{equation*}
    This is the semi-flat metric. Its restriction to each cotangent fibre is flat.

    Proposition 8.5.

    The metric \(g_{\rm sf}\) is hyperkähler on \(T^*U\text{.}\) It descends to the quotient \(J=T^*U/\Lambda\text{.}\) In the complex structure of the holomorphic cotangent bundle, its holomorphic symplectic form is \(\sigma_{\rm can}\text{.}\)

    Proof.
    Identify a real covector \(\alpha\) with the holomorphic covector \(\alpha-i\alpha\circ I_B\text{.}\) The special Kähler condition makes the horizontal splitting compatible with this complex structure [Fre99, Proposition 1.22]. In the splitting define
    \begin{equation*} I(a,\alpha)=(I_Ba,I_B^*\alpha),\qquad J(a,\alpha)=(-g_B^{-1}\alpha,g_Ba),\qquad K=IJ, \end{equation*}
    where \(I_B^*\alpha=\alpha\circ I_B\) and \(g_Ba\) is metric duality. Hermitian compatibility gives \(I_B^*g_B=-g_BI_B\text{.}\) Hence \(I^2=J^2=K^2=-1\) and \(IJ=-JI\text{,}\) and all three are orthogonal for \(g_{\rm sf}\text{.}\)
    The forms defined by \(\omega_T(\xi,\eta)=g_{\rm sf}(T\xi,\eta)\) satisfy \(\omega_J+i\omega_K=\sigma_{\rm can}\text{,}\) so \(\omega_J,\omega_K\) are closed. In affine Darboux coordinates \((x,y)\text{,}\) write a real fibre covector as \(\sum_i(a_i dx_i+b_i dy_i)\text{.}\) The remaining form is
    \begin{equation*} \omega_I=\sum_i dx_i\wedge dy_i-\sum_i da_i\wedge db_i. \end{equation*}
    It is closed as well. The closedness criterion for an almost hyperkähler triple gives integrability and parallelness of \(I,J,K\text{;}\) this is the argument of [Fre99, Theorem 2.1].
    Lattice sections are parallel for the connection induced on the cotangent bundle: their real parts are the defining parallel forms. Translation by any such section preserves the horizontal splitting, the metric and the three forms. The construction therefore descends to \(J\text{.}\)
    The same metric has an explicit period expression. For a principal polarization, let \(\theta_i,\phi_i\) be real fibre coordinates modulo integers and set \(\eta_i=d\theta_i+\sum_j\tau_{ij}d\phi_j\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} g_{\rm sf}=\sum_{i,j}Y_{ij}\,dz_i\,d\overline z_j+\sum_{i,j}Y^{ij}\eta_i\overline\eta_j,\qquad \sigma_{\rm can}=\sum_i dz_i\wedge\eta_i. \end{equation*}
    In the metric expression a product with its conjugate denotes its real symmetric part. The fibre holomorphic coordinates on the universal cover are \(\zeta_i=\theta_i+\sum_j\tau_{ij}\phi_j\text{.}\) Symmetry of \(\partial_k\tau_{ij}\) gives \(\sum_i dz_i\wedge d\zeta_i=\sum_i dz_i\wedge\eta_i\text{.}\) Thus the apparent additional terms involving \(d\tau\) cancel. For polarization type \((d_i)\text{,}\) the same expression holds with \(\theta_i\) taken modulo \(d_i\Z\) and \(\phi_i\) modulo \(\Z\text{.}\)
    A chosen local Lagrangian section transfers this metric from \(J\) to \(Z\text{.}\) A different section may translate by a closed one-form that is not parallel. Such a translation changes the horizontal splitting and can change the metric. The Jacobian has the canonical zero section; a torsor requires additional gluing choices for a global semi-flat metric. The period lattice and the special Kähler metric on its base do not have this ambiguity.
    Replacing the fibre term by \(\varepsilon\) times that term gives another hyperkähler metric, with holomorphic symplectic form \(\sqrt\varepsilon\,\sigma_{\rm can}\text{.}\) Locally it is the pullback of the preceding metric by fibre dilation by \(\sqrt\varepsilon\text{,}\) so the claim follows from the same construction and descends by parallel lattice translations. Its fibre diameters are of order \(\sqrt\varepsilon\text{,}\) while the horizontal metric is \(g_B\text{.}\) This describes a local collapse model on the regular locus.

    Section 8.6 Examples and collapse near the discriminant

    Example 8.6.

    Let \(\tau\) be a constant symmetric matrix with positive imaginary part. On a coordinate ball in \(\C^n\text{,}\) take \(F(z)=\frac12z^t\tau z\text{.}\) Both affine structures are linear, \(g_B\) is flat, and the semi-flat total space is locally a product with a flat polarized torus. Nontrivial integral monodromy may still occur after taking a quotient of the base; local constancy of periods is a local statement.

    Example 8.7.

    On a punctured disk \(0<|z|<1\text{,}\) choose a branch of the logarithm and let
    \begin{equation*} \tau(z)=\frac{\log z}{2\pi i},\qquad w(z)=\frac{z(\log z-1)}{2\pi i}. \end{equation*}
    Then \(dw=\tau dz\) and \(\Im\tau=-\log|z|/(2\pi)>0\text{.}\) A local primitive of \(w\,dz\) is a prepotential. The lattice generated by \(dz,dw\) defines a torus fibration over the punctured disk: after one positive circuit, \(dw\) becomes \(dw+dz\text{,}\) so the lattice is preserved.
    The base metric and the first affine monodromy are
    \begin{equation*} g_B=\frac{-\log|z|}{2\pi}|dz|^2,\qquad (x,y)\longmapsto(x,y-x). \end{equation*}
    The second monodromy is \((u,v)\mapsto(u,v+u)\text{.}\) The affine connections are flat, but \(\tau'(z)\ne0\text{,}\) so the Kähler metric is curved. The puncture is at finite radial distance because \(\int_0^r\sqrt{-\log t}\,dt<\infty\text{.}\) The lattice and the semi-flat metric have singular behaviour there. Extending across that point requires a singular-fibre model.
    For a compact IHS fibration \(f:X\to\PP^n\text{,}\) the preceding formulas apply on the complement \(B^\circ\) of the critical values. They do not determine how a Ricci-flat metric behaves uniformly near singular fibres. That comparison is an analytic theorem.
    Tosatti–Zhang prove the following for an existing projective hyperkähler fibration [TZ20, Theorem 1.2]. Choose Kähler forms \(\omega_B\) on \(\PP^n\) and \(\omega_X\) on \(X\text{,}\) and let \(\omega_t\) be the Ricci-flat representative of \(f^*[\omega_B]+t[\omega_X]\text{.}\) As \(t\to0\text{,}\) these metrics converge on compact subsets of the regular locus to the pullback of a special Kähler metric on \(B^\circ\text{.}\) Their Gromov–Hausdorff limit is the metric completion of that base metric. The completion is compact and homeomorphic to \(\PP^n\text{,}\) and its complement of \(B^\circ\) has real Hausdorff codimension at least two. The special Kähler normalization is the one associated with the chosen limiting metric and fibre polarization.
    This theorem begins with a holomorphic fibration and studies its collapsing Ricci-flat metrics. The metric existence theorem used in Chapter 4 begins instead with a degeneration and produces special Lagrangian tori in a specified class. Hyperkähler rotation connects these questions, but the period calculation and the semi-flat model alone do not prove either global analytic theorem.
