Chapter Notes on AI(4Math)

This text was organized with AI assistance from earlier ideas and discussions, then manually revised. It does not fully represent the author’s views.

Section 1. Norms and the Pace of Research

Standards can guide aspects of AI4Math, but once effective tools are widely available, it is difficult to coordinate a slowdown among all participants. Researchers, institutions, and companies have different goals. Using tools can bring direct benefits, while voluntarily slowing down may carry opportunity costs. Rules on publication, disclosure, verification, and evaluation can still influence research practices, but it is difficult to control the pace of all mathematical activity collectively. Developing and revising these rules may remain an ongoing process alongside the production of results; consensus cannot always be reached before new capabilities are put to use.

Section 2. AI Involvement and Disclosure

As AI becomes involved in reading, discussion, trial and error, checking, and writing, its role in ordinary research may become increasingly difficult to delineate. It is difficult to determine reliably from a finished paper what help went into an idea. Usage records may not allow the origins of an idea to be divided into percentages either: a single explanation, or a suggestion taken up by a collaborator, can indirectly change the course of research. Partial records can still help explain the process and guide verification, but the distinction between “used AI” and “did not use AI” conveys only limited information. If disclosure itself leads to a lower assessment of results of the same quality, it may encourage concealment. Certifying work as “entirely human” may, in turn, come to depend on reputation and personal trust. The role of disclosure needs to be considered in light of how tools were actually used and what the evaluation is intended to assess.

Section 3. Mathematical Value and Standards of Evaluation

The mathematical value of a result, an author’s actual contribution, and the independent ability demonstrated by that author are related but distinct. A result may be important even if the person who finally completes it has done relatively little work. Conversely, the emergence of a stronger result can change the merits of publishing work that took years to complete independently. Research training can emphasize the process, while evaluating results also requires attention to their content, methods, and impact. Publication rate, time spent, and methodological complexity offer only partial clues. High output does not automatically imply carelessness, and researchers are not obliged to be prolific. A direct method may be entirely appropriate; the value of another proof needs to be grounded in new structures, generalizations, or understanding. Relying on output, difficulty, or the extent of AI involvement to make all these judgments risks conflating different objects of evaluation.

Section 4. Harnesses and the Assessment of Contributions

A harness that organizes retrieval, search, verification, and coordination around a model can constitute a contribution to software or research methodology. Decomposing a particular problem, supplying lemmas, or changing the direction of a search may also involve mathematical contributions. Developers of a general-purpose system do not automatically become the discoverers of every result it produces, nor does the person who finally invokes the tool automatically deserve all the credit. “One-shot” also requires a clearly defined system boundary: one external run may contain many model calls, and substantial human preparation may already be embedded in the input. The First Proof third-batch benchmark, for example, defines a single attempt at the level of the entire system. Success rates, costs, robustness, and human intervention can be compared under fixed conditions, but these measurements cannot directly yield a uniquely determined division of credit for a theorem.

Section 5. Research Incentives and Updating Results

Evaluation practices influence what researchers are willing to make public. Consider an extreme but possible scenario: AI is asked only to revise a paper, yet unexpectedly proposes a stronger claim. Once that claim has been verified, the original result may become a special case, changing the merits of publishing the original manuscript on its own. The original method and insights still need to be assessed individually. If preserving an “AI-free” label is more rewarding than disclosing an improvement, researchers may have an incentive to withhold known results, putting evaluation in conflict with knowledge sharing. There can be sound reasons to limit a project’s scope, develop independent ability, or leave further directions unexplored; researchers are not obliged to pursue every possible generalization. The issue is how evaluation treats improvements already obtained and verified, rather than a demand to expand research without limit.

Section 6. Correctness, Verification, and Trust

Correctness should be a basic requirement of mathematical work; interest, importance, and an author’s reputation cannot replace proof. Mathematical practice already has a history of errors, gaps filled informally, and corrections that were never made public. Trust built over time must remain open to specific checks. AI can assist with review, but may repeatedly overlook the same missing assumption. The confidence provided by repeated checks depends on what was actually examined. Formal proofs provide a basis for independent verification, while still requiring confirmation that the statement faithfully expresses the original problem and clarification of the definitions and assumptions used. Lean’s guidance on validating proofs likewise distinguishes the validity of a proof from the meaning of its statement. Different verification methods provide different grounds for confidence; none should receive unlimited credibility merely because of its form or the status of those involved.

Section 7. Publication Venues and the Status of Knowledge

Journals, preprints, personal websites, and platforms such as GitHub serve somewhat different purposes. Journals generally organize selection and evaluation through editors and referees; preprints facilitate the public sharing of research; websites and code repositories are also suited to preserving explanations, programs, and ongoing revisions. arXiv explicitly states that its moderation is not peer review. There is no simple correspondence between venue and reliability: a refereed text may still contain errors, while a result that has not appeared in a journal may already have substantial support from verification. If AI significantly accelerates production and revision, these different states may fall further out of sync. Where a work appears, which version has undergone which checks, and whether the mathematical community has understood and used it need to be described separately.

Section 8. Priority, Contributions, and Reputation

The same work may first appear on a personal website or in a repository, then become a preprint and a journal article, with corrections, formalizations, and generalizations contributed by others along the way. The first public statement of a result, the completion of a crucial proof, the explanation of its significance, and its widespread adoption need not come from the same people. Dated records help reconstruct what happened, but cannot establish originality and contribution on their own; publishing an incomplete claim earlier does not automatically confer credit for everything that follows. Attribution may become harder if AI makes closely related results more likely to appear in quick succession. The allocation of credit and the formation of reputation do not proceed in step either: a later standard version may attract more citations, while the original discovery and maintenance work remain less visible. Attention on platforms and corporate publicity may also confer reputation before adequate checking has taken place. As opportunities for dissemination expand, contributions still need to be identified through the work actually done.

Section 9. Academic Authority and Long-Term Adjustment

Past achievements provide evidence of relevant ability, but do not guarantee the reliability of every present judgment. When AI helps people without comparable credentials recheck complex work, find counterexamples, or make progress on problems, some authority built on the scarcity of expertise may be challenged. Public research-level tests already provide records of capabilities that can be discussed, but lower barriers to independent judgment do not mean that positions, publication channels, and authority over appointments change at the same pace. Such adjustments may span generations, perhaps taking a generation or two. Accumulated experience and slowly formed consensus help guard against hasty judgments, preserve knowledge, and train researchers; the same stability may also shelter outdated standards and delay the acceptance of new evidence that has been thoroughly checked. Caution and stubbornness in the mathematical community’s response to change may coexist within the same mechanisms.

Section 10. Progress across Fields and Differences in Tools

Advances in AI may not translate evenly into progress across mathematical fields. How problems are expressed, the quality of the literature, existing tools, the difficulty of verification, and the background required may all affect how much a field benefits. Different models, reasoning budgets, and ways of using them can also produce very different experiences. When some powerful tools are not yet widely available, assessments of their capabilities may be especially out of step. Fields well served by available tools may attract more investment, but the mathematical value of slower-moving fields cannot be judged by output alone. If hiring, funding, and reputation continue to follow mainly results that are easy to showcase, these differences may further affect the movement of researchers and the choice of problems.

Section 11. Research Choices in Different Environments

Disagreements about AI may arise both from different experiences and from different priorities in research. Geography, education, secular outlooks, religious beliefs, and social norms can offer clues to these differences; funding, career circumstances, and access to tools also need consideration. These factors intersect within the same regions and academic circles. Avoiding new evidence that has been thoroughly checked is open to criticism, but continuing to work without AI assistance does not by itself show a failure to consider the future. Research without AI assistance, modest AI assistance, and heavy reliance on AI may coexist for a long time. They may place different emphases on personal understanding, training, or producing results, while still intersecting over priority, resources, and recognition. A single set of standards may not accommodate all of these differences.

Section 12. The Growth of Results and Shared Understanding

One possible scenario is that AI can produce correct, clear, and readily readable proofs, yet new material still arrives faster than humanity as a whole can absorb it. Selective reading can allocate attention, but may not eliminate the overall backlog. The number of propositions is not a direct measure of substantive new content: removing duplication, generalizing results, and developing unifying theories may reduce the burden of understanding, and AI may also participate in this work. There is no guarantee, however, that organization will always keep pace with production. To use more results promptly, some judgments may need to be delegated to machines; reliably using a result, explaining the reasoning of its proof, and incorporating it into a theory remain different levels of mastery. Knowledge may continue to accumulate while shared understanding takes longer to form.

Section 13. Evolving Knowledge and Community Norms

“Knowledge is alive; papers are dead” expresses a vision for organizing knowledge: papers preserve the historical record, while the sources, assumptions, proof versions, and corrections of mathematical statements continue to be updated, with relations of implication, equivalence, and dependency available for inspection. Achieving this requires more than a convenient interface. Identifying relationships across different definitions may itself require mathematical work, while maintenance requires funding, assigned responsibilities, and ways to resolve disputes. Public corrections may affect relationships and reputations, and efforts to organize knowledge coherently may change how existing contributions are credited. The accuracy and coherence needed by shared knowledge may not always align with individual publication incentives or the habits of particular research circles. AI may reduce some costs, but it will not automatically bring participation or recognition. Linking versions across different publication channels could also help preserve the history of corrections and contributions.

Section 14. Commercial Interest and Specialized Systems

Mathematical results can demonstrate AI capabilities, but it is uncertain whether sustained support for pure mathematics serves commercial goals. Companies may turn to other uses, and the benefits of continued progress in general-purpose models may not extend to tools and infrastructure specific to mathematics. Cheaper systems better suited to mathematics cannot be taken for granted as a fallback: building tools around models, adapting existing models, and training new research systems may require different resources and produce different capabilities. The costs of training, use, and maintenance need to be considered separately. Corporate investment and the suitability of available tools may also indirectly influence research directions, drawing more attention to problems that are easier to advance or showcase. This does not, by itself, establish that those problems have greater mathematical value.

Section 15. Supply and External Conditions

The development of AI4Math cannot be separated from the broader progress of AI and the world economy. Whether and when RSI occurs, and what it actually achieves, may change capabilities and costs; business conditions and research funding affect whether those capabilities are available. If research has come to depend on services that are difficult to replace, price increases, usage limits, or reductions in availability may slow the work. The community may struggle to coordinate a voluntary slowdown, yet be forced to slow down by changes in commercial supply. Existing knowledge would remain valid, but the ability to continue exploring and make rapid use of knowledge could decline. Even openly available literature may require paid tools to be fully organized and used. The availability of alternatives, and whether results and tools can be independently preserved and transferred, will affect the extent of this dependence.

Section 16. Societal Support and Research Training

Societal support for the contemporary professional community in pure mathematics need not closely correspond to the immediate uses of each research paper. A substantial share of its readily visible value may come from training, publicity, cultural and symbolic significance, and expectations of future applications. People trained in research may move into other industries, allowing the training itself to produce benefits; the NSF Workforce Program in the Mathematical Sciences also includes other professions among its training goals. AI may strengthen the benefits of this training, or prompt society to compare it with other training pathways. The broad uses of mathematical knowledge, the uses of particular research advances, and the societal returns of the professional system need to be discussed separately.

Section 17. External Assessments and Symbolic Value

Mathematicians may place weight on methods, depth, and a result’s place within a theory, while the public and governments may also focus on demonstrable breakthroughs, industrial capabilities, training, and returns on investment. Views within each of these groups still differ. The same AI4Math result can be seen as important mathematics, a demonstration of model capability, or evidence that research costs can be reduced. If some of the esteem for mathematics comes from individual genius, sustained exploration, and the human mind overcoming difficulties, that symbolic significance may diminish as the means of producing mathematics change. New forms of communication may also attract more attention, but admiration for results need not continue to translate into support for mathematicians and their professional community. Developments that appear successful from outside may coexist with pressures within the community.

Section 18. Lasting Confusion and Hopes for Flourishing Mathematics

The confusion brought about by these changes may persist for a long time. Tools continue to change, verification and understanding take time, and professional evaluation adjusts over longer career and institutional cycles. Records in journals, preprints, websites, and code repositories may not quickly lead to agreement about contributions either. A unified, stable order cannot be assumed to follow a brief period of dispute. There is still reason to hope for mathematics to flourish with AI: it could bring more reliable results, easier access to knowledge, and richer connections between theories. Yet growth in results, deeper understanding, easier use of knowledge, and a healthy mathematical community may reinforce one another or remain disconnected for a long time. The hope for such flourishing can be retained, while its conditions and possible shortfalls remain open to continued discussion.
